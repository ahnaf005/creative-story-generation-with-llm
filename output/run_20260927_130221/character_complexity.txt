The radiator of the overturned Subaru hissed, a wet, rhythmic spit against the hot manifold that sounded like a tongue clicking in disgust.

Julian did not run down the embankment. At sixty-two, with a calcified hip and knees that registered every drop in atmospheric pressure, running on loose shale was a recipe for two corpses instead of one. He picked his way down sideways, planting his ash-wood walking stick hard into the scree until dust coated the cuffs of his corduroys.

The car was wedged between two scarred lodgepole pines, sixty feet below the mountain road. The scent of spilled ethylene glycol—cloying and chemical, like boiled candy—had already chased away the clean terpene reek of the needles.

Julian knelt by the driver’s side door, which hung open like a broken jaw. The woman was suspended upside down by her seatbelt, her dark hair sweeping the shattered safety glass on the roof interior.

He reached inside, pressing two trembling fingers to the carotid. Cool skin, no flutter. The pupils were wide, black saucers, fixed and dry in the early November chill.

He sat back on his haunches, breathing hard, his heart hammering against his ribs in an ugly, irregular syncopation. He pulled out his phone. One bar, wavering like a candle in a draught. He dialed emergency services, spoke in clipped, dry sentences to a dispatcher three counties away, gave the mile marker, and listened to the woman’s calm, digitized voice inform him that the highway pass was iced over; the county SAR unit was coming from the valley floor. Two hours, minimum. Maybe three.

“Sir, are you safe to remain on scene?” the dispatcher asked.

Julian looked up the embankment. The road was empty. Behind him, the ridge rose in massive, blue-black folds of Douglas fir, rising toward the timberline where the first squalls of the season were already obscuring the peaks in a dull smear of grey.

“Yes,” he lied. He was wearing only a fleece vest over an old wool shirt, and the sun was slipping behind the ridge like a copper coin dropped into an iron grate.

He ended the call and pocketed the phone.

His instinct, forged by decades of avoiding complication, was to climb back up to his truck, drive three miles to his cabin, build a fire, and let the authorities handle their own ledger. He had worked thirty-five years as a structural claims assessor for commercial rail. He knew bodies. He knew that once the physics had ceased its work, whatever remained was merely debris awaiting salvage.

Yet he didn't move.

On the ground near her head lay her purse, split open. A brass lanyard held a state contractor’s badge: *Elena Vance. Hydrological Survey.* No makeup, no perfume bottle. Just three heavy brass keys, a pack of nicotine gum, and a court subpoena folded into fourths.

She was perhaps forty, with strong, square cheekbones and calloused knuckles stained with ink or grease. Not a fragile creature overtaken by tragedy, but a woman who looked as though she had been mid-stride in an argument with the world when the switchback caught her off guard.

A sharp gust dropped off the pass, setting the pines to creaking. Julian shivered violently.

“You made a mess of this turn, Elena,” Julian muttered. His voice sounded thin, immediately eaten by the vastness of the draw.

He pulled off his fleece vest—grudgingly, cursing his own absurd theatricality—and draped it over her exposed shoulders and torso. It did nothing for her, of course, but it covered the awkward twist of her collarbone. Instantly, the wind bit through his wool sleeves, sharp and petty. He already regretted giving up the vest, yet he couldn't bring himself to snatch it back.

He sat on a mossy boulder two feet from the wreckage, clasping his knees to keep them from knocking together.

“I spent nine years in an office overlooking the rail yards in Spokane,” he said. The words came out jerky, defensive, as if she were a supervisor questioning his timecard. “I watched a switcher engine derail once at four miles an hour. It didn't slam. It just nudged the earth, groaned, and settled on its belly like a sick mule. The engineer climbed down, sat on a tie, and ate an apple. He didn’t scream. That’s what they never put in the novels. People think disaster has a trumpet section. It doesn't. It’s mostly just bad geometry.”

He paused. The car's engine gave a long, cooling contraction—*tink, tink, tink*.

“My wife, Claire, left me on a Tuesday,” he continued, staring at Elena’s boots, which were scuffed Red Wings with red laces. “Not because I hit her, or drank, or went broke. I was simply very efficient at reducing the room around us. She told me living with me was like being trapped in an inventory report. Do you know what I said? I told her that without inventory, commerce fails. God, I was proud of that line. I thought it was steel. It took me twelve years to understand she wasn't asking for commerce. She was asking for an error. A margin for a spill.”

A raucous caw broke the silence. A Clark’s nutcracker dipped between the trees, landed on a high branch, cocked its gray head at the wreck, and flew on. It did not care about Elena’s subpoena or Julian’s dead marriage. The forest had its own indifferent work: the dead wood rotting into loam, the ice wedging fissures into the granite, the slow, mindless pulverization of everything that presumed to have an edge.

Julian rubbed his aching hip. “You’d think the permanence of these mountains would be comforting,” he told Elena’s still face. “Everyone in town talks about the timeless peaks. Nonsense. They’re collapsing. Just too slowly for us to hear the crash. In ten million years, this ridge is silt in an estuary. We're just flies arguing on a falling brick.”

He stopped, his teeth chattering. The cold had penetrated to his sternum now, a dull ache that made deep breaths difficult. He looked at the subpoena sticking out of the purse.

Curiosity—an old, intrusive professional itch—won out. He leaned forward, picked up the paper, and unfolded it. Elena had been ordered to testify regarding water rights diversion on the Clark Fork. There was a handwritten note in the margin in heavy pencil: *Tell them the truth about the July flow tests. Let the bastards sue.*

Julian chuckled. A dry, rasping sound.

“You weren't going to roll over, were you?” he said softly, putting the paper back exactly as he’d found it. He felt a sudden, unexpected kinship with the corpse. She had been heading into a fight, carrying her anger like a lit torch, and the slick road had simply snuffed it. All that leverage, all that carefully gathered evidence, evaporated into a damp ditch because of three feet of black ice.

“I spent my whole life building contingencies,” Julian said, his voice dropping an octave, slipping into something uncomfortably close to a prayer. “Pensions, secondary brakes, surplus cords, emergency blankets. And look at me. I’m freezing to death beside an overturned wagon because I gave my coat to a woman who doesn't even have a nervous system anymore.”

He looked up at the sky. Twilight had bled out. The mountains were no longer blue; they were a wall of solid, obsidian shadow, cutting off the horizon. Above them, the first cold stars pricked through the haze like broken needles.

He realized with sudden, startling clarity that he had not stayed out of compassion. He had stayed because he was terrified that if he walked away, the mountain would simply swallow this clearing whole—the car, the woman, the paper, the anger—leaving behind no record that a life had pushed itself to the absolute brink right here. He had stayed to be the auditor.

A long, low howl sounded from the ridge across the creek. A coyote, or a dog from a homestead miles down-valley.

Julian stood up, his joints protesting with audible pops. His fingers were stiff, wooden things. He reached out, his hand hesitating for a fraction of a second, and touched the fringe of Elena’s dark hair, brushing aside a fragment of glass that hung in it like a drop of water.

“You fought well,” he whispered.

Far up the switchbacks, through the dense lattice of pine trunks, a flash of red and blue light flickered against the snowpack, silent and searching, winding its way down through the dark.
