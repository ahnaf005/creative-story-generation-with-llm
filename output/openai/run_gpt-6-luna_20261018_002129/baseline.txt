### The Waiting

By the time Elias heard the crash, he had been walking for six hours.

The sound came from somewhere below the trail: a blunt crack, followed by a long, grinding slide. Then silence returned to the mountains, so complete that he could hear the small click of his walking stick against stone.

He stood still.

The trail crossed a shoulder of the ridge, high above a narrow road. In the valley, the road appeared as a gray thread stitched between dark firs. Elias had seen no one on it that morning. He had seen no one anywhere. The mountain was the sort of place that made solitude seem less like a choice than a condition of the weather.

He left the trail and began picking his way downhill.

The car had come off the road at a bend and struck a stand of young pines. It rested on its side, the front end crushed inward, its rear wheels half suspended above a ditch. One door hung open. A scatter of glass glittered in the weeds.

Elias called out. No answer.

He approached cautiously, speaking as he did so, because silence seemed suddenly dangerous.

“Hello? Can you hear me?”

The woman lay several yards from the car, on her back beside a flat rock. She wore a blue coat and one shoe. Her hair had fallen across her face, and a thin red line marked her temple. Elias knelt and touched two fingers to her neck.

He waited.

There was no pulse.

He tried again, careful not to press too hard, though he knew pressure would not summon what had gone. Her skin was already cool in the shade. He checked her breathing, then her wrist, then, foolishly, the other wrist.

Nothing.

He stood and looked up at the road. It seemed impossible that anything could have happened there. The bend was hidden behind trees; the mountains rose around it without explanation or concern.

His phone showed one bar. He called emergency services, gave the road number painted on a post, then described the car and the woman. The dispatcher asked him to stay on the line. Her voice sounded very far away, as if she were speaking from another season.

“Is she breathing?”

“No.”

“Are you able to move her?”

“No. I don’t think I should.”

“Stay where you are. Don’t enter the vehicle. Help is on the way.”

The signal broke, returned, and broke again. Eventually the dispatcher said she had his location and would send a rescue team. Elias gave the phone battery a worried glance, then put it away.

He took off his pack and set it beside the woman, though he could not explain why. The pack had traveled with him all morning, full of practical things: water, a jacket, a map, a little food. Now it looked absurdly useful.

He sat on the rock near her shoulder.

For a while he said nothing. The trees moved slightly in the wind. High above, a crow circled once, then vanished behind the ridge.

“My name is Elias,” he said at last. “In case that matters.”

It didn’t matter, of course. But speaking made the space less empty.

He thought of the first time he had been alone in the mountains. He was twelve, and his father had taken him up a small peak above the town where they lived. They had argued most of the way. Elias had wanted to turn back. His father, a carpenter with broad hands and a habit of treating every problem as if it could be repaired with patience, had kept walking.

At the summit they found a rusted tin cup wedged between two stones. His father filled it with snowmelt and drank from it.

“Someone left this here,” Elias had said.

“Or lost it,” his father replied.

That had been his father’s whole philosophy, as far as Elias could tell: things were not always where they were meant to be, and it was a mistake to assume intention.

He told the woman about the cup. He told her how his father had kept it afterward, washed it, and set it on the kitchen shelf for years, though it leaked and could not hold anything. When Elias was twenty, his father died of a heart attack in the workshop. The cup was still on the shelf. His mother had thrown it away when she sold the house.

“I was angry with her for that,” Elias said. “As if the cup were him.”

A breeze slipped through the trees and lifted the woman’s hair from her cheek. Elias lowered his eyes.

He had learned, slowly, that remembering a thing did not preserve it. It preserved only the fact that someone had once been there to remember.

The dispatcher had said help was on the way. He looked at the road, expecting headlights, but none appeared. The mountains kept their own time. A shadow moved across the opposite slope, and the sun brightened the wrecked car’s windshield. In the broken glass he saw a distorted reflection of himself: a gray-haired man in a green jacket, alone beside a stranger.

“Would you like another story?” he asked her.

He had not spoken to a dead person before. He found that he didn’t mind it. There was no danger of interrupting her.

He told her about Mara, whom he had loved at twenty-seven. They met in a bookstore where both of them had gone to avoid the rain. Mara had been reading a book she disliked and defending it with more energy than she had shown for any book she liked.

For five years, they built a life in a narrow apartment above a bakery. In the mornings the walls smelled of warm bread. They were not unhappy, though they were not always kind. They quarreled over money and work and the right way to load a dishwasher. When Mara left, she said she had been lonely for a long time. Elias had answered that she could have told him sooner.

“She said she had,” he told the woman. “I just hadn’t understood what she meant.”

Mara moved east. Elias stayed. They exchanged a few letters, then stopped. He had sometimes imagined her older, at a kitchen table, reaching for a cup. But that was another kind of cup: a small object he had filled with a life that might or might not have happened.

A branch cracked somewhere in the woods. Elias turned quickly. Nothing followed. He realized his hands were trembling and drank from his water bottle.

The rescue team would come. People were always coming, eventually. But the thought of them brought no relief. He had given the dispatcher the location; he had done what could be done. The rest belonged to a world he could not hurry.

The woman’s face held none of the expressions he was used to seeing on living people. No impatience, no faint questioning look, no effort to make him comfortable. Her stillness wasn’t peaceful. It was simply final.

He wondered what she had been doing that morning. Perhaps driving home from a visit. Perhaps going to a job she disliked, carrying a lunch she would forget in the car. Perhaps she had someone waiting for her to call. The mind made small, ordinary guesses, then arranged them into a life.

“I’m sorry,” Elias said.

He did not know whether he meant the crash, the waiting, or the fact that he could not tell her anything about herself.

Clouds gathered above the ridge. The light thinned. Somewhere high up, water ran beneath the ground, unseen. The wilderness was not cruel. Cruelty required attention. The mountain was only enormous, and the things that happened on it were small enough to vanish between one gust of wind and the next.

Elias thought of all the moments he had once believed would last: his father’s hand on his shoulder; Mara laughing in the bakery stairwell; the first day of the job he had kept for twenty years; the last night in the house where he grew up. They had not disappeared exactly. They had become something else, something without edges. He could walk through his memories as through a forest, but he could never return to the same clearing.

The woman’s blue coat stirred in the wind.

“I used to think that meant nothing mattered,” he said. “Because nothing stayed.”

He rubbed his palms together. “I think now it means the opposite.”

The first siren was faint, rising and falling far below. Elias stood. The sound vanished, then returned, closer. He waved both arms toward the road, though the rescuers could not yet see him. A moment later another siren joined the first.

The relief that came was physical. His shoulders dropped. He became aware of the cold in his knees and the ache in his back. He had been waiting so intensely that the world had narrowed to a few yards of dirt and stone. Now it widened again.

A rescue vehicle appeared around the bend. Two responders climbed out and came down the slope with a stretcher. One knelt beside the woman while the other asked Elias what had happened. He told them everything: the sound, the car, the call, the time he had found her. They listened without interruption.

When one of them asked him to wait by the road, Elias picked up his pack. He looked once more at the woman. The red line at her temple seemed no larger than a thread.

Then the responders were around her, lifting carefully, speaking in low voices. Their work was precise, practiced, and human. The trees stood behind them. Above the ridge, the clouds began to break.

Elias walked back toward the trail. Before he climbed, he turned around. The rescue vehicle blocked part of the road, its lights flashing silently in the afternoon sun. Beyond it, the mountains continued to rise, indifferent to the little drama beneath them and made larger, somehow, by the fact that it had occurred.

He began walking.

For a long time he heard no sound but his boots on the path. Then the wind moved through the firs, and the mountains answered with a whisper that could have been anything.
