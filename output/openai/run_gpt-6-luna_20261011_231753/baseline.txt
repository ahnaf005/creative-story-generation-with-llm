At dusk, Mara saw the couple beneath the pharmacy awning.

They were ordinary in the way strangers often were: the man carrying a paper sack, the woman holding his sleeve between finger and thumb. They stood close without speaking, watching rain silver the street. When the light changed, they crossed together.

Mara followed.

She told herself it was only because they walked the same way. Past the shuttered bakery, past the bus stop where rainwater spun wrappers in circles. At the corner, the man glanced back. Mara ducked into a doorway. When she looked again, they were gone.

Their house stood on a narrow street near the river, a small red-brick place with a porch light burning. Through the front window, Mara saw the woman set the paper sack on the kitchen table. The man took off her coat. They moved around each other as if repeating a dance they had learned long ago.

Mara stayed across the street.

By midnight, the rain had become a steady roar. Water slid along the gutters, then covered the curb. It reached Mara’s shoes, cold and full of grit. The couple’s porch steps disappeared beneath it.

Earlier, on her way home, she had passed a chair dumped beside the bins: a wooden armchair, blackened on one side, its stuffing showing through a split seam. Someone had left it in the rain. Mara remembered its crooked back and one pale strip of wood where the fire had missed.

Now she saw the woman in the window, standing very still.

The lamplight caught her face, then her shoulder. The woman leaned toward the glass. For an instant, the shape of her seemed to shift—one side dark and blistered, the other smooth and pale. A crooked angle at the shoulder. A split, ragged line down her dress.

Mara stepped closer to the house. Water climbed over her ankles.

Inside, the man came to stand beside his wife. He put an arm around her. She did not turn to him. She was looking out at Mara, though the glass was black with rain and reflection.

Behind Mara, something knocked softly against the bins.

The burned chair, carried loose by the flood, drifted past. It turned once in the water, its empty seat facing the house.

The woman raised her hand to the window.

Mara raised hers, too.
