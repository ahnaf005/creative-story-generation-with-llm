At first, there was nothing to notice about them. A man carrying a paper bag; a woman in a green coat, her gloved hand tucked through his arm. They walked slowly, stopping once to admire a dog in a shop window.

Mara watched them from the bus shelter. They seemed to know exactly where they were going. How rare, that was. How restful.

When they turned down Bell Street, she followed.

The chair had been waiting at the curb all afternoon: a wooden armchair, blackened on one side, its seat blistered to a brittle brown. Someone had left it beside the bins with a handwritten PLEASE TAKE ME sign, though nobody would. Mara had paused over it earlier, wondering what sort of fire made such a neat, dark crescent across the back.

Now the couple passed it without looking. The woman’s green coat brushed the burned arm.

Their house stood at the end of the street, low and narrow, with yellow curtains and a brass knocker shaped like a hand. The man opened the door. The woman glanced back—not at Mara, surely, but at the rain gathering in the gutter—and went inside.

Mara should have gone home. The rain was coming down harder, and the drains were already gulping and choking. But the yellow curtains drew her on. Just a minute. She could see them through the front window.

She crossed the street and stood beneath the bare branches of a tree. Inside, the couple moved about their ordinary evening. The man set out plates. The woman took off her coat. She had a pale line running from her left shoulder to her collarbone, like a seam repaired badly.

The water reached Mara’s ankles. Bell Street sloped toward the river, and the river was in no hurry to stay put. She stepped onto the low garden wall, gripping the wet brick. Still the curtains stayed open.

The woman sat in an armchair.

Mara leaned closer. It wasn’t the chair from the curb. This one was upholstered in blue; its wooden arms were polished smooth. Yet the woman sat with her left shoulder lifted, her right hand resting on the seat, her body angled as if making room for a missing leg.

Exactly as the burned chair had stood.

A wave of brown water rushed along the pavement, carrying leaves and a plastic bottle. It struck Mara’s knees. She clutched the wall. Surely the woman couldn’t see her through the rain. Surely the woman couldn’t see the old chair, either—couldn’t know how its blackened back had seemed, for a moment, to curve like a person turning to look.

Inside, the man said something. The woman didn’t answer. She kept her eyes on the window.

The water rose, and the yellow curtains trembled though the windows were shut.

Mara thought of the crescent scorch on the chair’s back. A mark left by something held too close, too long. The woman’s pale seam. The way she sat as if waiting for someone to pull her gently upright.

Then, in the dim room, the woman lifted one hand and pressed it to the glass.

The burned chair at the curb rocked once in the flood.
