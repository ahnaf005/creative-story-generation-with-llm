### The Space Between Bells

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara Weiss woke before the alarm.

For several seconds she lay still, one hand on the curve of her stomach, listening to the house settle around her. A radiator clicked in the bedroom. Outside, a plow scraped along the street, pushing old snow into ridges at the curb. The clock on the bedside table said 6:18.

The baby shifted beneath her palm.

“I know,” Barbara whispered. “It’s early.”

Her husband, Daniel, stirred beside her. “What is?”

“Everything.”

He smiled without opening his eyes. “Then we’ll have breakfast slowly.”

They had been married for six years. They knew how to answer each other’s words and how to leave some unanswered. Daniel understood that Barbara’s fear of the birth came and went like weather; she understood that he was more afraid than he let on. They had chosen the name Miriam if the baby was a girl, David if a boy. They had painted the nursery yellow, though Daniel had insisted it was cream.

That Saturday, they were going to synagogue.

Their congregation met in Southfield, just north of Detroit, in a low brick building with a blue door and a flagpole that leaned a little toward the parking lot. Barbara had grown up there. She knew the sanctuary’s uneven floorboards, the narrow windows, and the small patch of sunlight that reached the second row when the clouds cleared. She knew which coat hooks wobbled and which elder always arrived early to straighten the prayer books.

She knew Rabbi Adler’s voice.

He was a renowned teacher, though Barbara had never liked that word for him. Renowned belonged to people in photographs, she thought, and Rabbi Adler was simply the person who remembered what everyone’s children were studying and who always asked after her mother’s arthritis. He could explain a difficult passage so plainly that it seemed you had been carrying the answer yourself all along.

On the drive over, Daniel kept one hand on the wheel and the other near Barbara’s knee.

“You don’t have to stay for the whole service,” he said.

“I’m pregnant, not made of glass.”

“I know.”

“You say that, but you keep looking at me as though I might crack at a pothole.”

He glanced at her. “You are the most important person in the car.”

“That’s a very low bar.”

He laughed, and the baby moved again. Barbara watched winter sunlight fall across the dashboard and thought of how soon the shape of her life would change. Not in some grand way, exactly. In a thousand small ones: a crib, a bottle warming in a pan, a name spoken at midnight.

The synagogue’s parking lot was nearly full when they arrived. People stood in little groups, stamping snow from their shoes and calling greetings across the lot. Barbara felt the familiar relief of seeing them. A community was, she thought, a place where you could arrive with cold hands and someone would already be holding the door.

Inside, the building smelled of wool coats, floor polish, and coffee. Daniel took Barbara’s scarf and hung it on a sturdy hook. Then they went into the sanctuary, where the congregation was finding its places.

Rabbi Adler stood at the front, speaking quietly to an older man. A few silver strands showed at his temples. When he turned, his eyes found Barbara.

“Ah,” he said, coming toward her. “You’re carrying the whole congregation’s future today.”

“Only one person’s,” Barbara said.

“One person is plenty.”

He asked how she was feeling. She said fine. He gave Daniel a quick look that suggested he knew fine could mean several different things, then wished them both a good morning.

The service began.

Barbara settled into her seat and eased her back against the pew. Daniel sat beside her. Around them, people opened prayer books. A baby began fussing and was carried outside. The congregation rose and sat, rose and sat. The words moved through the room in a language Barbara knew by sound if not always by meaning.

She had felt impatient with the prayers lately. Not with the people, or with the old melodies, but with the sense that every day asked for the same things: peace, healing, shelter, wisdom. She wanted to say, Give us one ordinary morning. Let nothing happen. Let this baby arrive on a spring day with birds in the trees.

Then she looked at Rabbi Adler and remembered how he once told the congregation that prayer was not a way to bargain with the world. It was a way of refusing to meet the world alone.

The thought stayed with her.

The young man entered during the reading.

Barbara noticed him because he did not seem to know where to go. He paused just inside the doorway, his dark coat buttoned to the throat. He looked younger than Daniel, maybe in his early twenties. His hair was damp from the snow. For a moment, people near the entrance shifted aside to let him pass.

Then came the first sharp crack.

Barbara’s mind did not understand it. Her body did. She was on her feet before she knew she had moved, one hand reaching for the pew, the other pressed against her stomach. The room erupted in confusion. A second crack split the air, and people began shouting.

She saw Rabbi Adler stagger and fall.

A woman screamed his name. Daniel caught Barbara’s arm, pulling her down, but the crowd had already begun to surge toward the doors. Benches scraped. Prayer books tumbled to the floor. People pushed without seeing one another, their faces transformed by the same terrible knowledge: leave, leave, leave.

Barbara saw the young man standing near the front, his arm lowered now. He looked smaller than before, almost lost inside his coat. Then, in an instant, he turned the gun on himself.

Daniel pulled Barbara toward the aisle. Someone struck her shoulder. A man stumbled against Daniel, knocking him sideways. The doorway clogged with bodies, and all the order of the morning—the familiar voices, the careful greetings, the prayers—vanished beneath the crush.

Barbara tried to twist back.

“Rabbi Adler!” she shouted.

Daniel’s hand tightened around her arm. “Barbara, come on!”

But she had seen the young man’s face. Not clearly, not enough to recognize him, but enough to see that he was not a monster out of a story. He was a frightened, stunned boy—or perhaps not a boy, but someone too young to seem capable of what had happened. He lay near the front, alone in the space people were fleeing.

Barbara thought of him turning, of a mother somewhere who might be waiting for a phone call. The thought came with such force that it seemed to pull her toward him.

“I have to—”

“No,” Daniel said, and his voice broke on the word.

She reached out anyway. Her fingers brushed the sleeve of a woman being pushed past her. Then the crowd swept Barbara sideways. Someone took her by the waist to keep her from falling. Daniel pulled from the other side, and together they were carried through the doorway into the corridor.

The cold air outside struck her face. People spilled into the parking lot, crying, calling names, searching one another. Barbara found herself beside the blue door, with Daniel’s arms around her. She could not tell whether she was standing or being held upright.

“Are you hurt?” he asked.

She shook her head. “The rabbi.”

Daniel didn’t answer. He looked back toward the building.

A man was shouting for someone to call the police. Another person ran to a car. An elderly woman sat on the curb, staring at her gloves. Snowflakes drifted down, melting on Barbara’s cheeks.

She placed both hands over her stomach.

The baby moved.

That was when she began to cry.

Not loudly. The tears came with no warning, and she could not stop them. Daniel held her, his chin pressed against her hair. Around them, the parking lot filled with noise, but inside his arms there was a pocket of stillness.

“I tried to reach him,” she said.

“I know.”

“I couldn’t.”

“I know.”

The sirens arrived in the distance and grew louder. People asked questions no one could answer. Someone said Rabbi Adler had been shot. Someone else said the young man was dead. The words traveled through the crowd and seemed to leave a colder space wherever they passed.

Barbara looked at the synagogue’s windows. From outside they appeared dark, reflecting only the snow and the people gathered in the lot. She imagined the sanctuary as it had been a few minutes earlier: the prayer books open, the sunlight on the second row, Rabbi Adler standing at the front.

She thought of how he used to pause before answering a difficult question, as if he believed the person asking deserved the full space of his attention. She had never understood how a man could offer that kind of patience to so many people. Now the memory seemed almost impossible, like a story from another life.

Later, at the hospital, a nurse examined Barbara and told her the baby’s heartbeat was steady. Daniel sat beside her bed with his coat folded over his lap. He had a small scrape on one hand where he had been pushed against the doorway. He kept turning it palm-up, then down again, as if checking that it belonged to him.

Barbara stared at the ceiling tiles.

“I keep thinking I should have done something,” she said.

“You were trying to help.”

“I didn’t help anyone.”

“You got out. You kept the baby safe.”

She turned toward him. “That feels like a very small thing.”

“It isn’t small to me.”

The nurse came in again and checked the monitor. Its steady rhythm filled the room, quick as a bird’s heart. Barbara closed her eyes and listened.

Over the next days, newsmen called the synagogue. People brought casseroles. The congregation gathered in borrowed rooms, where the old prayers sounded changed but not broken. Barbara kept expecting Rabbi Adler to appear at the back of the room, late and smiling, to ask how her mother was.

He did not.

She wondered about the young man, too. The newspapers gave him a name and fragments of a life, but none of it made the morning understandable. She never learned what he had thought as he crossed the sanctuary threshold. She never knew whether he had wanted someone to stop him, or whether the thought had even crossed his mind. The unanswered questions remained, like a door in a dark hall.

In April, Miriam was born during a rainstorm.

Daniel held the baby first, then brought her to Barbara, his face crumpling as he placed her in her mother’s arms. Miriam had a crease between her brows and a fierce little mouth. Barbara touched her cheek with one finger.

For a long while she could say nothing.

At home, they set the crib beside the window. A week later, Daniel found an old prayer book among the things Barbara had brought from the synagogue. Its cover was bent, one corner darkened where a shoe had scuffed it. Barbara opened it carefully.

Inside, in the margin beside a familiar prayer, someone had written Rabbi Adler’s neat handwriting:

*No one carries hope alone.*

Barbara read the words aloud. Miriam slept in her arms, warm and impossibly light.

She thought again of the crowd pressing her away from the young man. She could still feel the tug of that moment, the desperate reach of her hand, and the shame that had followed. But she understood, at last, that the crowd had not been made of strangers. It had been made of frightened people trying to keep one another alive. Daniel had held her. Someone else had caught her when she stumbled. The nurse had listened to Miriam’s heart. The congregation had made a place for sorrow, and then, slowly, for the baby.

Barbara closed the book and drew Miriam closer.

Outside, the rain tapped at the window. Inside, the child breathed in small, even sighs. Barbara listened to them both—the rain and the baby—and let the morning pass without asking it to explain itself.
