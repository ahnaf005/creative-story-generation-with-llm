### The Blue Bowl

By the time Mara reached Bellwether, the heat had settled over the town like a hand laid gently on a forehead. The station clock said eleven, though the little platform seemed to have stopped somewhere in the previous century. A boy in a red cap pedaled past with a paper bag of cherries. The bakery still had its striped awning. The pharmacy still had the same faded sign promising *Reliable Remedies Since 1932*.

And her mother still lived at the end of Willow Street, in the narrow white house with the blue door.

“You’re late,” Ruth said when Mara came in.

“By six minutes.”

“Late is late.” Her mother smiled and held out both hands, as if Mara had arrived from a distant country rather than the city two hours away. Her hands were smaller than Mara remembered, the knuckles prominent beneath thin skin. “You look tired.”

“You always say that.”

“You always look tired.”

Ruth was eighty-four and lived alone, though she insisted she lived “with the house,” which was different. The house had its own moods, she explained: pipes that knocked when rain was coming, a stair that complained in winter, a kitchen window that stuck unless you lifted it just so. Mara had heard it all before. She was glad to hear it again.

They sat in the kitchen, where the clock was slow and the curtains smelled faintly of sun. Ruth put on the kettle. She still reached for the chipped blue bowl to measure the tea, though the tea came in bags now. There was no need for the bowl. There had never been a need for it, really. Her mother had always used it anyway.

“Your father hated tea,” Ruth said, peering into the bowl. “He used to say it tasted like a wet newspaper.”

“He said that about coffee.”

“He said it about everything he didn’t like.”

She set out two cups, one with a hairline crack. Mara reached for it, and Ruth nudged it toward her.

“That one doesn’t leak.”

“Not yet.”

“Don’t be gloomy. It’s served me for forty years.”

They drank tea that was too hot, as they always did, and watched a pair of sparrows squabble over the crumbs on the sill. Ruth asked after Mara’s work, her apartment, whether she was still “seeing that nice man with the beard.” No, his name was Daniel, and no, they weren’t seeing each other anymore. Ruth absorbed this without alarm.

“Well,” she said, stirring her tea though the sugar had dissolved. “There’ll be somebody else.”

“Maybe.”

“There will. You’re not difficult to love.”

Mara looked out the window. A truck rattled down the street, and a loose branch tapped against the glass. She could not remember the last time anyone had said that to her. Was it something her mother knew, or something mothers said because there was no better answer?

“Are you still walking to the post office?” she asked.

“Of course.”

“In this heat?”

“I go early.”

“You went yesterday at noon.”

“I had a letter.”

“You could have waited.”

“Letters don’t improve by waiting.”

There was no arguing with that. Ruth had kept up the habit of walking to the post office every weekday, even though most of her bills came online and the letters she sent were increasingly rare. Mara suspected the walk mattered more than the mail: the hello to the woman at the dry cleaner, the stop at the little park, the proof that the world was still there and had not gone on without her.

After tea, they took out the old photographs. Ruth had them in a biscuit tin on the sideboard, neatly stacked and sorted, except for the ones that had escaped into the bottom and become mixed up with buttons and rubber bands. They sat at the dining table with the fan turning in the doorway.

“Your father had more hair than he knew what to do with,” Ruth said, handing Mara a picture of him on a beach. He was young enough to look like someone Mara might have passed on the street. His trousers were rolled to the knee. He was laughing at something outside the frame.

“He looks happy.”

“He was. He was also sunburned for three days.”

Ruth tapped the photograph with one finger. “We drove down in that old Morris. No air conditioning, of course. The windows were open and the road dust came in. I had to wash the curtains when we got home.”

“You always washed the curtains.”

“Someone had to.”

The pictures carried them through years they had not discussed in years: Mara with missing front teeth, her brother in a paper crown, Ruth at the kitchen sink with her sleeves rolled up. When they reached a photograph of Mara at eight, standing beside a bicycle with a crooked basket, Ruth laughed.

“You wouldn’t let go of the handlebars.”

“I was learning.”

“You knew how to ride. You just didn’t trust the bike.”

“It was a bad bike.”

“It was perfectly good.”

“It had a broken bell.”

“Then it was a slightly imperfect bike.”

Mara remembered the chalky feel of the pavement beneath her palms, the bruised knees, her mother running beside her, one hand close to the seat but not touching. She had been sure that if Ruth let go she would fall. Later, when she realized her mother had already let go, she had been furious.

“You ran behind me for blocks,” Mara said.

“Did I?”

“You were out of breath.”

“I must have been.”

For a moment, Ruth studied the photograph. Her thumb moved over its curled corner. “I suppose I wanted you to think I was still holding on.”

The fan clicked in the doorway. Mara looked at her mother’s hands resting on the table. What a strange kindness, she thought, to keep pace with a child who did not know she was already free.

At lunch, Ruth made egg salad. She put too much pepper in it and insisted the bread was stale, though it was soft. They ate in the shade of the kitchen, and afterward Mara washed the plates while her mother dried them. The old division of labor returned without discussion: Mara at the sink, Ruth at her shoulder, taking each plate with a quick, practiced flick of the towel.

“You don’t have to dry,” Mara said.

“If I didn’t, you’d put them away wet.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“You would.”

She put the last plate into the cupboard. “There. The house is still standing.”

By midafternoon, the sun had turned the garden white with glare. Ruth went to the back door and looked out at the small lawn, the row of foxgloves, the leaning fence.

“Those flowers need water,” she said.

“They can wait till evening.”

“They don’t know that.”

“Mom.”

“I’m only going out for a minute.”

Mara put down her glass. “No. It’s too hot.”

Ruth turned. “I’m not made of paper.”

Nobody was. Not even at eighty-four. Still, Mara found herself standing between her mother and the door, a foolish guard at a gate.

“I’ll water them later,” she said.

“You won’t remember.”

“I’ll set an alarm.”

“Imagine needing an alarm to water a flower.”

Ruth was amused, not offended. In the end she let Mara fill the watering can. Mara carried it into the garden herself, and Ruth stayed inside by the open door, offering instructions.

“Not the leaves. At the roots.”

“I know.”

“You’re drowning that one.”

“It’s thirsty.”

“It’s a flower, not a camel.”

When Mara came back in, they had coffee. Ruth sat beneath the kitchen window, where the light made a bright square on the floor. She told Mara about Mrs. Bell across the street, who had begun leaving her bins out on the wrong days, and about the new family at number twelve, with the little boy who liked to draw on the pavement. Her voice grew softer as the afternoon went on, but she did not seem tired. She looked pleased to have the day filled up.

“You should come more often,” she said.

“I know.”

“I’m not scolding you.”

“I know.”

“Your brother comes on Sundays.”

“He lives nearby.”

“That’s no excuse for you not to come.”

Mara laughed. “You’re not scolding me?”

“Not at all.”

They both knew the difference between scolding and asking. Her mother did too, though she preferred not to name it.

At six, Mara packed her bag. Ruth wrapped two apples in a paper towel and put them inside.

“You’ll forget to eat on the train.”

“I’ll buy something.”

“Something expensive and tasteless.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know trains.”

At the door, Ruth touched the sleeve of Mara’s coat, as if checking the fabric. “You’ll come again before long?”

“Yes,” Mara said. She believed she meant it. “Next month.”

“All right.”

The front garden smelled of hot stone and rosemary. Mara walked down the path, then turned back. Her mother was still in the doorway, one hand resting on the blue frame. She lifted it in a small wave. Mara raised her hand in answer and kept going.

The call came four weeks later, just after dawn.

Ruth had been found in the garden by the postman. She had gone out in the late afternoon to water the foxgloves. The doctor said sunstroke, dehydration, a spell of confusion. She was in hospital, stable, but they wanted someone there. Mara took the first train.

The house was locked, the curtains drawn against the heat. She let herself in with the spare key, and the air inside smelled of closed rooms and the faint sweetness of something left too long in a bowl. For a second she stood in the hall, waiting for the familiar voice from the kitchen.

No voice came.

The house had not been tidied after the last visit. Why would it have been? The blue bowl was on the counter, a tea bag dried to the bottom. Two cups stood beside the sink. One still had a faint brown line around its rim. On the table, the photographs lay scattered where they had left them. The picture of the bicycle sat on top.

Mara touched its edge. There she was, eight years old, white socks slumped around her ankles, eyes fixed on the camera with the fierce suspicion of a child who knew someone was holding something back. Behind her, Ruth’s shadow stretched across the pavement.

She set the photograph down.

The watering can stood by the back door. Its handle was warm from the sun that had passed through the glass. Outside, the foxgloves drooped over the fence, their tall bells browned at the edges. Someone—perhaps the postman, perhaps a neighbor—had watered them since. The soil was dark around their roots.

On the kitchen table, the paper towel that had held the apples had been folded into a small square. Mara could not remember doing that. Perhaps she had. Beside it lay the local paper, open to the crossword. Ruth had filled in four answers in blue ink. One word remained unfinished: *departure*.

Mara heard her mother’s voice in the room—*Letters don’t improve by waiting.* She heard the clatter of plates, the complaint of the stair, the old joke about the flowers needing their drink. How much of a day could fit into the things left behind? A cup, a photograph, an unfinished word. Not much. Everything.

At the hospital, Ruth was asleep when Mara arrived. Her hair had been brushed back from her forehead. Without the usual alertness in her eyes, she looked smaller than she had in the kitchen, as though the bed had taken up the room around her.

Mara sat beside her and held the hand that had once hovered behind a bicycle seat.

When Ruth woke, she blinked at the ceiling, then turned toward Mara.

“You came,” she said.

“I said I would.”

Ruth frowned slightly, concentrating. “Did you water the flowers?”

“I did.”

“Not the leaves?”

“Not the leaves.”

“Good.”

Her eyelids lowered. Mara kept her hand around her mother’s, careful of the thin bones. It was a warm day, but the hospital room was cool. Beyond the window, the town carried on in its old ways: postman on his route, bakery awning stirring, the clock on the station marking an hour that might or might not be right.

Ruth’s breathing settled. Mara sat still, listening. There would be time to talk when her mother woke again. There would be time to ask about the crossword, the neighbors, the curtains. For now, she could hold on without pretending her mother needed her to.
