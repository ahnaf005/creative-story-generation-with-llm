By the last week of the residency, Mara could tell the hour by the noises in the house. At six, the kettle in the kitchen. At seven, the screen door slamming as someone went running. At midnight, the pipes knocking like a visitor who had reconsidered.

Elias had been the one to suggest the beach. “One last trip,” he said, as though they were a pair of people who did things together. Then he looked down at the peel of his orange, and Mara had to decide what he meant.

The residency occupied a converted school on a hill above the town. Their rooms were on different floors, which had seemed important the first night and less important by the third. For six weeks, they had worked, eaten, and wandered the narrow streets together, and sometimes, when the others were asleep, they had shared the narrow bed in Elias’s room. In the morning he would make coffee and ask about her work. He was very good at asking about her work.

Now there were four days left. His train left Saturday; hers left Sunday. Such a small difference, one day. A person could do a lot in a day. Or nothing at all.

They missed the nine-thirty bus because Elias went back upstairs for his sunglasses, which he had left on the table beside the door. Mara waited at the stop in the sun, watching the bus pull away with what seemed like deliberate satisfaction.

“You don’t need them,” she said when he came hurrying back.

“I’m going to the beach.”

“Then you definitely don’t need them.”

He laughed, but the bus was gone. They had to take the next one, which stopped at every village between them and the water. Elias sat by the window, his knee touching hers when the bus swayed. Mara could have moved away. She could also have leaned into him. Instead she watched the sea appear and disappear between the houses.

They got off too early. The driver had said something about the beach, and Elias had nodded with the confidence of a man who had not understood a word. They walked along a road lined with sun-bleached stone walls, past a vineyard and a closed café, the sea flashing below them.

“Worth it,” Elias said.

He had already decided. Mara carried both their water bottles, because he’d packed his in the wrong bag and hers was heavier. It was exactly the sort of thing she would remember later, though there was no reason to remember it.

At the beach, they found a place near the edge of the water. It was smaller than Mara had imagined, all pale pebbles and dark shelves of rock. Elias took off his shoes and waded in, his trousers rolled up to his calves. Mara unpacked the food: bread, apricots, cheese wrapped in paper. A gull landed nearby and eyed the cheese with professional interest.

“Don’t encourage it,” she said.

“I’m not.”

“You’re looking at it.”

“I can look at a gull.”

The gull lunged. Elias flapped his arms; Mara grabbed the paper; the cheese fell onto the stones. For a moment they both stared at it, as though the cheese might apologize and return to its wrapping.

Then Elias said, “Five-second rule?”

“It’s been five seconds already.”

He picked it up anyway. Mara watched him brush grit from the rind and put it back. They ate it. This, apparently, was what the day had come to.

They swam after lunch. The water was cold enough to make Mara gasp, which Elias took as a reason to splash her. She tried to splash him back and slipped on the stones, scraping her palm. He was beside her at once, his hand around her wrist.

“Let me see.”

“It’s nothing.”

It was a small cut, bright with blood. He rinsed it with seawater, then wrapped it in the clean corner of his handkerchief. His fingers were careful. They stayed around hers for a second longer than necessary.

There. That was the kind of thing that made a person foolish. Not the bed, not the kisses, not even the way he had once reached for her in his sleep. A handkerchief, a scraped palm. The knowledge that he had noticed.

They lay in the sun afterward. Elias fell asleep with one arm over his face. Mara read the same paragraph six times. When she gave up, she watched the small muscle in his cheek twitch. She wondered whether he would remember this day in winter, and how he would describe it if someone asked. A beach. A bus. A gull. A woman who had once slept in his bed. He would leave out the handkerchief.

She wanted to ask him what they were doing. A ridiculous question, given that they had been doing it for weeks. Still, it seemed possible that a name might make it less temporary. It might also make it impossible to go back to the easy version, where nothing had been promised and therefore nobody could be disappointed.

Elias woke when a cloud passed over them. “We should go soon,” he said.

“Why?”

“Last bus.”

He said it lightly. Mara looked up at the sky. One cloud, no threat of rain. “There’s another one.”

“Not if we miss the connection.”

Of course. She had forgotten the connection. She had been thinking of staying until the sun went down, walking back along the shore, finding dinner somewhere. A whole evening, as though they had an evening to spare.

They packed. The handkerchief was stiff with salt and blood. Elias tucked it into his pocket rather than throwing it away.

On the way back, they stopped at the bus shelter, which was not a shelter so much as a signpost beside a field. The next bus was an hour away. They sat on the low stone wall, passing the water bottle between them.

A car slowed at the corner and turned down the hill toward the beach. Dust drifted behind it. Somewhere beyond the wall, a dog barked.

“Could walk to the station,” Elias said. “Maybe forty minutes.”

“Maybe.”

They started walking. The road was narrower than it had seemed from the bus, with no pavement and tall grasses brushing their legs. At the first bend, Mara’s sandal broke. The strap came loose from the sole, and she stopped, holding the two pieces in her hand.

“Of course,” she said.

Elias offered his handkerchief. She looked at the red-brown stain on it.

“Not that.”

“I have nothing else.”

“You have a belt.”

He considered this. Then he took off his belt, threaded it through the sandal, and fastened the buckle around her ankle. It was absurd and uncomfortable, but it held. She walked on with the belt tapping against her heel.

“You look very fashionable,” he said.

“You look like you’re carrying a secret.”

“That’s just my trousers.”

She laughed. He laughed too, and for a little while the road seemed to belong to them. Then a small truck came around the bend too fast. They squeezed against the wall as it passed, its wing mirror missing Mara by inches. Elias’s hand settled at her back. It stayed there until the truck had vanished.

At the station, they discovered they had walked to the wrong one. The sign was clear enough, once they had stopped to read it. Elias blamed the bus driver. Mara blamed the map, though she had been the one holding it. Neither of them blamed themselves, which was a kind of agreement.

They arrived back at the residency near dusk. In the kitchen, people were drinking wine and asking about the beach. Mara told them about the gull and the broken sandal. Elias showed them the handkerchief, which had dried into a stiff red flag. Everyone laughed. It was a good story. It had all the necessary parts: mishap, danger, a touch of blood. No one asked what had happened in the water.

Later, Mara went to her room and washed the salt from her hair. She found a thin red mark on her palm where the cut had opened again. The belt had left a bruise around her ankle. Both injuries looked more serious than they were.

There was a knock at her door.

Elias stood in the hall, holding her book. She had left it on the beach.

“I thought you might want it back.”

“I thought you’d forgotten it.”

“I nearly did.”

He came in when she stepped aside. There was nowhere to put the book except on the desk, between her unfinished pages and the paper schedule for the final week. Elias glanced at the schedule. His train time was printed in blue ink; hers, in black.

“You’re leaving Sunday,” he said.

“So are you.”

“Saturday.”

“Yes. I know.”

A strange silence gathered. The rooms were quiet now; even the pipes had gone still. Elias looked at her, and she could feel the familiar question approaching, or perhaps only the familiar lack of one.

He could stay. He could say something simple. He could say he wished they had met earlier, or that he would miss her, or that the beach had been better than he expected. He could say any of these things, and she would know what to do with them. Probably.

Instead, he said, “Your sandal held up.”

“It did.”

“I’ll see you at breakfast.”

“Probably.”

He nodded and left.

Mara stood in the middle of the room, listening to his footsteps cross the hall and fade down the stairs. It was not a goodbye. It was almost worse: a sentence that could be used any day of the week.

In the morning, she found him in the kitchen with two cups of coffee. He had already packed his bag; it rested by the door, tidy and square. He handed her a cup.

“You have my belt,” he said.

She looked down. She was still wearing the sandal, the leather looped around her ankle. “I’ll give it back.”

“Keep it. You’ll need it.”

It was a practical thing to say. He was good at practical things.

At the station, the next day, Mara watched his train pull away. He stood by the window and raised one hand. She raised hers in reply. Then the train rounded the bend, and there was nothing to wave at.

The platform was hot and empty. She sat on her suitcase and removed the sandal. The belt had left a faint red line around her ankle. She rubbed it with her thumb, as though it might fade faster if she tried.

It would fade. The cut on her palm would close. The residency would become a handful of scenes she could arrange however she liked: the gull, the wrong station, the cold water; a handkerchief kept too long in a pocket. She could make it a story about misadventure, or about a summer, or about two people who had not asked for more than they could give.

For now, she put the sandal back on and carried her suitcase toward the road. The belt tapped once against her heel, then again. She kept walking.
