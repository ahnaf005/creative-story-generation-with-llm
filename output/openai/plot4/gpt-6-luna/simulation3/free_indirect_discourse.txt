### The Last Beach

By the final week, everyone at the residency had begun speaking in the past tense.

The kitchen had been lively once, full of arguments about olives and the proper use of the washing machine. Now the drawers stood open while people searched for packing tape. A half-finished mural in the stairwell had acquired the sad, temporary look of a thing already being painted over.

Nora had come to Bellwether House to make a series of small clay boats. She had made one, which cracked in the kiln, and spent the rest of the month listening for the sea from her room at the back of the building. Theo had come to record the sounds of abandoned places. He had recorded the old schoolhouse, the empty swimming pool, and, apparently, Nora sleeping. At least, he had once played her a thirty-second clip of a room at night, with her breathing in it, and said it was the wind.

It could have been.

For three weeks they had found reasons to be together after dinner. A walk to the harbor. A bottle of wine on the roof. A kiss beside the laundry room while someone’s sheets turned in the dryer. Then, two nights later, the same kiss again, as though repetition might make it either more serious or less.

It had done neither. How efficient of it.

On Sunday morning, Theo stood in the doorway of Nora’s studio with two paper cups of coffee and the expression of a man who had already been forgiven.

“The bus leaves at ten,” he said.

“Does it?”

“I checked.”

Nora looked at the clay dust on her hands. She had been scraping at the cracked boat, though it was beyond repair. “And the bus goes to the beach?”

“It goes to a beach.”

“That’s reassuring.”

He held out the coffee. They had agreed to go the day before, after everyone else had declined. Too tired, too much packing, too many goodbyes. Nora had said she wanted one last swim. Theo had said he did too. Neither had mentioned that this was their last chance to be alone before she took the early train on Tuesday and he drove north on Monday.

The whole month had been made of last chances. There was no reason to start counting now.

They missed the bus by two minutes.

Nora watched its white back vanish around the bend, a clean and final gesture. Theo consulted his phone. No reception.

“You checked the schedule?” she asked.

“I checked the time.”

“An important distinction.”

There was another bus in two hours. Or, according to a handwritten sign on the shelter, there had been another bus in two hours before the summer timetable ended. They could wait, or walk to the next village, where a bus might pass, or take the coastal path, which Theo confidently identified on a map he had downloaded three days ago.

It was not the path on the map.

For a while, the walk was pleasant. The morning was bright and breezy, the sea shining between fields. Nora let herself believe they had stumbled into a better plan. They climbed a hill, passed a farm where a goat regarded them with intimate contempt, and followed a track between hedges.

Then the track ended at a locked gate.

Theo tested it. “It says there’s a way through.”

“It says there’s a gate.”

“There’s usually a way through.”

Usually, perhaps. Nora leaned on the post and watched the gate flex without opening. On the other side, the track continued downhill toward the sea.

Theo looked at her, smiling as if they were already making a story they would tell later. She wanted to kiss him. She wanted him to stop making everything easier to shrug off.

They turned back.

By the time they reached the road, the coffee had worn off, the sun had sharpened, and Nora’s canvas bag had developed a wet patch at the bottom. The jar of olives she had packed as a gesture of affection—or of lunch, it was hard to say—had leaked oil across a notebook and one of Theo’s shirts.

“Good,” he said, looking at the damage. “Now we have a reason to swim.”

“Olive oil is not a reason to swim.”

“It’s a very good reason.”

The next bus was a small, rattling thing that smelled of hot rubber. It took them to the wrong beach.

Not a bad beach. A stony cove beneath a row of summer houses, with a café shuttered for the season and a sign warning of strong currents. There were three people on the shore: a couple under a striped umbrella and a boy throwing a stick for a dog. Beyond them, the water made the kind of blue that seemed intended to settle arguments.

Theo laid out his shirt to dry. Nora put the oil-soaked notebook in the sun, where the pages began to curl.

“We made it,” he said.

“Here, yes.”

“Do you want to swim?”

She looked at the water. The stones shifted under the edge of each small wave. She had wanted to swim all week. In practice, the idea had become less appealing. Still, she took off her shoes.

Theo waded in first, with a little shiver he tried to disguise as a laugh. Nora watched him go until the water reached his waist, then followed. It was cold enough to make her breathe through her teeth. For a moment she felt young and unobserved, her body reduced to the bright shock of water, the rocks underfoot, Theo’s head moving farther from shore.

Then he splashed her.

It was not a good splash. A weak, badly aimed one. She splashed him back, harder. He ducked and came up laughing, and she was laughing too, though the water was cold and her hair had got in her mouth.

When they came out, the boy’s dog ran off with Nora’s sandwich.

It had been wrapped in paper, cheese already soft from the heat. The dog carried it several yards before the boy caught up, apologizing in the cheerful, useless way of someone whose dog had done this before.

“Honestly,” Nora said, watching the sandwich disappear into a bin. “It’s all right.”

Theo offered her the remaining half of his apple. He had a bite of it, then gave it to her. She ate around the mark. Such a small, domestic gesture. It was almost unbearable.

They sat on a low wall above the beach. Theo’s phone, which he had been checking every few minutes for a signal, finally buzzed.

A message, then a call. He turned away to answer. Nora did not mean to listen. But the cove held sound close; she heard the quick, familiar ease in his voice, the word Mum, then Monday, then yes, I’ll be there.

Of course. He was leaving tomorrow.

When he came back, he slipped the phone into his pocket. “My sister’s picking me up.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Yeah. She’s coming down from Leeds.”

Nora brushed sand off her knees. “That’s convenient.”

“It is. I hate the drive.”

There. Nothing had happened. No name had been mentioned, no invitation made. She could have asked whether he would call when he got home. She could have said she would like him to. A woman could put a sentence into the air and see what became of it.

She was tired of seeing what became of things.

“Your train’s Tuesday?” he asked.

“Six forty.”

“Brutal.”

“Good. I’ll be too tired to miss anything.”

He looked at her, then at the water. His expression was not unreadable; it was merely familiar. That was worse. She knew his face after a sleepless night, the small crease between his brows when the house’s Wi-Fi failed, the way he tucked his hands into his sleeves when he was cold. She knew him well enough to know how little she knew.

A gull landed on the wall between them and watched their lunch disappear into the distance.

They found a second route back to the village, which involved a narrow bridge and a stretch of road without a footpath. At the bridge, Theo’s notebook slipped from beneath his arm and fell into the stream. He lunged after it, missed, and nearly went in himself. Nora grabbed the back of his shirt. For one brief second they stood together, unbalanced over the water, her fist twisted in the fabric between his shoulder blades.

“You’ve got me,” he said.

“I’ve got your shirt.”

“Same thing, surely.”

She let go.

The notebook caught on a branch downstream. Theo retrieved it with a stick and shook it out, sending droplets over both of them. The pages were ruined. He studied them, damp and darkening.

“Recordings are backed up,” he said. “Notes aren’t.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I’m not. They were probably bad notes.”

He said it lightly. Nora wondered if he meant the notebook, or the things he had almost said in it. She didn’t ask.

They were back at Bellwether House by late afternoon. The residents were having a farewell meal in the garden, though none of them called it that. Someone had bought too much wine. Someone else had put all the chairs in a circle, which gave the evening the strained atmosphere of a meeting.

Nora showered and changed into a clean shirt. The cracked clay boat sat on her desk, gray and lopsided. She had thought about taking it home, but she could not imagine explaining it. The boat was an attempt to make a thing that could carry something over water. It had failed before it touched any.

Outside, Theo was telling a story about the gate. He made Nora sound braver than she had been, and himself more foolish. People laughed. She laughed too, from the kitchen doorway.

After dinner they carried the empty wine bottles to the recycling bins. The night was cool, with a little salt in it. The courtyard lights gave the house a temporary glow, as if it had been built only for this month and would be gone by morning.

“Did you have a good day?” Theo asked.

“A very successful one.”

“We did get to the beach.”

“We saw a beach.”

“We swam.”

“A bit.”

“We lost your sandwich.”

“We lost a notebook.”

“I got wet.”

“You did.”

They stood by the bins, neither moving back toward the house. The space between them was small enough to cross and large enough to keep.

He looked tired. She wanted to touch his cheek. She wanted him to ask her to stay, though she had no intention of staying. He could not ask. She would not say yes. Still, the wanting sat there, undamaged by reason.

“Will you come to the opening?” he asked.

“Which opening?”

“The one in October. At the gallery. They’re showing some of the recordings.”

“I don’t know where I’ll be in October.”

“Right.” He nodded. “Of course.”

The phrase had the soft, polished sound of a door closing.

“Send me the details,” she said.

“I will.”

She could have said, and I’ll come if I can. But she didn’t know if she would. She could have said, I want to see you again. But that was not the same as wanting to go to an opening, and surely he knew the difference. Surely he didn’t. Surely it was unfair to expect him to know what she had taken such care not to tell him.

The next morning, before the others woke, Nora went downstairs to get water. Theo’s room stood open. His bed was stripped, the window still cracked to let in the sea air. His car was gone.

On the kitchen table, beside the empty jar of olives, he had left a note: *Made it through the gate in the end. Sort of. Thanks for the shirt rescue.*

There was a small drawing of a goat underneath.

Nora read it twice. Then she folded it and slipped it into the notebook with the curled, oil-stained pages. It was a ridiculous thing to keep. It was also the only thing she had from the beach that hadn’t been lost, eaten, or soaked through.

By six forty, the station platform was bright with morning. Nora stood under the timetable with her bag between her feet. The cracked boat was wrapped in a towel inside it. She had decided, at the last minute, to bring it home.

A train arrived, doors opening with a sigh. She stepped aboard. The residency had been good for her, she thought. Not in the ways she’d planned, perhaps. Not every good thing needed to survive the place where it began.

The doors closed. Behind her, the platform slid away, carrying no one she knew. Ahead, the rails held their course. For a while, the sea kept appearing and vanishing between the fields, blue as a promise no one had made.
