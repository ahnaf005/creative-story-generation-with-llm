On the last morning of the residency, Mara and Elias missed the bus to the beach because Elias had gone back for his notebook and Mara had gone back for the scarf she’d already decided not to wear.

They watched the bus turn the corner without them.

“That’s probably for the best,” Elias said. He held his notebook against his chest, though the sky was clear.

Mara looked at the scarf in her hand. “Probably.”

They were staying in an old boarding school above the harbor, where the residency had put twelve artists in rooms designed for children who had since grown old enough to have children of their own. Their work filled the classrooms. In the corridors, the radiators knocked all night, and someone was always looking for a phone charger. For six weeks, Mara had painted in the former music room while Elias wrote in the little library at the end of the hall. They had learned each other’s habits by accident: that he peeled labels from bottles when he was thinking, that she sang only when she couldn’t hear herself, that neither of them slept well when the wind came in from the east.

They had learned other things on purpose.

Now the residency had three days left. In the morning, Mara would take a train north. Elias would fly home the next evening. The beach trip had been suggested by one of the other residents, who had then developed a migraine and stayed behind. Mara and Elias went anyway, though neither had said exactly why.

The next bus was in an hour. Elias proposed they walk to the car rental place.

“I thought you said you didn’t want to drive.”

“I don’t. But I want to be at the beach before it gets crowded.”

“It’s a Tuesday.”

“People have Tuesday.”

At the rental place, the clerk gave them a small white car with a smell of coconut air freshener and a warning that the fuel gauge was unreliable. Elias drove. Mara read the directions aloud, then stopped when he missed the turn.

“You’re allowed to tell me,” he said.

“I did tell you.”

“You said, ‘There’s a road.’”

“There was a road.”

He took the next street, which ran between two rows of houses and ended at a hedge. They turned around while a man watering his front garden watched them with the grave attention of a referee.

By the time they reached the beach, the tide was already pulling back. It left long bands of wet sand shining under a thin veil of cloud. There were families at the far end, and a dog with a red ball, but the stretch beside the rocks was nearly empty.

Mara kicked off her shoes. Elias stayed beside the car, peering at the fuel gauge.

“It’s fine,” she called.

“I’m checking.”

“You’ve checked.”

“I’m checking again.”

She walked down to the water. The sea was cold enough to sting her ankles. She stood there until Elias joined her, carrying the picnic bag and a folded blanket.

“What did you bring?” she asked.

“Bread. Cheese. Apples. A thermos.”

“Of what?”

“Coffee.”

“You hate coffee.”

“I thought you liked it.”

“I do. I don’t like yours.”

He looked at her. “You’ve never had mine.”

“I’ve watched you make it.”

This seemed to bother him. He put the bag down, careful not to let it tip. The blanket opened in the wind, snapped once, and went skittering away across the sand. Elias ran after it. Mara ran after Elias. The blanket lodged against a rock and, when he tried to free it, the corner tore.

They carried the damaged thing back between them.

“I can sew it,” Mara said.

“You can paint a ceiling with a mop.”

“That was a different situation.”

“It was the ceiling of your studio.”

“The ceiling had water damage.”

“The ceiling was blue.”

“It was meant to be blue.”

They laughed. The sound went out over the water and came back smaller.

They spread the blanket in a hollow between two dunes, weighting the corners with shoes. Elias poured coffee into the thermos cap and offered it to Mara. She took a sip. It was too hot and too bitter. She swallowed without making a face.

“Well?” he asked.

“Exactly as I expected.”

He smiled, but his eyes stayed on her a moment longer than the joke required.

They ate bread and cheese. Elias sliced an apple with his pocketknife, cutting uneven quarters. Mara had always liked his hands: long-fingered, ink-stained around the nails, a pale crescent scar on his thumb. She had once asked about the scar and he’d said it was from a jar. The answer seemed designed to stop questions. She hadn’t asked again.

A gull swooped down and stole half an apple. Elias swore, sprang up, and chased it a few steps. The bird rose with its prize, looking offended by the pursuit.

“Your lunch is gone,” Mara said.

“Most of it.”

“You could have shared.”

“I’m not sure it would have thanked me.”

The gull dropped the apple on a rock. It bounced once and disappeared into a tide pool. Elias watched it go.

“Should we get it?” Mara asked.

“No.”

“Good. I didn’t want to.”

They sat facing the water. Elias told her about a book he was trying to write, not the one he’d described during the residency’s weekly readings, but another one. It had no title yet and, as far as she could tell, no plot. He talked about a man who returned to his childhood town and found that everything was just slightly smaller than he remembered.

“Why smaller?” Mara asked.

“Because he was bigger.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No,” he said. “It isn’t.”

She told him about a painting she wanted to make when she got home. A row of windows at night, each one holding a different version of the same room. She hadn’t told anyone about it. She wasn’t sure it was an idea so much as a feeling she’d been carrying around.

“Will you paint it?” he asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You should.”

“That’s easy for you to say.”

“I’m saying it because I want to see it.”

She picked at the edge of the torn blanket. “You won’t.”

He looked toward the water. “I could.”

The words were small. They found their way between them anyway.

Mara tucked her hands into her sleeves. The wind lifted her scarf and pushed it against her cheek. She had brought it after all. It smelled faintly of turpentine from where she’d left it draped over a chair in her studio.

“What time do we have to return the car?” she asked.

“Five.”

“It’s almost three.”

“We have two hours.”

“We could swim.”

“I didn’t bring a towel.”

“We have a blanket.”

“We also have a blanket with a hole.”

“Then it’s a towel with a hole.”

Elias sighed as if she’d asked him to climb a mountain, but he stood. They waded in together. The cold struck hard at their knees, then their waists. Mara yelped and grabbed his arm. Elias laughed, then yelped himself when a wave hit his stomach.

They made it no farther than thigh-deep. Mara tried to splash him and missed. Elias tried to splash her and soaked his own shirt. A larger wave rushed in and took his glasses.

For several seconds they stared at each other, blinded in different ways. Elias bent to search the shallows. Mara crouched too, sweeping her hands across the sand. The water clouded around them.

“I can’t see anything,” he said.

“I know.”

“Can you see them?”

“No.”

“Then what are you doing?”

“Helping.”

They found the glasses half-buried by the time the tide began pulling back again. One arm had bent outward. Elias held them up, dripping.

“They’re ruined,” Mara said.

“They were already old.”

“Convenient.”

He slid them on, crookedly. “I can still see you.”

She didn’t answer. The water folded around their legs and withdrew.

On the shore, they dried themselves as best they could with the blanket. Elias’s shirt clung to him. Mara rubbed her arms, laughing at nothing. Then he took her hands, briefly, to warm them between his palms.

It was not the first time he had touched her. It was the first time she had felt him waiting to see what she would do.

She could have leaned in. She knew that. She also knew he might have kissed her, and she knew she would have kissed him back. The knowledge sat between them like something packed for a journey.

Instead she said, “Your glasses are crooked.”

He let her straighten them.

At four, they packed up and walked toward the car. The fuel gauge, which had been hovering near empty all afternoon, dropped to nothing as soon as Elias turned the key. He stared at it.

“You said it was fine,” Mara said.

“I said I was checking.”

The engine started, which seemed to surprise them both. They drove toward the nearest service station, following the signs until they found themselves on a narrow road that ran inland through fields. The car began to sputter.

“Do we have enough?” Mara asked.

“I think so.”

“You thought it was fine.”

“I thought the gauge was unreliable.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it’s unreliable in a different way.”

The car shuddered once, then rolled to a stop beside a field of yellow flowers. Elias turned the key. Nothing happened.

They sat in silence.

Mara looked through the window at the flowers, which bent and lifted in the wind. “It’s a nice place to break down.”

“Could be worse.”

“Could be raining.”

“Could be in the sea.”

She laughed. He did too, and then they both stopped.

Elias called the rental company. They were told a tow truck would arrive within an hour. While they waited, they walked along the verge to a farmhouse and asked for water. The farmer gave them a jug and looked at their wet clothes, the torn blanket, Elias’s crooked glasses, and the white car abandoned by the roadside.

“Day at the beach?” she asked.

“Something like that,” Mara said.

The farmer brought them a plate of plums. They sat on a low stone wall and ate them one at a time. The fruit was soft and sweet. Juice ran down Elias’s wrist. Mara wiped it away with her thumb before thinking. He watched her do it, then looked down at the plum in his hand.

The tow truck came. It took them back to town in the cab, where the driver asked if they were on holiday.

“Not exactly,” Elias said.

“Almost finished,” Mara added.

The driver nodded as if this explained everything.

By the time they reached the boarding school, the other residents had gathered in the courtyard for their last dinner. Someone had set out paper lanterns. Someone else had made too much rice. The evening had the busy, brittle feeling of a room where no one wanted to be the first to leave.

Mara and Elias stood at the edge of it, still damp and smelling of salt.

“Your hair’s full of sand,” he said.

“Yours too.”

He reached as if to brush it away, then let his hand fall.

They were leaving in the morning. In their rooms, their suitcases waited open. The house would empty in stages: a cab at dawn, a train after lunch, a taxi to the airport at dusk. Soon the rooms would belong to someone else, and the radiators would knock for people who had not yet learned their names.

“I’ll send you the painting,” Mara said.

“Good.”

“You’ll send me the book?”

“When it has a title.”

“That could take years.”

“I know.”

She watched him look across the courtyard at the lanterns, their pale light trembling in the wind. She wanted to ask him to stay one more night. She wanted to ask whether he would have kissed her if she’d leaned closer. She wanted to ask whether any of this meant the same thing to him as it did to her.

Instead she said, “I’m going to find some dry clothes.”

“Me too.”

They went inside separately.

In the morning, Mara left first. Elias came down to the front steps carrying his suitcase and the damaged glasses. He stood in the doorway while she put her bag in the taxi.

“Safe trip,” he said.

“You too.”

There was a pause. The driver waited with one hand on the wheel.

Mara looked at Elias, at the scar on his thumb, the ink still dark beneath his nails. He looked back at her. Neither of them moved.

Then he leaned forward and kissed her cheek. His face was cool from the hall. The kiss was gentle, almost formal, and it left her more uncertain than a goodbye would have.

She got into the taxi.

As it pulled away, she saw him turn toward the house. She watched until the corner took him from view. Then she opened her phone and started a message: *The windows should be lit from inside.*

She read it once, deleted it, and watched the road unspool ahead of her. Outside, the sea appeared between the buildings and disappeared again.
