### The Last Beach

On the morning of their last beach trip, the bus left without them.

It did not even have the decency to hesitate. Mara saw its red rear lights slide around the corner of the square while she and Elias were still arguing over whether the map on her phone was upside down. They stood beneath the residency’s archway with a paper bag of peaches between them and the sea somewhere beyond the hills, unreachable for at least another hour.

“Your map says we’re in the harbor,” Elias said.

“It says the harbor is south.”

“And we’re facing?”

“South.”

“Then why does it say we’re in the harbor?”

The bus pulled away. He looked after it, pleased with the outcome, as if the map had arranged the whole thing for his entertainment.

Mara put her phone away. “We’ll catch the next one.”

“There isn’t a next one until two.”

She knew that. She had read the schedule at breakfast, though she’d pretended not to care. Two o’clock meant a shorter day at the beach, which was the point of going: to have one last whole day before the residency ended, before everyone scattered to the places they’d come from or the places they’d been promised. She and Elias had not said “last” aloud. It had been hovering between them since Tuesday, like a gull deciding whether to steal something.

They could walk to the train station, he said. There was a train to the coast.

“Of course there is,” Mara said.

It took them forty minutes to reach the station, partly because they went the wrong way, and partly because Elias kept stopping to photograph things that were not, to Mara’s mind, worth stopping for: a bent road sign, a dog asleep beneath a bakery table, a row of plastic chairs stacked in a courtyard.

“Do you need a picture of every chair in France?”

“Not every chair.”

“Just the important ones?”

He gave her the bright, untroubled smile that had got him through the residency dinners. He would take a picture of the chairs and leave them behind. He would take a picture of the dog and leave that behind too. His camera was full of the evidence of being somewhere, with none of the inconvenience of staying.

At the station, the ticket machine ate her coin. Elias hit the green button twice, with unnecessary tenderness. The machine kept the coin and offered them a ticket for the wrong town.

“See?” he said. “The day’s already giving us something.”

“It’s giving us a ticket to Saint-Roch.”

“A beautiful place.”

“We’re going to Saint-Roch.”

The train was delayed, then changed platforms. When it arrived, it was packed with people carrying sun hats and coolers. Mara and Elias stood in the aisle, their shoulders pressed together whenever the train took a curve. It was almost like holding hands, if one made no effort to hold. She wondered whether he noticed that she was keeping her fingers curled around the strap of her bag.

At the coast they bought bread, cheese, and two bottles of water from a shop where the woman behind the counter spoke to them as if they were children who had wandered away from school. Elias bought a small tin of sardines too, though neither of them owned a can opener. He said he would manage. He always said that.

They walked down to the beach past a row of shuttered cafés. The tide was out, leaving a broad plain of wet sand scored with channels. Beyond it, the sea shone silver under a sky that could not decide whether to be blue.

“Perfect,” Elias said.

A gust lifted the paper bag from Mara’s hand. The peaches rolled down the slope. One stopped against a rock. The other kept going, bouncing, then dropping neatly into a shallow pool.

“Still perfect,” he said.

She retrieved the dry peach. Elias went after the wet one and came back with a sandy sleeve and a peach that had split open in his palm. He ate it over the water, juice running down his wrist.

They found a place above the high-water line and laid out their things. The beach was nearly empty. A few families clustered near the lifeguard hut; farther down, two men were walking a white horse in the shallows. It was the kind of day Mara would have wanted to remember. The trouble with wanting to remember was that it made each minute feel like something she was already losing.

Elias took off his shirt and went into the water. He turned at the edge of the waves and called to her.

“It’s freezing.”

“You’re the one who said you wanted to swim.”

“I said I wanted to come to the beach.”

He splashed water at her. She stayed where she was. The wind tugged at the hem of her dress and cooled the backs of her knees. Elias dove under a wave, surfaced, and pushed his wet hair out of his eyes.

She could have gone in. There would have been his hand, offered without question. The sun on their shoulders later. The easy, private quiet of drying beside him. Instead she sat down and unwrapped the bread.

The gull stole half their lunch.

It came down fast, seized the cheese, and flapped off before Elias could even stand. A second gull waited on the rock above them, watching.

“Thief,” Mara said.

“Resourceful.”

“It was my lunch.”

“It was our lunch.”

“Then why didn’t it take the sardines?”

He looked at the unopened tin. “It knows a bad bargain.”

They ate what was left, picking sand out of the bread. Elias told her about the grant in Rotterdam, which he had mentioned once before, though only as a possibility. It had become a certainty while she was sleeping, or maybe while he was sleeping. He would leave in three weeks. The work would be good for him. A studio by the river, access to equipment, money for six months. He was telling her the practical facts, not asking for her opinion. Still, some part of her waited for him to add a second sentence. The one that would make it their news.

“That’s good,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

They were both silent. The sea moved in and out below them, rearranging the sand with the patience of someone tidying a room they would soon leave.

Mara had her own news. A teaching job in Vermont, temporary, only a semester, but it would pay her rent. She had accepted it on Monday. The email was still unanswered in her inbox, though it was not the email that required an answer. The hard part was already done. She had told the director yes, and told Elias nothing.

He lay back on his elbows. “You look like you’re about to say something.”

“I’m thinking.”

“That’s usually what happens before you say something.”

“Not with me.”

“No?”

“No.”

He looked at her, waiting. There it was: the small, open space where a question could go. She could ask if he ever intended to come back. She could tell him about Vermont. She could say that she liked the way he fell asleep with one hand open beside her, as if even in sleep he were prepared to receive something. Or ask him what, exactly, they were supposed to call the weeks they’d spent crossing from one room to another after midnight.

A wave broke on the rocks below. The white horse, farther down the beach, had stopped to drink.

Instead she said, “Your camera’s getting wet.”

He looked at the camera beside his bag. The sea breeze had blown a spray across the lens. He began wiping it with the edge of his shirt.

“Careful,” she said.

“Now you’re interested.”

“I’m interested in not carrying a wet camera back to the bus.”

“It’s a train.”

“Whatever we’re missing next.”

He laughed, and the moment moved on. It did not disappear. It simply went somewhere they couldn’t reach.

They walked toward the headland in the afternoon. There was supposed to be a path along the cliffs, but the first section had been closed with a sign they didn’t understand. Elias climbed over the low barrier. Mara followed, annoyed that he’d made it seem effortless.

The path narrowed and turned behind a stand of scrub. On the other side, a man in a fluorescent vest waved them back. There had been a small rockslide. Nothing dangerous, he said, but they would have to go around. By the time they found the alternate route, the clouds had thickened and the air smelled metallic.

“We could turn around,” Elias said.

“Why?”

“Because it’s going to rain.”

“Then we’ll get wet.”

He studied her for a moment. “Right.”

It began raining before they reached the beach. Not a storm, only a quick, hard shower that drove them beneath the overhang of a low stone wall. They stood close together, listening to the rain tick against the grass. Elias’s sleeve brushed hers. He smelled of salt and the peach he’d eaten.

Mara wanted to touch his face. Not as a question. Not to see what would happen. Just because she wanted to, which was a poor reason and the only one she had.

He turned toward her. His gaze dropped briefly to her mouth. Then he looked past her, toward the beach, where the rain was already thinning.

It was a small thing, that glance away. A sensible thing, even. But it found the tender spot she’d been keeping covered.

“Your Rotterdam people must be excited,” she said.

“My Rotterdam people?”

“The ones with the money and the river.”

“They don’t know I’m coming yet.”

“You said you’d accepted.”

“I said I’d probably accept.”

“Oh.”

It was the first time she had heard the uncertainty in his voice. He’d spoken about the grant as if its future were a train timetable, fixed and indifferent. Perhaps it wasn’t. Perhaps he had been waiting to see if she would ask him to stay. The thought was so tempting she distrusted it immediately.

“Well,” she said. “You should go.”

“That’s a very useful thing to hear.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

They walked back to the station in the rain, taking the longer road to avoid the rockslide. The train was on time. They found seats facing each other, knees nearly touching between the benches. Elias took out his camera and scrolled through the photographs. The bent sign, the sleeping dog, the stacked chairs. The split peach. The empty beach under the silver sky.

He stopped at one of Mara.

She was sitting above the tide line, looking away from the camera. Her hair had blown across her cheek. In the picture, she looked both bored and alert, like someone waiting to hear her name called.

“You took that without asking,” she said.

“It was a good moment.”

“It was me sitting down.”

“Good moments are often that.”

She wanted him to delete it. She wanted him to print it. She wanted to know whether he would remember the dress, the rain, the way she had nearly touched his face and then hadn’t.

“Send it to me,” she said.

He did. Her phone chimed. She looked at the photograph on the small screen. It was not how she remembered herself, which was perhaps the point.

Back at the residency, the courtyard was full of suitcases. Someone had put a bottle of wine on the stone table and written LAST NIGHT on a paper napkin. A group of residents were arguing over music in the kitchen. There were hugs already, although most people would not leave until morning.

Elias had to pack. Mara did too. Her room was still full of wet canvases and folded clothes and the particular mess of a person who had been waiting to see how long she could stay.

In the corridor outside his room, he leaned against the doorframe.

“Are you leaving tomorrow?” he asked.

“On Friday.”

He nodded. “Vermont?”

She had not told him. The knowledge arrived in his face a second before she understood he’d found out from someone else.

“Yes.”

“Teaching?”

“Just for a semester.”

“Good,” he said.

There was their word again, doing the work of a bridge neither of them intended to cross.

He stepped closer. For a second she thought he might kiss her. He did not. She could have kissed him. She could have put both hands against his chest and made the decision for them, though what decision? That it mattered. That it had mattered. That it was ending.

Instead, she reached out and picked a piece of dried grass from his shoulder.

“There,” she said.

“Thanks.”

He caught her hand before she could pull it away. His thumb rested against the base of her finger, warm and steady. He could have held on. She could have turned her palm up. Neither of them did.

“Good night, Mara.”

“Good night, Elias.”

She went to her room and finished packing. The photograph remained open on her phone: herself on the beach, looking away. She tried to decide whether he had caught her in a moment of leaving or one of staying. She could not tell. Outside, the last train of the day passed through town, its windows lit against the dark.

In the morning, they would take different buses. The sea would be there without them, washing the sand clean of their footprints. How kind of it, to keep nothing. How cruel. She put the phone face down and folded her dress into the suitcase. At least the peach had been good. At least the train had arrived. At least, for one afternoon, they had been side by side in the rain, not saying what they wanted, and neither of them had mistaken silence for nothing.
