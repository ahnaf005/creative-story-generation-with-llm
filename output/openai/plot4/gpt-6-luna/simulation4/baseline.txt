### The Last Beach

On the last Friday of the residency, Mara found Lev in the kitchen trying to open a jar of apricot jam with a spoon.

“You’re going to break the spoon,” she said.

“I’m trying not to break the jar.”

The spoon bent. The jar remained sealed.

They were leaving the next morning: Mara by train, Lev in a rented car headed west. For six weeks they had shared the narrow bed in his room, though not every night, and had never discussed what the arrangement might be called. In the residency’s old schoolhouse, where the other artists slept behind numbered doors and the corridors smelled of damp wool, it had seemed unnecessary to name anything. Tomorrow, they would carry their bags downstairs and become people who had once known each other very closely.

“Last beach trip,” Mara said. “We should bring the jam.”

Lev put the bent spoon in the drawer. “It may be safer to leave it here.”

They set off in the residency’s battered green minibus after lunch. The other residents had planned to come, but one had a migraine, two were finishing a film, and the sculptor from room twelve had disappeared with a local fisherman. In the end there were only Mara and Lev, plus the minibus, which shuddered whenever it went uphill.

Mara sat by the window with her recorder in her coat pocket. She collected sounds: pipes clicking, floorboards sighing, the particular silence after a door closed. Lev made paintings of people he didn’t know. At the residency he had painted Mara twice, though neither portrait looked like her. In one she had a fox’s narrow face; in the other she seemed to be listening to someone just outside the frame.

The road to the beach had been closed by a fallen tree. Lev followed a detour that led them through a village, past a church and a shop selling bait, then onto a lane that narrowed until the hedges brushed the minibus’s sides.

“Are you sure this is right?” Mara asked.

“No.”

The lane ended at a farm gate. A handwritten sign said PRIVATE ROAD. The minibus’s engine made a faint ticking sound.

“Good,” Mara said. “I was worried we’d have a normal afternoon.”

Lev grinned, but only briefly. He had been doing that lately: offering a smile, then withdrawing it as if he had remembered it belonged to him.

They retraced their route and found another turnoff, this one leading to a beach marked on Lev’s phone as CROW’S END. It was a strip of stones beneath a high, grassy bank. A dog raced past them carrying a red rubber boot. Its owner hurried after it, shouting apologies that the wind took apart before they reached the two of them.

“Do you think it’s a sign?” Lev asked.

“Of what?”

“Couldn’t say.”

They climbed over the bank. The sea was slate-colored and restless, its white edges folding over each other. Mara took off her boots and rolled her trousers to her knees. Lev kept his shoes on, though he stood at the water’s edge long enough for the next wave to fill them.

He looked down at his wet feet. “There’s a lesson here.”

“About footwear?”

“About trying not to get wet.”

They had packed sandwiches, two apples, a thermos of coffee, and the unopened jar of jam. Lev had also brought the residency’s striped picnic blanket, which he had spread on a patch of dry stones. The wind lifted one corner. Then all four. The blanket wrapped around him like a sail, and Mara caught it just as he staggered backward into a tide pool.

She laughed so hard she had to sit down. He bowed, dripping. For a while they were the people they had been earlier in the summer, when the days were still long and nobody was packing.

They ate their sandwiches. A gull swooped down and snatched the cheese from Lev’s hand.

“An accomplished thief,” he said, watching it rise.

“You looked too generous.”

“I am.”

Mara handed him half her apple. “Don’t start.”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“No. You left room for me to imagine it.”

That was how their last few days had been. Every ordinary remark arrived with a second meaning, and neither of them acknowledged it. He asked what time her train was. She asked whether his car had enough space for his paintings. They discussed luggage as if luggage were the only thing they would have to leave behind.

They walked west along the stones, where the beach narrowed beneath the bank. The tide was coming in. Mara found a piece of blue glass and held it to the light. Lev picked up a smooth black pebble, turned it once in his palm, and slipped it into his pocket.

“What are you doing with that?” she asked.

“Keeping it.”

“You don’t keep things.”

“I know.”

They continued until the headland blocked the beach. Beyond it, a shelf of rock ran toward a cluster of caves. Lev stepped onto the first slab, testing it with his heel. Mara followed. The rocks were slick with weed, and halfway across she slipped. Lev caught her arm.

For a moment, they stood close together, her hand tight around his sleeve. He smelled of coffee and the oil paint that never quite left his skin. His thumb rested against her wrist.

“Careful,” he said.

“I am.”

Neither of them moved.

Then a wave slapped the rocks below and sprayed cold water up their legs. They scrambled back, laughing again, though Mara’s pulse continued to race after they were safely on the stones.

On the way back, they found that their shoes had been nudged by the rising tide. One of Lev’s was full of water; the other had vanished altogether. They searched the beach, scanning the line of debris and kelp, until Mara spotted it wedged between two rocks. She pulled it free by the heel.

“You see?” she said. “You’re not allowed to leave things behind.”

“Not even a shoe?”

“Especially a shoe. It’s embarrassing.”

He sat to empty it. She crouched beside him, and they bumped heads.

“Ow.”

“Sorry.”

“You’ve been very reckless with my head today.”

“You’ve been putting it in my way.”

He looked up. There it was again: the almost-smile, the possibility of more. She wanted to say, Stay another week. She wanted to ask if he had ever thought of her outside the rooms they’d borrowed from the residency. Instead she brushed a grain of sand from his collar.

“Your train’s at ten?” he asked.

“Ten twenty.”

“I’ll drive you.”

“You have to head west.”

“Later.”

“You’ll have your paintings, your bags, and a rental car.”

“I can manage.”

The offer was small enough to accept and large enough to misunderstand.

“Thank you,” she said.

They sat on the bank while the tide slid over the stones below. The wind combed at Mara’s hair. Lev took the black pebble from his pocket and turned it between his fingers.

“Where will you go after?” she asked.

“First the coast. Then my sister’s for a few days. Then I suppose I’ll find somewhere to work.”

“You always say ‘suppose’ when you already know.”

“I know where I’m going. I don’t know what I’m going to do there.”

She waited. He looked at the pebble, not at her.

“And you?” he asked.

“Back to Toronto. My studio. The pipes that bang all night.”

“You miss them?”

“I miss knowing when they’ll make a sound.”

He nodded, as if she had told him something difficult. She considered telling him that she would miss his footsteps crossing the corridor, his half-asleep hand reaching for her, the way he sang wrong lyrics while making coffee. She considered asking whether he would miss her. The beach kept making its restless, unanswering noise.

The weather turned suddenly, as it had all day. A fine rain began to tick against the bank. They hurried to the minibus, where Lev discovered that he had locked the keys inside.

Mara stared at him. “You did what?”

“I put them on the seat.”

“Why?”

“I was carrying the blanket.”

“The blanket is not a key.”

They tried the passenger door. It was locked. Lev crouched at the window and peered in, as if the keys might feel ashamed and jump into his hand. The rain thickened. Mara covered her head with the picnic blanket, now damp and smelling of salt.

A woman from the nearby farm appeared on a quad bike. She listened to their story, laughed, and called her brother, who arrived with a long metal rod and a careful expression. He wedged it between the door and the frame while Lev offered advice no one needed. At last the lock clicked open.

Back at the schoolhouse, they carried the wet blanket and sand-filled shoes inside. The others had gathered in the dining room for a final supper. Someone had made lentil stew; someone else had opened a bottle of wine. The evening took them in, as though nothing had happened except a pleasant trip to the shore.

Later, in the corridor, Mara and Lev stopped outside his room. He had packed most of his paintings already, stacking them against the wall like another set of doors.

“Thank you for today,” she said.

“Thank you for the shoe.”

He held the black pebble in his palm. For a second, she thought he might give it to her. Instead, he put it on the windowsill between them.

“Take it,” he said. “You’re better at keeping things.”

She looked at the pebble, then at him. “That’s not true.”

“No?”

“No.”

He leaned toward her. She thought he was going to kiss her, and then he did. It was a careful kiss, nothing like the first one, which had happened in the dark after a late dinner, both of them laughing at the sound of someone practicing trumpet in the courtyard. This kiss felt like a question that neither of them wanted to ask aloud.

When they parted, he rested his forehead against hers.

“Ten twenty,” he said.

“I know.”

In the morning he drove her to the station, her suitcase wedged beside a stack of canvases. The sky was clear. The town looked newly rinsed and oddly familiar. At the platform, Lev lifted her case down and set it beside her.

“I’ll write,” he said.

She smiled. “You hate writing.”

“I’ll send a photograph, then.”

“Of what?”

He glanced at the train pulling in. “I’ll see.”

She reached for him, and he took her hand. For one brief moment, it was easy: his warm fingers around hers, the train doors open behind her, the day still waiting.

Then the doors chimed. She let go.

As the train gathered speed, Mara watched Lev grow smaller on the platform. He raised a hand; she raised hers back. She thought of the pebble on the windowsill, the gull carrying away the cheese, the sea lifting and lowering itself around the stones. At the next bend, the station disappeared. In her coat pocket, her recorder clicked softly against her phone, holding the sound of the train, the wind, and—if she listened closely—the door closing.
