The car smelled of wet wool and peaches.

Mara had put the peaches in the back seat because Theo said the cooler was leaking, though the cooler was not leaking; it was merely damp around the zipper, like everything in the residency house. Now the peaches rolled whenever he took a turn, tapping the door with their small, accusing bodies. The sea was still forty minutes away. Their last beach trip, he had said, as if the phrase belonged to the calendar and not to them.

“Did you bring the towels?” he asked.

“I thought you brought the towels.”

He looked at her. She looked back. They had each brought a book.

The residency had given them six weeks in a former school for girls on a hill above the harbor. The building had narrow stairs, a bell nobody could make work, and a dining room where eleven artists tried not to ask one another what they were making. Mara had been making charcoal drawings of empty rooms. Theo said they looked like places somebody had just left. He said it as a compliment, or she had decided it was one.

They had started sleeping together in the third week, after a storm kept everyone inside and the power went out. It was not, strictly speaking, an arrangement. Arrangements had rules. They had only the repeated discovery that the other was awake, and that the walk between their rooms was very short. In the mornings he made coffee; she stole it. In the evenings he sometimes came to her door and sometimes did not. She had learned not to count.

Now he was leaving for Berlin on Monday. Mara was leaving for Chicago the day after. There was a group dinner on Sunday, a reading on Saturday, and the beach today. A little weekend of good behavior before they returned to being people who had once met.

Theo pulled over at a gas station. “I’ll get ice.”

“Could you also get towels?”

He grinned. “They have towels at the beach.”

“Not the kind you want to lie on.”

He was gone long enough for the car to begin feeling like a decision. Mara watched him through the window as he stood in line behind a man buying scratch tickets. A woman in a pink visor was filling a paper cup with coffee. Behind the counter, a radio announcer reported rain tomorrow. The world had made all its plans without consulting them.

When Theo came back, he had ice, two bottles of water, and no towels. He held up a packet of gummy worms.

“Emergency supplies,” he said.

“You’re very brave.”

“I’m trying to keep us alive.”

They drove on. In the glove box, the directions had softened at the folds. Theo’s phone had lost signal and the paper map was upside down. He turned it around, then turned it again. Neither of them said that they had passed the road. They had both seen the sign; they had both thought the other would speak.

At last Mara pointed. “That one.”

“Was that the one?”

“It was the one with the beach picture.”

“Good. I like a road with a clear sense of purpose.”

The road narrowed between pines and opened, suddenly, to a small parking lot above the water. The beach was nearly empty. A gray-blue afternoon, though the forecast had promised sun. Far out, a line of gulls stood on the surface of the sea as if it were a pavement. They carried the peaches, the gummy worms, and two books down the sandy path.

They had no towels. This became a problem immediately.

Theo spread his jacket on the sand. Mara sat on one side and tried not to notice that the hem was wet. He put the other jacket over a rock, which seemed less like a plan than a refusal to admit there was no plan. She laughed, and he laughed too. For a moment it was easy. The air smelled of salt and pine. He looked younger in the wind, his hair blown into his eyes, his face unguarded.

“Should’ve brought the towels,” he said.

“I did bring one.”

She pulled a thin dish towel from her bag, a checked thing she’d used to wrap a sketchbook. It was too small for either of them. Theo examined it gravely.

“For a very small beach,” he said.

“Or one person who doesn’t mind being damp.”

“I’ll be the damp one.”

He sat on it, and she leaned against him. They ate a peach with their hands, juice running down their wrists. The peach was bruised on one side. It tasted better than the good ones.

“You ever think about staying here?” he asked.

“On this beach?”

“At the residency. Longer.”

“They don’t let you stay longer.”

“They could. They’ve got the attic.”

“They’ve got an attic full of dead mice.”

“Only one dead mouse.”

“Three.”

“Two and a half.”

She wiped her fingers on the grass. It was an ordinary question, maybe. A question about the residency. A question about the attic. Not about staying in any other way. Still, some small inward part of her had stood up, as if summoned, and now had nowhere to go.

“I think I’m ready to leave,” she said.

“Right.”

He bent to pick up a piece of shell. He kept it in his palm, turning it over. There. It was possible to put a thing in your pocket instead of asking it to become something.

After lunch, the tide had crept higher. They wandered along the waterline. Theo found a stranded jellyfish, transparent as a crumpled glove. Mara took a photograph. He said it looked like his first year’s portfolio. She said his first year’s portfolio had more ambition.

A gust of wind lifted her scarf and carried it toward the water. Theo went after it, slipping on the wet stones and catching himself with both hands. The scarf floated beyond him, then snagged on a piece of driftwood. He retrieved it triumphantly, soaked from the knees down.

“You’re a hero,” Mara said.

“I’d like that in writing.”

“You can put it in your statement.”

He wrung out the scarf. For a moment they were close enough for her to see a fleck of sand on his cheek. She could have brushed it away. She didn’t. He could have kissed her. He didn’t. The sea filled the pause with its indifferent shushing.

Then he said, “Your phone’s buzzing.”

The phone was in her bag, and her bag was on the jacket, and the jacket was on the rock. She ran back up the beach. It was her mother, asking which day she was flying. She called back and said Tuesday. Her mother asked if she was bringing the blue vase. Mara said she didn’t know. She said she would call later. By the time she turned around, Theo had found a path through the dunes and disappeared into it.

She followed, calling his name once. The path wound through beach grass and came out at a little wooden stairway. Below, the beach opened into a cove where a dozen people were gathered around a dog. Theo was standing among them, listening as a woman explained that the dog had swallowed a crab. The dog, a golden retriever with the solemn air of a priest, looked perfectly well.

Mara came down the steps.

“Should we call somebody?” Theo asked her.

“About the crab?”

“About the dog.”

The woman shook her head. “He does it every year.”

The dog shook himself, spraying them all with seawater. Everyone laughed. The woman apologized. Theo’s trousers were already wet, so it hardly mattered. Mara was laughing too, although she hadn’t meant to. The whole beach seemed to have conspired to make them ridiculous and harmless.

When the group dispersed, they walked back toward the car. A gull had found the bag of gummy worms and torn it open. Pink and green candies lay scattered in the sand. The gull stood over them, offended by their lack of nutritional value.

“You left the bag open,” Theo said.

“You bought them.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“Somehow it is.”

He tried to rescue the remaining worms. The gull flapped at him. Mara laughed until she had to sit down. Theo came back with three gummy worms, triumphant again, and offered one to her.

They ate them in the car, where the heater rattled and failed. The peaches had rolled under the passenger seat. Theo found one, inspected it, and gave it to Mara. On the drive back, the clouds gathered over the water. The rain began just as they reached the highway, sudden and hard, and the windshield wipers made a noise like a small animal protesting.

At the residency, the front door was locked. They had left their keys inside.

Theo looked at the windows. “Can you climb?”

“I can climb a ladder.”

“There’s no ladder.”

“Then no.”

He went around to the back of the building, where the kitchen window was propped open an inch. He tried to lift it. It stuck. He pushed harder, and the window popped up; a flowerpot tipped from the sill and fell, breaking in the weeds. The sound brought the caretaker out, in slippers, holding a mug.

“Not that window,” she said.

There was another key under the stone by the steps. Of course there was. The caretaker went back inside. Theo stood in the rain, looking at the broken pot.

“It was empty,” he said.

“It was a very meaningful pot.”

“I’ll pay for it.”

“You don’t have to.”

They looked at each other. The rain ran from his hair down his face. She wanted to say something sharp, something that would make the moment less tender. Instead, she said, “You could help me carry my suitcase down on Tuesday.”

“Sure.”

“You leave Monday.”

“Then I’ll do it Sunday.”

“Your flight’s early.”

“I’ll manage.”

There it was, a promise with an expiration date. Mara could have asked whether he’d come to Chicago. She could have asked if she could visit him in Berlin. She could have asked what he wanted, if he wanted anything at all beyond the room where they happened to be. But she had been practicing not asking, and practice, like any other discipline, made the thing possible.

So she said, “Okay.”

That night, the group gathered in the dining room for the last dinner before everyone began leaving. The residency director opened a bottle of wine and made a speech about risk, community, and the importance of finishing things. Someone had made lentil soup. Someone else had bought a cake with blue icing. Theo sat across from Mara, his knee touching hers beneath the table. When she looked up, he was listening to a sculptor describe a piece made from fishing line and old letters. He seemed completely present. It was one of the things she liked about him, and one of the things she distrusted.

After dinner, he walked her to her room. The hall smelled of dust and boiled cabbage. At her door, he kissed her once, with no urgency, and kept his hand at the back of her neck.

“I’ll see you in the morning,” he said.

“Probably.”

He smiled. “Probably.”

Inside, Mara took the damp scarf from her bag and hung it over the chair. The checked dish towel lay on the floor. She folded it and put it away. She thought of the sea, how it had kept coming in whether or not anyone was watching, and of the gummy worms scattered on the sand, bright little things the gulls did not want.

In the morning, Theo was in the kitchen making coffee. He poured her a cup. She drank from it while he stood at the counter, and he let her. A sensible arrangement, then. A habit, perhaps. The house was stirring around them, doors opening and closing, suitcases dragged down the stairs.

“Sunday,” he said.

“Sunday,” she answered.

Outside, a van waited by the entrance. One of the artists was leaving early. The driver leaned on the horn, though there was nowhere to go. Mara put the coffee cup in the sink and watched the van pull away, its windows clouded with breath.

Theo was beside her. Neither of them reached for the other. There was still time, which was the trouble. There was always enough time for one more hour and never enough for anything else.
