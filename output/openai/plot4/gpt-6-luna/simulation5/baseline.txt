### The Last Beach

On the morning of the beach trip, Mara found a crab in the residency kitchen.

It was small and blue-black, no bigger than a plum, and it had wedged itself beneath the industrial refrigerator. When she crouched to coax it out with a wooden spoon, it snapped at the spoon and retreated farther into the dark.

“Don’t antagonize it,” Elias said from the doorway.

“I’m not antagonizing it. I’m negotiating.”

“You’re holding a spoon.”

“It understands the universal language of leverage.”

Elias leaned against the doorframe, wearing the yellow raincoat he had bought from the charity shop down the road. The sleeves ended an inch above his wrists. He had a paper cup of coffee in one hand and, in the other, the bus schedule they had been given the night before.

“Bus leaves in twelve minutes,” he said.

Mara glanced at the crab. “Then it has twelve minutes to come to terms.”

The crab did not.

The residency occupied an old hotel on a headland, a white, peeling building where six artists had been invited to spend three weeks making work. The invitation had promised “space for exchange, solitude, and creative risk.” It had not mentioned the damp, the strange plumbing, or the crabs that climbed out of the drains during high tide.

Mara was a printmaker. Elias wrote plays, though he had spent most of the residency making small sculptures from driftwood and bits of wire. They had met on the second evening, over a bottle of wine neither had particularly liked, and had since developed the habit of finding one another wherever the house grew noisy.

They had not called it anything. It seemed to belong to the residency, like the shared groceries or the borrowed tools: available for a time, not to be taken home.

“Come on,” Elias said.

She left the crab in the kitchen.

They ran down the hill with the other residents, carrying towels, sandwiches, and the cheerful conviction that they had plenty of time. At the bus stop, the bus was already pulling away. Elias lifted his arms as though he might flag it down, then thought better of it.

“Next one?” Mara asked.

He checked the schedule. “Tomorrow.”

The others had made it aboard. Through the windows, their fellow residents waved with the cruel, affectionate delight of people who had secured seats.

There was a second route, the bus driver had told them the day before, that went to a village two miles inland. From there, they could walk to a beach. Elias found the route on his phone, but the phone had no signal. Mara found a paper map in her bag, folded into a shape that made no sense.

“Are we still going?” she asked.

“Obviously.”

“Good. I was afraid of what the answer might reveal.”

They set off down the road.

The morning was bright but windy, the clouds moving quickly over the fields. Beyond the hedgerows, the sea showed itself in pieces: a dull silver strip, then nothing, then a sudden flash between two hills. Elias walked ahead, stopping at every fork to argue with the map. Mara followed, enjoying the temporary permission not to speak.

After an hour they reached the inland village, which consisted of a church, a pub not yet open, and a shop selling postcards of beaches they had not visited. They bought crisps, two bruised apples, and a small jar of pickled onions. The shopkeeper told them the coastal path was closed.

“Closed how?” Elias asked.

“Closed as in don’t go that way,” she said, with the calm of someone who had been asked this question many times.

They went that way.

The path was blocked by a length of orange fencing, but the fence had collapsed in the middle. Beyond it, the trail ran along the cliff, narrow and muddy. They climbed over, then immediately regretted the decision. A gust of wind snatched the map from Mara’s hand. Elias caught it against his chest, where it stuck to his wet raincoat.

“Excellent,” Mara said. “Now it’s a garment.”

The path dipped through a stand of pines and came out at the edge of the beach. It was smaller than the one in the photographs, a crescent of dark sand between two jutting cliffs. The tide was low, leaving a broad stretch of wet sand, tide pools, and wrack-line debris: rope, kelp, one orange bucket with no handle.

No other people were there.

They stood for a moment, both breathing hard. Behind them, the clouds were gathering.

“This is better,” Elias said.

“Than the beach we missed?”

“Than the one we were supposed to go to.”

He said it lightly, but Mara heard something else beneath it. Lately, she had been hearing things beneath everything. The next morning, Elias was leaving for London. She was taking a train north the day after. At the residency’s closing dinner, the director had made a toast to “all the beginnings that take place here.” Mara had wondered how many of them were only endings wearing nicer clothes.

They spread their towels above the tide line. Elias took off his shoes and rolled his trousers to the knee. Mara did the same, then stepped into the sea and shouted at the cold.

“Bracing,” he said.

“Cowardly.”

He came in beside her. For a few seconds they stood ankle-deep, the water tugging at their feet. Then a wave broke against their shins, and they ran back up the beach, laughing.

They ate the sandwiches they had brought. Elias’s had been crushed flat in his bag. Mara’s had attracted a gull, which swooped down and took half before she could stop it. The bird landed nearby and stared at them with the chilly attention of an unpaid judge.

“I feel like we’re being evaluated,” Elias said.

“Maybe it’s the residency director.”

The gull seized the other half of her sandwich.

“Apparently I’m not doing well.”

Elias gave her his apple. It was soft on one side. She ate the firm half and handed it back without comment. He took a bite from the bruised end.

The clouds lowered. The light changed, turning the sea from silver to slate. Elias pulled the hood of his raincoat over his head.

“Do you ever wonder,” he said, “how long you have to know someone before you’re allowed to ask them what happens next?”

Mara picked at the edge of her towel. “That depends on whether you mean next week or next year.”

“I was thinking next week.”

She looked toward the water. “Next week you’ll be in London.”

“Yes.”

“And I’ll be in Northumberland.”

“Yes.”

“So probably not long.”

He watched her, waiting. She wished he would ask plainly. She wished he wouldn’t.

A gust of wind lifted the corner of her towel and sent it tumbling down the beach. Elias ran after it. He caught it just before it hit the water, but the towel wrapped around his legs, and he fell hard onto the wet sand.

Mara laughed so much she had to sit down. He stayed on his back, one arm raised, the towel draped over him like a defeated flag.

“I’m glad we’re having this conversation,” he said.

“I’m trying to maintain the right tone.”

He got up, brushing sand from his coat. A dark wet patch spread across his shoulder. Mara reached out as if to touch it, then folded the corner of her own towel instead.

The rain began with a few scattered drops. They gathered their things and went looking for shelter among the cliffs, where a shallow hollow offered some protection. Elias found a narrow cave at the base of one rock and ducked inside. It smelled of salt and old seaweed. Mara squeezed in after him.

They sat close because there wasn’t room not to. Their shoulders touched. Outside, rain stippled the beach.

“I’ll miss this place,” Elias said.

“The kitchen crabs?”

“Some of them.”

“Not me, though.”

“I didn’t say that.”

The silence that followed was not empty. It had too many possible meanings crowded into it. Mara became aware of the warmth of his arm against hers, and the small, damp space between their knees.

She could have leaned toward him. She could have asked him to come north. She could have said that she had been imagining the mornings after the residency, how strange it would be to wake without hearing him in the corridor, humming tunelessly while he searched for coffee.

Instead she said, “The roof in the studio leaks over my desk.”

“I know.”

“And the tea tastes like hot paper.”

“I know that too.”

The rain passed as quickly as it had arrived. They emerged from the cave, blinking into the pale light. The tide had turned. The sea crept higher across the sand, swallowing the shallow pools.

They walked back along the cliff path, more slowly this time. At the fence, Elias climbed over first and held out his hand. Mara took it. He pulled her up, then didn’t let go at once. His thumb rested against the inside of her wrist, where her pulse beat visibly.

“Come to London,” he said.

The words startled her with their plainness.

“For what?”

He looked down at their hands. “For a weekend. For a week. I don’t know.”

Mara felt the offer opening in front of her, small and real and already shadowed by distance. She had the impulse to say yes at once, before either of them could make it difficult. She had the equal impulse to ask whether he wanted her there, or only wanted to keep the residency from ending.

“I could,” she said.

He nodded. “You could.”

Neither of them moved.

Then, from somewhere behind them, came the thin, furious bleat of a sheep. It had its head through the fence and was chewing the corner of the map, which Elias had tucked under his arm.

“Your garment,” Mara said.

They rescued what remained of the map. By the time they reached the road, the sky had cleared. They walked back toward the residency without hurrying, their shoes muddy, their towels damp, the jar of pickled onions leaking into Elias’s bag.

At the house, the crab had vanished from the kitchen. The final dinner began at seven. Someone had made a cake that collapsed in the middle. The director gave another toast. The artists promised to visit one another, and exchanged email addresses they already had.

After dinner, Elias found Mara on the back steps, where she was watching the dark line of the sea.

“I meant it,” he said.

“I know.”

“About London.”

“I know.”

He sat beside her. Their knees touched. In the window behind them, the others were moving around the common room, bright and blurred by glass.

For a while they listened to the wind.

Then Elias kissed her. It was not quite a first kiss; they had kissed before, in hallways and once in the print studio while the drying rack turned between them. But this one felt different because there was no excuse for it. No music, no wine, no late hour to blame.

When he pulled away, Mara kept her eyes closed for a moment.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow you leave.”

“Right.”

She opened her eyes. “I could come to London.”

He smiled, but not as if he had won anything. “You could.”

They did not make a plan. That night, she lay awake in her narrow bed and listened to the building settle. In the morning, she helped carry his bag down the stairs. He stood at the door in the yellow raincoat, its sleeves still too short.

“Write when you get there,” she said.

“I will.”

She wanted to say something that would make the promise larger. Instead she kissed him once, on the cheek, where the skin was cold from the walk downstairs.

Then he was gone, crossing the lawn toward the road with his bag bumping against his knee. Mara watched until the gate swung shut behind him.

In the kitchen, beneath the refrigerator, something scraped softly against the floor. She looked down. The crab emerged from the dark, paused in the middle of the tiles, and lifted one small claw.

Mara crouched.

“All right,” she said. “You first.”
