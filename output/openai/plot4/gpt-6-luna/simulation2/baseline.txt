### The Last Beach

By the time they found the beach, the day had already gone wrong in three separate ways.

First, the residency van had overheated on the road out of town, coughing steam beneath a row of eucalyptus trees. Then Eli had mistaken a private lane for a public access road and had to reverse nearly half a mile while Mara stood outside, waving him away from a stone wall. Finally, the paper bag of peaches had split open in the back seat, leaving one peach rolling under the pedals and another mashed into the floor mat.

“Good omen,” Eli said, when they were walking the last stretch with their bags.

“For what?”

“An auspicious day.”

“You’re confusing auspicious with sticky.”

The path opened between two dunes. Beyond them, the sea lay flat and silver, as if someone had ironed it. They had the beach to themselves except for a man in a red cap far down the shore, walking a small dog that seemed to be dragging him toward the water.

Mara set down her bag and watched the waves. “We made it.”

“We did.”

Neither of them said that it was their last beach trip. They’d been saying it all week in other ways: the last dinner at the residency, the last reading in the barn, the last night when the kitchen still belonged to everyone. The residency house, a former convent on a hill above town, was due to empty by Monday. People had started rolling their suitcases into the hall. The walls were coming back into view, pale and unadorned, as the work disappeared into boxes.

Mara had been at the residency for six weeks. Eli had been there for seven, having arrived early to help with the sound installation that filled the old chapel with recordings of the surrounding woods: wind through needles, a crow calling, someone closing a gate. They had spent their first month behaving as though they had only just met. Then, one rainy night, they’d stayed late in the kitchen, drinking the bad red wine left over from a visiting curator’s dinner. After that, they stopped pretending not to notice when the other one entered a room.

They had been careful with each other. Careful in the way people are when they know there is a calendar on the wall.

Eli spread a towel on the sand. It was one of the residency’s thick blue bath towels, and he had written his name on the tag in marker. Mara placed her jacket beside it.

“You brought no towel?” he asked.

“I brought a sweater.”

“You thought the beach would be cold?”

“I thought you’d bring a towel.”

He regarded her with mock disappointment. “You placed a lot of faith in me.”

“I’m revising my expectations.”

They ate the surviving peaches, which were warm and bruised. Eli had brought bread, olives, and a jar of mustard from the residency kitchen. Mara produced two boiled eggs in a napkin. The dog walker disappeared behind a low headland. The tide was going out, leaving long bands of wet sand.

“Your train’s at six tomorrow?” Eli asked.

“Five-forty. I have to change in Lyon.”

“And then?”

“Montreal. My sister’s coming to the airport. She’ll ask if I’ve met anyone.”

“Will you tell her?”

“I’ll say I met a lot of people.”

“Technically true.”

“Technically is where I live.”

He smiled, but it was a small smile, gone quickly. He was flying to Berlin the next afternoon. A sound company there had offered him a six-month contract. He’d accepted it in the second week of the residency, though he hadn’t told Mara until much later, when she’d asked why he was always checking his phone.

She had no reason to be angry. They had never made promises. Still, it had been strange to learn that the future was already in motion, passing quietly between them.

They swam before the water could seem too cold to enter. Mara went first, running in until the waves reached her waist, then ducking under with a sharp cry. Eli followed, swearing at the cold. They stayed close to shore, where the water was choppy enough to make conversation impossible. When they came back, Mara’s hair was plastered to her cheeks, and Eli had sand stuck to one side of his face.

“You look like a dog that’s been buried,” she told him.

He tried to brush it away and made things worse.

She reached up and wiped his cheek with her thumb. It was a familiar gesture, but it became strange as soon as she did it. Her hand lingered. He didn’t move.

Then a wave ran up the beach and soaked their clothes in a wide, cold sheet.

They jumped back, laughing, and for a moment the awkwardness broke apart. Eli chased the retreating water, his jeans dark to the knees. Mara’s laugh caught in her throat when he turned toward her, grinning, and the look between them opened like a door.

She shut it by picking up the mustard jar.

“Let’s find the tide pools,” she said.

The tide pools were farther along the coast than they looked. They followed the curve of the shore, crossing shelves of rock slick with seaweed. Eli slipped once and caught himself on his hands. Mara laughed until she slipped too, landing on her hip with an undignified yelp.

“Now it’s auspicious,” Eli said, helping her up.

She had scraped her palm. He held her hand to inspect it. There was a shallow red line across the heel, already beading with seawater.

“It’s nothing.”

“It’s bleeding.”

“It’s a scratch.”

He kept hold of her hand a moment longer than the injury required. She let him. The wind lifted his hair and pressed his shirt against his chest. He had a small crescent scar at the base of his thumb, from a glass he’d broken at the residency. Mara remembered his telling her about it in the kitchen, holding up his hand as if it belonged to someone else.

At the pools, they found a crab no bigger than a coin, two anemones folded into themselves, and a plastic comb wedged between the rocks. Eli tried to record the water with his phone. The wind kept swallowing the sound.

“Maybe the sea doesn’t want to be in your project,” Mara said.

“Maybe it wants a better microphone.”

“Maybe it wants to be left alone.”

He lowered the phone. “You’re always defending things from being made into art.”

“Only when they’re doing fine without it.”

“Are you doing fine without it?”

She looked at him. The question was too close to another one, or perhaps to several. Behind him, a thin cloud was spreading across the sky.

“I’m going to miss the house,” she said.

He nodded. “Me too.”

“I don’t mean the house.”

The wind moved between them. The crab had vanished beneath the rock.

Eli looked down at his wet shoes. “I know.”

Neither of them said more. They walked back along the beach as the cloud thickened, and the first rain began in fine, cold drops. They hurried toward the dunes, carrying their bags and the damp towel. Halfway across the sand, Mara realized she’d left her jacket by the water.

“I’ll get it,” Eli said.

“You’ll get soaked.”

“I’m already soaked.”

He ran back. Mara watched him bend to pick up the jacket, then wave it above his head as he sprinted toward her. At the dune path, he skidded on loose sand and went down on one knee. The jacket flew from his hand.

Mara was laughing again, and that made him laugh too. By the time they reached the road, the rain was coming hard enough to blur the trees.

The van wouldn’t start.

Eli turned the key. The engine clicked, made a soft whine, and went silent.

“Didn’t you say it was only the battery?” Mara asked.

“I said it might be the battery.”

“You said you knew how to fix it.”

“I said I knew how to call someone who knows how to fix it.”

“That is a very different skill.”

He leaned his forehead against the steering wheel. She rested her palm on the wet dashboard. For a few seconds they sat without speaking, listening to the rain drum on the roof. The air smelled of salt and warm engine.

At last Eli said, “We could walk to the village.”

“In this?”

“I’ve walked in worse.”

“When?”

He shrugged. “I don’t remember. I’ve forgotten most things that were worse.”

She looked at his profile, the rain silvering the window beside him. She wanted to ask whether he’d ever wondered what would have happened if they’d met somewhere without an end date. She wanted to ask whether he had been choosing Berlin before or after he began sleeping in her room. Instead, she said, “The peaches are all ruined.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You didn’t make them.”

“I put them in the bag.”

“Eli.”

“I’m sorry.”

She reached over and squeezed his shoulder. He turned his face toward her, and for a moment she thought he might kiss her. Instead, he placed his hand over hers, and they stayed like that until the rain eased.

A mechanic from the village arrived an hour later. He had a cigarette tucked behind one ear and a boy in the passenger seat who stared at them as though they were a puzzle. The problem was the battery after all. The mechanic charged it enough to get them back to the residency, where they parked beneath the kitchen window and carried their wet things upstairs.

That night, the house was noisy with packing. Someone had found an old bottle of liqueur and was passing it around the sitting room. A painter from Oslo was trying to fit a framed canvas into a suitcase. In the kitchen, the residency director labeled the last jars of jam with masking tape.

Mara stood in the doorway to the chapel. Eli’s installation had been switched off. In the dark, she could hear only the house: the pipes ticking, someone laughing upstairs, the soft scrape of chairs across the floor.

Eli came to stand beside her.

“I never heard the gate,” she said.

“What?”

“In your piece. You said there was a gate.”

“There is.”

“I heard the crow. I heard the wind. I don’t remember a gate.”

“It was very quiet.”

She looked at him. “I might have liked it.”

“I could have turned it up.”

“No. I mean I might have liked not hearing it.”

He nodded, as though he understood, or wanted her to think he did. Behind them, the house continued its last-night noises.

When they said goodnight, they stood in the corridor with the uncertain formality of people deciding what a goodbye should look like. They had kissed in that corridor before. Once, at the beginning, and once after she’d learned about Berlin. The second kiss had been longer, but afterward neither had mentioned it. Now Eli brushed a fleck of lint from her sleeve.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow.”

He went up the stairs. She watched until he turned the corner.

In the morning, he was already gone when Mara came down. He’d left the blue towel folded on the kitchen table, his name still written on the tag. On top of it lay a small shell, striped white and brown. She picked it up and turned it over in her hand.

Outside, a car honked. Her sister would be waiting at the station in Montreal, with questions. The residency director had put a box of leftover peaches by the door, bruised but still sweet. Mara took one.

For a moment, she considered going upstairs to look for Eli. Then she slipped the shell into her pocket and carried her bag outside.

The morning was clear. At the end of the hill, the sea showed between the buildings, bright and distant. She could still hear, if she tried, the waves folding against the beach—and the small silence between them.
