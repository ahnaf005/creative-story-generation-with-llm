### The Last Beach

By the time the residency minibus left them at the station, the sky had turned the color of a spoon. Mara had packed for sunshine, though she had been living with the sea for six weeks and should have known better. The sea did not care what anyone packed.

“Last beach trip,” said Julian, lifting the paper bag of peaches like a flag.

“Last everything,” Mara said.

He smiled as if she’d made a joke. He was good at that: giving her words a lighter meaning than the one she’d intended. It had got them through the residency. It had also made a mess of the nights.

The minibus coughed away, leaving them beside the road with their tote bags, a beach umbrella, and Julian’s borrowed bicycle. He had insisted the ride would be easy. It was downhill, he’d said, and almost all the way to the water. Mara looked at the bicycle’s loose chain.

“Almost,” she said.

“An important distinction.”

They set off, Julian pedaling, Mara walking beside him. The road wound through the village and past the shuttered bakery, where a paper sign advertised apricot tarts in a hand that seemed to have given up halfway through the word. Beyond the last house, the path began to climb.

Julian’s chain slipped before they reached the hill. He put one foot down, then the other, and wobbled into the hedge.

“Elegant,” Mara said.

“I’m conserving energy.”

“For the beach?”

“For the romance of it.”

She laughed. It came out too easily. That was the trouble with a final day: everything had an echo. Even laughter sounded like something being remembered in advance.

They left the bicycle at a stone wall and walked the rest of the way. The umbrella, which Julian carried, kept turning inside out in the wind. They passed vineyards, then a stand of pines. The sea appeared suddenly between the trees, flat and bright, as though it had been waiting there all along.

The beach was nearly empty. A child in a red hat was digging an ambitious hole near the waterline. Farther down, two old men sat beneath a striped awning and watched the horizon with the solemnity of judges. Mara and Julian found a patch of sand above the wrack line and spread their towels.

“Here?” Julian asked.

“Unless you’ve found somewhere better.”

He glanced toward the water. “I was thinking we might walk.”

They had walked every day of the residency. Around the village, along the harbor, through the market, to the ruined chapel where the artists had taken photographs and pretended not to be taking photographs. Walking was what they did when they didn’t know what to say. Mara was a little tired of the landscape receiving their conversations on their behalf.

“After lunch,” she said.

Julian sat down. He had brought bread, cheese, olives, peaches. His lunch was always more optimistic than hers. Mara had packed two boiled eggs and a packet of salted crackers, as if the beach were a place where provisions needed to survive.

They ate in the wind. The bread kept trying to leave. Julian caught it with his elbow.

“Your studio visit went well,” he said.

“Did it?”

“You know it did.”

The visiting curator had spent twenty minutes with Mara’s work and called it “an inquiry into absence.” Mara had thanked him, then gone outside and stood behind the laundry shed until the feeling passed. “Absence” was the sort of word people used when they wanted you to be grateful for being misunderstood.

“I don’t know what I’m making,” she said.

“Most people don’t.”

“Most people say they do.”

“Most people are lying.”

He said it kindly, and she believed him. Julian was kind in ways that had no cost to him. He could ask after her work, remember her sister’s name, save the last plum. The more difficult things he could not seem to do. Such as tell her whether, in the city, they would meet again. Such as ask whether she wanted to.

Mara brushed crumbs from her shorts. The child’s hole had reached the size of a small grave.

“What are you doing in August?” she asked.

“Going to Berlin.”

She waited.

“Then my brother’s in Lisbon,” he added. “A week or so.”

“After that?”

He picked up an olive and turned it between finger and thumb. “I have to see.”

Of course. The sacred freedom of not knowing. Mara had spent the residency trying to make a sculpture from a broken door and a coil of copper wire. Julian had spent it being invited to dinner by everyone who passed through the kitchen.

“Lucky you,” she said.

He looked up. “You’re going back to Manchester?”

“On Monday.”

“And then?”

“Teaching. My mother’s birthday. Probably the usual collapse of plans.”

“Could come see you.”

Could. A word with a door in it, and no promise of a handle.

The wind lifted the corner of her towel. Julian pinned it down with his sandal. “You want to swim?”

“Not particularly.”

“Then we should.”

He was already standing, pulling his shirt over his head. She watched him run toward the water, all lean limbs and careless confidence. At the edge he stopped, because the water was colder than he expected. The child laughed. Julian looked back at Mara.

“Coward!” he called.

“Correct.”

He went in anyway. The first wave knocked him sideways. For a moment he disappeared, then surfaced sputtering, triumphant, with his hair plastered to his forehead. Mara laughed again, and this time it was not an echo. She kicked off her sandals and went in.

The water seized her ankles. It was shockingly cold, then only cold, then almost pleasant. Julian swam out a few strokes and turned onto his back. His face tipped toward the sky. He looked completely at ease, which was irritating, since no one was ever that at ease. Not even him, probably.

A wave broke against Mara’s ribs and she gasped. Julian splashed her. She returned the favor. For several minutes they were only bodies in water, shouting, laughing, trying not to lose their footing. Then a bigger wave came up behind them. Mara ducked; Julian didn’t. It rolled him forward and stole his sunglasses.

They searched for them in the shallows, sweeping the sand with their feet.

“I can’t see anything,” Julian said.

“That’s because you’re looking for sunglasses.”

“I’m looking with my eyes.”

“Try using your feet.”

They found nothing. Julian’s glasses were expensive and, he said, prescription. Mara felt bad until he grinned and admitted they weren’t. He’d bought them at a market in Marseille for five euros.

“Then you’ve lost five euros.”

“And the mysterious look they gave me.”

Back on the towels, the wind had scattered their lunch. One peach rolled downhill and came to rest in the child’s hole. The child had gone; the old men were still watching the water, as if none of this concerned them.

Julian retrieved the peach. It had a small bruise but was otherwise intact. He wiped it on his shirt and handed it to Mara.

“An offering,” he said.

“To the beach?”

“To whoever’s willing to eat it.”

She took it. The skin was warm from the sun. She bit into it, and juice ran onto her wrist.

They stayed until the clouds thickened. Julian lay on his back with one arm over his eyes. Mara sat with her knees drawn up, watching the water darken. The beach was empty now except for the child, who had returned with a bucket and was rebuilding the hole. It would be filled by the tide before evening. The child knew this, presumably. There was no reason not to build it.

“Do you ever think,” Julian said, “that we make things too important by naming them?”

Mara looked at him. “Are you asking me a question?”

“I’m asking the beach.”

“Then the beach says yes.”

He lowered his arm. “You always know what to say.”

She almost told him that she didn’t. She almost said: I know what to say when the answer doesn’t matter. Instead she shook sand from the hem of her shorts.

He had kissed her on the third night, outside the print studio, while a storm rolled over the hills. Afterward he had touched his forehead to hers and said, “Well, that was overdue.” The next morning he brought her coffee. Nobody asked what it meant. They began sharing a bed, then stopped sharing it when the other residents came back from a late dinner. They kissed in doorways. They avoided the kitchen at the same time. By the end, there was a kind of tenderness between them that looked, from a distance, like an arrangement.

It was not nothing. It was not enough to carry home. These were facts Mara had been carefully not arranging beside each other.

Julian sat up. “We should head back before dark.”

The bicycle had been exactly where they left it, though the chain had fallen completely off. They pushed it along the road. At the top of the hill they found a small café with its shutters half closed. Julian went in to ask for a tool. The woman behind the counter gave him a wrench, then pointed at the bicycle and said something sharp in the local language. He nodded as though he understood.

“What did she say?” Mara asked when he came out.

“She said we’re idiots.”

“Fair.”

They got the chain back on, but it left a black stripe across Julian’s palm and another across Mara’s pale skirt. She tried to rub it out with a napkin. The mark only spread.

“Now it’s art,” Julian said.

“Don’t.”

“An inquiry into absence.”

She stared at him. He raised both hands, the wrench between them. “Sorry.”

The road into town was mostly downhill after that, as promised. Julian rode, carefully, and Mara walked beside him. At the bottom, near the station, the bicycle chain slipped again. This time he didn’t bother fixing it.

“I’ll return it in the morning,” he said.

“You won’t be here in the morning.”

“Right.”

He looked at her then, perhaps waiting for a reply, perhaps only checking the traffic. A car passed, its tires whispering on the road. The station was just ahead. The minibus would take them back to the residency house, where everyone would be packing and saying how strange it was that the month had gone so fast.

At the crossing, Julian’s hand brushed hers. Neither of them moved away. A beat passed, and then another. It was nothing, and it was not nothing, and there was no use asking the sea to settle it.

“Monday,” he said. “Manchester.”

“Yes.”

“I’ll write.”

There it was: the modest promise, dressed for travel.

“All right,” Mara said.

They crossed the road. He pushed the broken bicycle beside her. The station smelled of diesel and warm stone. Behind them, the sea had gone out of sight, but the wind followed, carrying the salt into town. Mara could still taste the peach on her tongue. She could still feel the cold water closing over her ribs.

At the platform, Julian leaned toward her, then hesitated. A goodbye kiss would be too much, perhaps; a farewell without one, too little. How exhausting, this measuring of a thing they had never named.

Mara took his face in both hands and kissed him. His mouth was cold from the water, then warm. It lasted only a moment. Long enough to make the truth plain, though not the future.

When she drew back, he looked surprised. Good. So did she.

“Don’t make it important,” he said softly.

She wanted to tell him that importance wasn’t something people could simply decline. That some things mattered even if they ended at a station, even if they were built to last only six weeks, even if one person was already looking elsewhere.

Instead she said, “I won’t.”

The minibus arrived. Julian lifted the bicycle onto its rack, and the driver complained at him in a language neither of them spoke. Mara climbed inside. Through the glass, Julian raised his blackened hand in farewell.

She raised hers. The stain on her skirt remained.

All the way back, she watched the road unspool toward the residency house, the orchards, the chapel, the place where the sea disappeared behind the hill. Monday was waiting, with its ordinary demands. There would be no grand lesson, no clear shape to carry away. Just a bruise on a peach, lost sunglasses, the salt drying on her skin.

That was enough to remember. Not enough to keep.
