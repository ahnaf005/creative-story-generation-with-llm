On the last Saturday of the residency, the bus to the beach left at nine, which meant everyone was late by nine-twenty, standing in the gravel courtyard with towels under their arms and sunglasses on their heads, pretending not to mind the sun. The house, pale and municipal behind them, had already begun to look like a place they had stayed in once. Windows open, sheets hauled off beds and heaped in the laundry room, the kitchen smelling faintly of burned coffee and bleach. Departure made everything uglier and more precious.

Mara had packed too much: book, notebook, sweatshirt, two peaches, sunscreen, a scarf in case of wind. It was only a day trip, absurd. But then wasn’t everything now absurdly overprepared and underfelt? She was leaving Monday. In forty-eight hours she would be on a train, watching the hills reverse themselves, her month at the residency shrinking into anecdote. A productive month, she would say, though she had written almost nothing. A restorative month, she would say, though she had slept poorly and developed a habit of listening for Leo’s footsteps in the hallway after midnight.

Leo arrived last, carrying nothing but a towel and a paper bag. Of course. He wore the same faded blue shirt he had worn the first night, when he’d stood in the doorway of the dining room, newly arrived, smiling as if someone had just told him he was forgiven. Mara looked away too quickly and busied herself with the buckle of her sandal.

“Any contraband?” Nina called from the bus door.

Leo lifted the bag. “Apricots. Three stolen rolls. A knife I found in the pantry.”

“Perfect,” said Nina. “We’re all going to prison by the sea.”

They climbed in, twelve of them total, though by the end of the week it had felt like fewer. People had become versions of themselves reduced by repetition: Tomas with his headphones, Priya taking photographs of doorways, André complaining amiably about the coffee, Nina organizing everyone with the despairing competence of someone who had not asked to be loved. Mara took a window seat halfway back. There was a pause—too brief to count as decision, long enough to haunt her—before Leo dropped into the seat beside her.

“Is this taken?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said. “By my composure.”

He laughed. That low startled laugh of his, as if amusement had caught him from behind. It was unbearable, how much she had come to wait for it.

The bus lurched out through the residency gates, past the cypress trees and the cracked stone wall where lizards flattened themselves into warmth. The driver had hung a rosary from the mirror; it swung madly at every turn. Someone up front began distributing packets of crackers. Someone else opened a beer, though it was not yet ten. The road to the coast wound through small towns with shuttered pharmacies and dogs asleep in strips of shade.

Mara watched the landscape with exaggerated attention. The yellow fields, the silver-green olive groves, the distant seam of water appearing and disappearing between hills. If she looked at Leo, she would have to manage her face.

He had been at the residency for three weeks when she arrived, already established in its private weather. The others spoke of him casually—Leo knows where the laundry tokens are, Leo found the path to the tower, ask Leo, he understands the stove—as if he belonged to the building. He was a composer, or not exactly a composer: he made sound installations out of recorded rooms, mechanical hums, people breathing without knowing they were being recorded. This should have irritated her. Instead she had asked too many questions at dinner and later found herself standing with him by the vending machine at one in the morning while he explained how silence was almost never empty.

“Listen,” he had said.

So she had listened: refrigerator buzz, old pipes, a faraway car, their own breathing arranged awkwardly between them.

Now, on the bus, his knee knocked hers when the driver took a curve too fast. He did not move it away at once. Neither did she. How adolescent, how beneath her, how fully alive she felt.

“You packed for evacuation,” he said, glancing down at her bag.

“I believe in contingencies.”

“You believe in carrying the entire interior life of a small nation.”

“It’s called preparedness.”

“It’s called fear.”

She turned to him. “And your paper bag is what? Courage?”

“Improvisation.”

“Male privilege.”

“Also that.”

It was easy, this kind of talk. They had perfected it quickly, over communal dinners and late walks around the grounds, each joke a plank laid over something deep and fast-moving. She had told him about her novel, the one she was supposed to be revising, though she spoke of it with the embarrassed resentment of someone describing a relative who had behaved badly. He had told her about Berlin, where he lived in a room above a bakery and never felt clean. They had not told each other certain things. His girlfriend, for example, existed in a blurred tense. Mara had a former husband who lived in Toronto and sent her articles about climate anxiety as if continuing a marriage by link. These facts floated around them, acknowledged and then politely ignored.

Halfway to the coast, the bus broke down.

It did not break down dramatically. There was no smoke, no gasp, no heroic swerve to the shoulder. It simply sighed and stopped beside a field of sunflowers whose heads had all turned away, as if disappointed. The driver stepped out, opened the front panel, stared into it with priestly concern.

Everyone spilled into the road. The heat rose in visible sheets. Tomas immediately put on his headphones. Priya began taking pictures of the broken bus, the rosary, Leo holding his towel like a flag of surrender.

“Do you think we’ll still make it?” Mara asked.

“To the beach or in life?” Leo said.

“Either.”

“No.”

She smiled despite herself.

They waited forty minutes. The driver made several phone calls involving broad gestures and the repetition of “impossibile,” which required no translation. Nina took charge of shade allocation. André produced cigarettes. The residency’s director, who had insisted this trip would be simple, sat on a rock and looked personally betrayed by transportation.

Mara and Leo walked a little way down the road, not far enough to be accused of disappearing. The field beside them was fenced with rusted wire. Beyond it, the land dipped toward a dry creek bed crowded with reeds.

“I wanted one last swim,” he said.

“One last?”

“I leave tomorrow.”

She knew this. Everyone knew this. His flight was Sunday morning, obscenely early. Still, hearing him say it was like seeing a door she had been leaning against swing open.

“Right,” she said. “Berlin.”

“Paris first. Two days. Then Berlin.”

“Ah. Glamour.”

“My layover is ten hours and I will sleep on a floor.”

“European glamour.”

He smiled, then looked at the road. There were little white scars on his forearm she had noticed before and invented several explanations for: childhood accident, cat, art-related mishap, a past more interesting than hers. She wanted to touch them. She wanted not to want anything so obvious.

“And you?” he asked.

“Train Monday. Then home.”

“Home,” he repeated, as if it were an experimental concept.

“Yes, people have them.”

“Do you?”

A rude question, perhaps. Or only intimate. She could have deflected it, should have. Instead she watched a grasshopper spring from the dust.

“I have an apartment,” she said. “I have books. A kettle. A plant that may or may not have survived my absence.”

“A life.”

“Allegedly.”

He nodded. Something had shifted, or she imagined it had. All month she had been conscious of his attention as one is conscious of weather: changing, ambient, not to be confronted unless extreme. There had been the night of the storm when the power went out and they drank wine in the corridor with a flashlight standing upright between them. There had been the morning he brought her coffee in the studio because he had made too much, though the cup was exactly how she took it. There had been yesterday in the archive room, reaching simultaneously for the same box of old photographs, their hands touching with the comic precision of a bad film. They had both apologized. It had been devastating.

He might say something now. She might. The field hummed. The bus sat behind them full of stranded artists, all of them pretending not to observe the small gravitational systems forming and failing around them. Mara felt the sentence in her mouth before she knew its shape. Maybe when we get back— No. Maybe before you leave— Worse. I’ll miss— Impossible.

A horn sounded. Another bus had arrived, smaller and green, with curtains printed in a pattern of lemons. Everyone cheered too loudly. The moment folded itself and went into the place where such moments went: the private museum of almosts, poorly lit and overcrowded.

The second bus got them to the beach after noon. By then the day had hardened into brightness. The sea lay below the parking lot, intensely blue and indifferent, fringed by a strip of pale sand crowded with umbrellas. Children shrieked in several languages. A vendor dragged a cooler through the dunes, calling something melodic and incomprehensible. The group descended in a disorderly procession, towels flapping, sandals filling with sand.

Misadventure continued at once. Nina discovered she had left the lunch bag on the first bus. The director lost his glasses, then found them in his hat. André was stung by a jellyfish within five minutes of entering the water and emerged cursing the Mediterranean as a fraudulent sea. Tomas, trying to help, offered to urinate on him and was told with unusual clarity that this would not be necessary.

Mara spread her towel near a cluster of rocks. Leo dropped beside her, close but not too close. There it was again, their whole arrangement expressed in inches.

“Contingencies?” he asked.

She opened her bag with dignity and produced the peaches, sunscreen, a slightly flattened packet of almonds, and the scarf.

“You see?” she said. “Civilization.”

He bowed his head. “I apologize for mocking your nation.”

They ate the peaches over the paper bag, juice running down their wrists. The fruit was warm from her bag, almost indecently soft. Leo licked his thumb. Mara looked at the sea.

Around them, the others entered the loose democracy of a beach day. Priya wandered off with her camera. Nina negotiated lunch from a kiosk with the ferocity of a diplomat. The director fell asleep sitting upright. The residency, with its chore charts and studio assignments and grant-funded seriousness, dissolved into bodies: freckled shoulders, knees, stomachs, vulnerable backs.

Mara went into the water first. It was colder than she expected; she gasped, hated herself for gasping, then dove. Underwater, the noise vanished. Her hair lifted. For three seconds she was no one with no deadlines, no unfinished manuscript, no man on the shore who would leave tomorrow and become a story she told badly or never. She surfaced and wiped salt from her eyes.

Leo was wading in, making a pained face.

“It’s freezing,” he said.

“It’s character-building.”

“I have enough character.”

“No one thinks that.”

He splashed her. She splashed back. It became ridiculous at once, childish and exhilarating, both of them laughing, retreating, attacking with cupped hands. A boy nearby joined in, assuming they were safe adults, which was flattering and damning. Leo surrendered theatrically, arms raised. His shirt clung to him. Mara was old enough to know desire often looked stupid from the outside and still it moved through her with solemn force.

They swam out to the buoy line. Not far, but farther than the others. The beach became a band of color and motion. Leo floated on his back, eyes closed.

“I had a dream about this place,” he said.

“The beach?”

“The residency. Before I came. I dreamed there was a house where every room contained the sound of someone leaving.”

“That’s very on brand for you.”

“I know. Embarrassing.”

“What did it sound like?”

He opened one eye. “Like someone trying not to wake anyone.”

She treaded water. Her legs were getting tired. Why did everything he said arrive already furnished with meaning? It wasn’t fair. Perhaps he was not deep at all; perhaps she had made him so, out of boredom and salt and the cruel compression of time. A month at a residency was a factory for significance. Everyone became luminous because no one had to buy toilet paper except communally.

“I don’t like leaving quietly,” she said.

“No?”

“No. I want credit.”

“For leaving?”

“For having been there.”

He looked at her then. Really looked, or so she decided, because she needed it. A swell lifted them both and set them down.

“You were there,” he said.

It should not have been enough. It was not enough. It entered her anyway.

On the way back to shore, Mara’s foot cramped. A sharp little betrayal in her arch. She flailed, cursed, swallowed seawater.

“Are you okay?” Leo asked, suddenly near.

“Fine,” she sputtered, not fine, furious at her body for staging a metaphor.

“Hold on.”

“I said I’m fine.”

“Of course. You’re drowning with great competence.”

He put a hand under her elbow—not rescuing, exactly, but insisting. She let him. Together they made it to where their feet touched sand. She limped out of the water with whatever dignity remained, which was none. On the towel, Leo knelt and pointed at her foot.

“May I?”

“Absolutely not.”

He waited.

“Oh, for God’s sake,” she said, and gave him her foot.

He pressed his thumbs into the arch, firm and careful. Pain flared, then loosened. The intimacy was absurdly disproportionate. Her heel in his hand, his wet hair dripping onto his wrist, the beach roaring around them with other people’s ordinary happiness. She wanted to laugh. She wanted to cry. She wanted to say, Don’t be kind to me unless you mean it, which was a childish and unfair demand, since kindness was not a contract.

“Better?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He did not immediately let go. Or perhaps he did and she imagined the delay. She pulled her foot back, reaching for the scarf in her bag as if this had been the plan all along.

Clouds moved in during the afternoon, sudden and theatrical. The wind picked up, flipping towels, sending plastic cups skittering across the sand. Nina announced that the replacement lunch—fried calamari, bread, oranges, two bottles of white wine—should be eaten immediately before nature developed further objections. They sat in a rough circle, sandy and sunburned, passing food with greasy fingers. Someone made a toast to “work completed and lies told about work completed.” Everyone drank.

Leo sat across from Mara now. This was safer and worse. She could watch him without appearing to watch: his face turned toward whoever was speaking, his habitual half-smile, the way he tore bread into precise small pieces. Priya asked him about Berlin. He answered lightly, naming neighborhoods, a project, a friend with a spare room. No mention of the girlfriend. Mara despised herself for noticing.

Then Nina, who had consumed enough wine to become benevolent and dangerous, said, “You two have been thick as thieves.”

Mara felt the group’s attention tilt. Not fully, not cruelly. Just enough.

“We run a small nation,” Leo said, nodding toward her bag.

“Ah yes,” said Mara. “He’s applying for citizenship.”

There was laughter. The moment passed, except it did not. Under the laughter was a heat more scalding than embarrassment: recognition. So they had been visible. Their discretion, so intricate, so mature, had been a child hiding behind a curtain with shoes sticking out.

After lunch the weather worsened. Not rain, but wind strong enough to sandblast their legs. The sea darkened. Umbrellas snapped shut along the beach like startled birds. They gathered their things, chasing wrappers and towels. Mara’s scarf blew toward the rocks; Leo ran after it and caught it just before it reached a tide pool. He returned triumphant, scarf streaming.

“My flag,” she said.

“Our flag.”

There it was. Our. Tiny, careless, catastrophic.

Their return bus was late. Of course it was late. They waited in the beach parking lot while the sun lowered behind clouds, everyone tired now and less performative. André’s jellyfish sting had become a legend he was already improving. The director counted heads obsessively. Mara sat on a low wall with her sweatshirt around her shoulders. Leo stood beside her, close enough that his hip brushed her arm when he shifted.

“I’m bad at this part,” he said.

“What part?”

“The end of things that weren’t exactly things.”

She kept her face toward the parking lot. A gull stalked a discarded cone, suspicious of its own luck.

“Maybe those are the only things that end properly,” she said. “Actual things linger. Paperwork. Regret. Shared subscriptions.”

He laughed softly. “You’re very comforting.”

“I’m famous for it.”

Silence. Not empty. Never empty; damn him for teaching her that. The wind against the bus shelter. Nina shaking sand from a tote bag. A child crying because his balloon had become abstract, high above the parking lot.

Leo said, “Mara.”

She looked at him. He seemed tired, younger than he was or older. The blue shirt had dried stiff with salt. There were grains of sand along his jaw. Kiss me, she thought, with sudden irritation. Or don’t. But don’t make me stand here inside the possibility of it until I become ridiculous.

“I’m glad you came,” he said.

It was both exactly right and an insult.

“To the beach?” she asked.

“To the residency.”

“Oh.”

A bus turned into the lot then, exhaling, merciful and unforgivable.

On the ride back, people slept. Heads knocked windows; mouths fell open. The interior smelled of salt, damp towels, oranges. Mara and Leo sat together again, because changing seats would have meant something, and not changing seats meant something else. There was no neutral act left to them.

The sky bruised purple over the hills. As the bus climbed inland, the sea vanished behind them without ceremony. Mara leaned her forehead against the glass. She could see Leo’s reflection beside hers, faint and superimposed on the passing dark. If she turned, their faces would be too close. If she stayed still, they could remain like this: two ghosts traveling in the same direction.

He reached down between their seats. For a wild second she thought he would take her hand. Instead he picked up the paper bag of apricot pits and peach stones, folded it neatly, and held it in his lap.

How tender, how disappointing, how like life.

At the residency, the house was lit from within, windows glowing amber. Everyone unloaded slowly. Sand spilled onto the gravel. Good nights were murmured, plans made for final drinks, showers, packing, sleep. Leo carried nothing but his towel and the folded bag. Mara had her overfull tote, heavier now with wet things.

At the foot of the stairs, they stopped. The corridor above led one way to his room, another to hers. All month, the building had conspired in meetings: by the kettle, by the mail table, under the fig tree, outside the studio where one of them happened to be passing. Now it offered only geometry.

“I have to leave at six,” he said.

“I know.”

“I’ll probably see you at breakfast.”

“There won’t be breakfast at six.”

“No.”

“So.”

“So.”

Someone dropped a shampoo bottle in the upstairs bathroom; it bounced twice, loud as a verdict. They both laughed, grateful for the interruption and hating it.

Leo stepped closer. Not much. Their shadows joined on the wall before they did. He smelled of salt and dust and the cheap soap from the outdoor showers. Mara’s heart behaved with embarrassing literalness.

He kissed her cheek.

Not her mouth. Her cheek. A kiss so brief it might be filed under friendship, under Europe, under farewell. Yet his lips paused near the corner of her mouth, and his hand touched her shoulder, and for a moment her entire life narrowed to the half-inch he had not crossed.

“Good night, Mara,” he said.

“Good night.”

He went upstairs. She watched him until the turn in the stairwell took him. Then she stood a moment longer, holding her bag, feeling foolish and spared and bereft.

In her room, the bed was stripped. She had forgotten she’d done that in the morning, efficient woman, enemy of future comfort. The mattress lay bare and institutional. Her suitcase gaped on the floor, half-packed, accusing. She peeled off her sandy clothes, showered badly, put on a clean shirt. In the mirror her face looked windburned, ordinary. So this was the face of a woman to whom almost something had happened.

She sat at the desk. Outside, voices rose from the courtyard: the others opening wine, refusing the end a little longer. She should go down. She would go down in a minute. Instead she opened her notebook.

For weeks the pages had resisted her, all false starts and brittle sentences. Now she wrote: The house had already begun to forget them. She paused. Too neat. Too true. She kept going.

She wrote about a bus that broke down beside a field of sunflowers, about stolen rolls and a green bus with lemon curtains, about a man who made recordings of silence and did not kiss a woman when perhaps he should have, or kissed her exactly as much as he could. She did not write his name. She did not write hers. That was one kind of mercy.

At some point, laughter rose sharply from below—Leo’s, maybe, or someone else’s. She stopped, pen hovering. There was still time to go downstairs. Still time to stand near him under the courtyard lights, to say something brave and unbeautiful, to convert the almost into error or memory. But she remained where she was.

Not cowardice, necessarily. Or not only. There were desires that lived because they were unfed, taking their shape from restraint. There were departures that made a room inside a person and left it furnished forever with salt, wind, a paper bag, the pressure of thumbs against the arch of a foot.

Tomorrow he would leave before sunrise. Monday she would leave too. The residency would receive new bodies, new hesitations, new jokes in the kitchen. Her room would be cleaned. His towel would vanish from the balcony rail. The beach would go on being blue for strangers.

Mara bent over the notebook. The house hummed around her: pipes, voices, a door closing softly somewhere upstairs. Silence, almost never empty. She wrote until the page filled, until the night outside the window deepened, until the thing that had not happened became, by degrees, the thing she had.
