By the time they reached the coast, the sandwiches had gone warm, the camera had stopped working, and Miko was pretending not to be carsick.

“It’s because you insisted on sitting in the back,” Ellis said, glancing at her through the rearview mirror.

“It’s because Jonas drives like he’s being chased by a minor god.”

Jonas, hunched at the wheel of the residency’s borrowed van, laughed without looking away from the road. “This is called confidence.”

“This is called manslaughter in its larval stage,” said Miko.

Ellis smiled, though no one could see. He was in the middle seat, wedged between tote bags, beach towels, and a cardboard box of supplies donated by the kitchen: oranges, a jar of olives, two bottles of cheap white wine, the sandwiches wrapped in wax paper, and a watermelon they had bought impulsively from a roadside stand because Miko had pointed at it and said, with theatrical sorrow, “It looks lonely.”

That had been three hours ago, back when the mountains still held the road on both sides and the residency house had been only twenty minutes behind them. Now the landscape had opened and flattened. Low salt marshes shone in the afternoon light. The air coming through the cracked windows had changed, grown damp and mineral, threaded with the faint promise of rot and seaweed.

Their last trip to the beach.

No one had called it that when they left. Officially it was a group outing: five residents escaping the old estate for the day before final presentations, final dinners, final packing. Jonas had organized it, because Jonas organized everything he had not been asked to organize. Priya had come because she said she needed “sky larger than my own thoughts.” Amalia came because she never refused an opportunity to swim.

Miko came because Ellis had said, two nights ago, leaning against the kitchen counter while the rest of the house slept, “I’ve never seen the Atlantic from this side.”

And she had looked at him over the rim of her mug. “Then it would be rude not to go.”

Now, in the front passenger seat, Priya held her phone above her head, searching for signal. “The map says we turn soon.”

“The map,” Jonas said, “has betrayed us twice already.”

“The map is the only one in this vehicle with a plan.”

Amalia, asleep beside Miko, snored once very softly and woke herself up. “Are we there?”

“We are nowhere,” Miko said. “We are between versions of ourselves.”

Ellis turned his head toward her. She was wearing the blue shirt she had worn when they first met in the studio hall six weeks earlier, the one with paint along the cuff though she wasn’t a painter. Her hair was braided today, though strands had escaped and stuck to the side of her face in the heat. She caught him looking and raised one eyebrow.

“What?”

“You all right?”

“Perfect. I adore nausea. It makes me feel vivid.”

He wanted to say, I know when you’re lying now. Instead he said, “There’s ginger candy in my bag.”

She blinked, surprised. “You have ginger candy?”

“I’m a person of depth.”

“Where’s your bag?”

“Under the watermelon.”

This turned out to be a problem. The watermelon, lonely no longer, had rolled during a sharp turn and lodged itself behind the cooler. Amalia, revived by the possibility of action, attempted to excavate it and elbowed Jonas in the shoulder. Jonas shouted. The van swerved. Priya shrieked, “Brake!” at precisely the moment a flock of gulls exploded from a roadside ditch like torn paper.

They pulled into the beach parking lot ten minutes later with everyone alive, though Jonas claimed he had aged significantly and would be applying for a pension.

The beach was not beautiful in the expected way. It was too windy, for one thing. The sand was coarse and mixed with shells that pricked their feet. A row of low dunes, roped off with sagging twine, divided the parking area from the shore. Beyond them, the ocean moved under a pewter sky, roughened by wind. Far down the beach, a lighthouse stood blunt and white at the end of a jetty.

“It’s perfect,” Priya said.

Miko had gone quiet. She stood with her sandals in one hand, staring out at the water as though listening to someone speak from very far away.

Ellis carried the box of food down to the sand. Jonas immediately lost one of the bottles of wine, though not in the usual way; it slipped from the box, rolled into a shallow tide pool, and was claimed by a surprisingly forceful wave. Amalia chased after it, laughing, and returned soaked to the knees but victorious, holding the bottle aloft like a mythic sword.

“Sacrifice rejected,” she announced.

They laid out towels behind a dune where the wind was weaker. The sandwiches were, as predicted, warm and structurally compromised. The olives spilled twice. Someone had forgotten cups, so they drank wine from the bottle, passing it from hand to hand. Jonas attempted to cut the watermelon with a pocketknife too small for the task, until Miko took it from him and performed the operation with calm brutality, splitting the fruit open on a flat rock.

“Where did you learn that?” Ellis asked.

“My uncle used to sell melons in summer.”

“You have an uncle who sells melons?”

“I contain multitudes, Ellis.”

“Yes,” he said, and she glanced at him sharply, as if his tone had done something it shouldn’t.

He looked away first.

That was how it had been between them for days now: every joke carrying another, hidden object inside it. Every ordinary sentence brushing against what neither of them had said.

In the beginning, their friendship had seemed inevitable and unserious. They were the only two residents whose studios were in the converted stables, where the roof leaked and the mice conducted reckless night expeditions. Ellis was a composer from Toronto trying to finish a piece about weather systems. Miko was a poet from Manila working on a sequence about hotel rooms, grief, and saints. Their schedules crossed at odd hours. She worked late, he early. Often, at dawn, he would find her in the kitchen, barefoot, making toast after having never gone to bed.

The first time, she had said, “Don’t speak to me until I’ve burned something.”

He had silently handed her the butter.

After that came walks, cigarettes she never finished, arguments about whether the residency piano was haunted or merely out of tune. She listened to his drafts with her eyes closed. He read her poems and pretended not to recognize himself in certain lines about weather, though maybe he was arrogant to think so. Maybe everybody thought poems were secretly about them.

Three weeks in, during a thunderstorm that knocked out power across the estate, they sat on the back stairs under a shared blanket while rain hammered the gravel courtyard white. Miko had placed her cold fingers against the inside of his wrist to prove a point about circulation. Neither of them moved for a long time. Then Priya had opened the kitchen door behind them, illuminating them in yellow light, and said, “Oh. Sorry. I thought you were raccoons.”

Nothing happened after that.

Or everything did, depending on one’s standards.

On the beach, the afternoon expanded and thinned. Amalia swam despite the cold and came out roaring. Jonas tried to teach Priya how to skip stones and hit himself in the ankle. The sky brightened briefly, then clouded again. Ellis walked to the edge of the water and let it take his feet. The Atlantic was colder than he expected. It made him gasp.

Miko came to stand beside him.

“You see?” she said. “Rude not to go.”

He nodded. The wind pressed her shirt against her body and then released it. Her braid had mostly given up. Loose hair whipped around her face.

“Do you leave on Tuesday?” he asked.

“Monday morning.”

He knew this. Everyone’s departure time had been posted on the corkboard in the entry hall. Still, hearing it from her changed it.

“Right,” he said.

“My flight is obscenely early. A car is coming at four.”

“That’s not morning. That’s still night.”

“I know. I plan to be insufferable about it.”

A wave slid up around their ankles, foamed, withdrew. He watched their feet sink slightly as the water pulled sand from under them.

“And you?” she asked.

“Wednesday. Train to New York, then up to Montreal for a few days, then home.”

“Composers move like spies.”

“Poets move like fugitives.”

“Accurate.”

He waited for her to say something else. She didn’t. Behind them, Jonas was shouting about the loss of a Frisbee to the wind. Priya laughed so hard she had to sit down. The sound came thinly across the beach.

Miko crossed her arms. “What happens to the piece?”

“My piece?”

“No, Ellis. The Lord’s Prayer.”

He smiled. “I don’t know. It’s not finished.”

“You always say that.”

“It’s always true.”

She turned toward him. “You’re afraid of finishing things.”

It might have been teasing if she hadn’t looked so serious.

He bent to pick up a shell, then dropped it because it was occupied by something small and outraged. “Maybe.”

“I didn’t mean it as an insult.”

“I know.”

“I mean, I did a little.”

He laughed, but it came out softer than he intended.

She looked back at the sea. “I’m afraid of beginning things.”

This was the nearest they had come.

He felt suddenly that if he moved, if he even breathed too distinctly, the moment would scatter like the gulls from the ditch. He wanted to ask what she meant. He wanted to tell her that for weeks he had lived in a ridiculous suspension, measuring days by whether she knocked on his studio door, whether she sat next to him at dinner, whether her name appeared in his mind before sleep with the regularity of prayer. He wanted to ask if she felt it too, this strange brief life they had made inside a life designed to end.

Instead, because cowardice often wore the costume of gentleness, he said, “Beginning things can be overrated.”

Miko’s expression changed. Not much. Just a small withdrawal, like a curtain lifting in reverse.

“Yes,” she said. “Probably.”

A voice called from behind them. Amalia stood on the towel, holding Ellis’s dead camera. “Who knows how to fix old machinery?”

“Hit it with a rock,” Jonas suggested.

“Do not hit my camera with a rock,” Ellis called.

The interruption arrived like weather. They returned to the towels, where the camera was diagnosed by committee. Priya suggested rice, though there was no rice. Jonas suggested opening it with the same pocketknife that had failed the watermelon. Miko turned it over in her hands carefully, as though it were a bird.

“When did it stop?”

“In the van,” Ellis said.

“Maybe the battery.”

“It’s not digital.”

“Then maybe its soul.”

“Can you fix souls?”

“I’ve had mixed results.”

She lifted the camera to her eye and pointed it at him. “Say something memorable.”

He looked at her through the dark little circle of glass. “I’m sorry,” he said.

The others kept talking. The wind moved around them. Miko lowered the camera.

“For the camera?” she asked.

“No.”

She held his gaze for one second, two. Then Jonas dropped an olive into the sand and shouted, “Five-second rule,” and whatever had opened closed again.

Later, they walked to the lighthouse.

Not all of them. Amalia fell asleep under a towel. Priya stayed behind to write in her notebook. Jonas decided he had “a complicated relationship with jetties” and declined. So Ellis and Miko went alone, though nobody remarked on it, which felt both merciful and cruel.

The beach narrowed as they walked. The tide was coming in, erasing the smooth sand nearest the water. They climbed over black rocks slick with weed. At one point Miko slipped, and Ellis caught her by the elbow. Her skin was cool and gritty with salt. She steadied herself but did not immediately pull away.

“I’m fine,” she said.

“I know.”

“You can let go.”

“Right.”

He did.

At the lighthouse, the wind was stronger. A sign warned them that climbing was prohibited, but the door stood open a few inches, banging softly against its frame.

“That seems like an invitation,” Miko said.

“That seems like tetanus.”

“You’re so Canadian.”

“That’s not what that means.”

Inside, it smelled of rust and bird droppings. The metal stairs spiraled upward into dimness. They climbed anyway, slowly, one hand on the rail. Halfway up, Miko began laughing.

“What?” Ellis asked.

“Nothing.”

“That’s never true.”

“I just thought: if we die here, Jonas will make it about his driving.”

Ellis laughed too, and the sound rose strangely in the narrow tower. At the top, they emerged onto a small circular platform enclosed by cloudy glass. The view was imperfect but wide: beach, marsh, parking lot, the toy-sized van, their friends reduced to bright dots on the sand.

Miko leaned against the railing. “I love seeing people from far away. It makes them seem forgivable.”

“Are they not, up close?”

“Not always.” She wiped condensation from the glass with her palm. “Up close, people want things from you.”

Ellis stood beside her. “And far away?”

“Far away, they’re only moving.”

He thought of all the distances waiting for them: airports, cities, time zones, obligations, the ordinary gravity of separate lives. He thought of emails written too late, calls not made because of the time difference, the gradual embarrassment of having once been briefly necessary to someone.

“You could visit,” he said.

Miko looked at him.

He forced himself to continue. “Or I could. Visit you. Sometime.”

“Sometime,” she repeated.

“Yes.”

“That’s a very large word.”

“I know.”

She smiled sadly. “Ellis.”

His name, in her mouth, sounded like something being set down with care.

“I don’t mean tomorrow,” he said. “I just mean—”

“What?”

He had no answer simple enough.

She turned away from the view and faced him fully. The wind rattled the glass. Her face was open now, and tired. “Tell me what you mean.”

He looked at her, and all his practiced evasions deserted him. “I mean I don’t want this to be only this.”

Her eyes shone, though whether from wind or feeling he couldn’t tell.

“And what is this?” she asked.

He laughed once, helplessly. “That’s the problem.”

“No,” she said. “That’s the truth.”

Below them, a gull cried like a hinge.

Miko pressed her hands together, fingers to lips, then lowered them. “In two days I’ll be on a plane. In three, I’ll be in my mother’s apartment pretending I’m not a different person. In a week you’ll be somewhere cold making thunder on a piano. We’ll write at first. Long emails. Then shorter ones. Then holiday messages. Then one day one of us will see the other has published something and feel strange over coffee.”

“You sound very sure.”

“I’m not sure. I’m terrified.”

“Of what?”

“That if we try to make it larger, we’ll ruin the part that was true.”

He wanted to argue. He wanted to say truth was not a porcelain cup, not something saved by keeping it on a shelf. But he also knew she was not entirely wrong. The residency had held them in an enchanted parenthesis: meals cooked by someone else, time unspooled, selves temporarily freed from context. They had met in weather that could not last.

Still.

He stepped closer. Not touching. “I don’t want to be sensible.”

Miko laughed, and this time the tears were obvious. “That is the least sensible thing you’ve ever said.”

“I have range.”

She reached for him then, or he reached for her; afterward he could not have said. Their kiss was brief at first, surprised. Then less brief. Her mouth was cold from the wind. His hand found the back of her neck, the loose braid, the salt-tangled hair. She leaned into him with an urgency that felt less like beginning than admission, as if they had been kissing for weeks in every silence and had only now become visible.

When they broke apart, both were laughing softly, though nothing was funny.

“Well,” she said.

“Yes.”

“That was unwise.”

“Deeply.”

She touched his cheek once with her thumb. “We should go back.”

“We should.”

Neither moved.

Then, from below, distant but unmistakable, Jonas yelled, “The tide! The tide is doing a thing!”

By the time they descended, the strip of beach by the jetty had nearly vanished. Water surged between the rocks they had crossed, leaving no dry route back. Miko removed her sandals with the grim composure of someone entering battle.

“This is your fault,” she said.

“My fault?”

“You distracted me with emotional recklessness.”

They waded thigh-deep through freezing water, shrieking despite themselves. Ellis slipped and soaked his shorts. Miko lost one sandal, recovered it, then lost her dignity entirely when a wave struck her from behind and made her grab him around the waist. They stumbled onto the wider beach breathless, drenched, and laughing so hard Priya stood up in alarm.

“What happened to you?”

“Nature,” Miko said.

“Hubris,” Ellis said.

Jonas looked between them, eyes narrowing with theatrical suspicion. “The lighthouse changes people.”

“No one ask them anything,” Amalia mumbled from under her towel. “They’ll only lie.”

They ate the rest of the watermelon as the light lowered. The wind eased. Clouds broke in the west, and the sun came through beneath them, turning the wet sand gold. Ellis’s camera, having been given up for dead, suddenly clicked when Miko pressed the shutter. Everyone cheered. She took one photograph of Jonas bowing to the rescued wine bottle, one of Priya squinting into the sunset, one of Amalia wrapped in towels like a disgruntled queen.

Then she handed the camera to Priya and pulled Ellis down beside her on the sand.

“Take one,” she said.

Ellis looked at her.

She did not look away.

Priya raised the camera. “Closer. You both look like you’re attending a municipal hearing.”

Miko leaned her shoulder against his. Ellis, after a second, leaned back. The shutter clicked.

On the drive home, everyone slept except Jonas, who sang softly to keep himself awake, and Ellis, who sat in the back this time beside Miko. At some point in the dark, her hand found his under the towel between them. They held on without speaking. It seemed important not to pretend, and equally important not to explain.

The residency house was lit when they returned, every window golden, moths circling the porch lamps. Inside, the others dispersed—showers, tea, frantic last edits, the small domestic mess of leaving. Miko and Ellis stood in the entry hall among bags of sand-crusted towels.

“So,” she said.

“So.”

“I have to pack.”

“I know.”

“And you have to pretend your piece is unfinished.”

“It is unfinished.”

She smiled. “Of course.”

For a moment, the house around them seemed to listen.

Ellis said, “Can I see you tomorrow?”

“You’ll see me at breakfast.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know.” She took a breath. “Yes.”

It was not a promise beyond morning. It was barely a promise at all. But it was something placed carefully in the space between them, and neither wanted to break it by asking it to become more.

At the foot of the stairs, she turned back. “Ellis?”

“Yes?”

“When you finish the piece, don’t make the ending too neat.”

He nodded. “When you write about this, don’t make me too noble.”

“I would never.”

Then she went upstairs, barefoot, carrying her sandals in one hand.

Ellis remained in the hall a little longer, listening to the old house settle. From somewhere above came the sound of a suitcase zipper, then laughter through a wall, then water running in the pipes. Outside, beyond the dark lawns and darker trees, the road to the coast lay unseen, already belonging to yesterday.

He would remember the day incorrectly, he knew. Memory would fix the weather, improve the sandwiches, make the ocean bluer than it had been. It would turn misadventure into charm and silence into meaning. Perhaps it would even suggest that their departures had been tragic, when really they were ordinary, scheduled, printed on the corkboard in black ink.

But not yet.

For now there was sand in his shoes, salt drying on his skin, and the faint pressure of her hand still held in his palm. For now, the ending had not ended. It had only begun to move away.
