By the time the bus finally arrived, Mira had already eaten half the peaches meant for the beach.

“They were bruising,” she said when Leo looked into the paper bag.

“They were not bruising,” he said. “They were ripening. There’s a difference.”

“Everything’s ripening until it isn’t.”

This was the kind of sentence Mira had been saying all month at the residency, and the kind Leo pretended to hate. She said things that sounded accidental, tossed off, and then they stayed with him at inconvenient hours: over morning coffee, in the studio when the light was wrong, in bed beneath the slow fan that clicked once every rotation like a tiny metronome measuring out the days they had left.

Today they had one day left.

Tomorrow, the residency van would shuttle everyone to the train station in two batches: sculptors and composers before breakfast, writers and painters after. By evening, the old villa on the hill—its cracked courtyard, its sour oranges, its parade of wet swimsuits along the balcony—would belong to a new group of strangers. The month would fold shut as if it had been invented for no other purpose.

Leo and Mira stood at the roadside with their towels, two dented water bottles, a bag of peaches, and the shared silence of people who have spent too much time together without deciding what that time means.

The bus sighed to a stop in front of them. Its blue paint was sun-scoured almost white. A hand-lettered sign behind the windshield read: SPIAGGIA / CIMITERO / PORTO.

“Beach, cemetery, port,” Mira translated, climbing aboard. “Perfect itinerary.”

The driver, who wore sunglasses with only one arm, did not look up as Leo fumbled with coins. The bus lurched before he had sat down, sending him into the lap of an elderly woman carrying a net bag of mussels.

“Scusi,” he said, flushing.

The woman patted his wrist as if he had accomplished something charming.

Mira watched from the back seat, trying not to laugh and failing.

“You could have helped,” Leo said when he reached her.

“I was preserving the moment for later.”

“For your novel?”

“For court testimony.”

The bus shuddered down the hill from the residency, past groves of olive trees and houses the color of old teeth. Below, the sea appeared in flashes between buildings—hard blue, metallic, impossible to own. Mira pressed her forehead to the window. In profile, she looked younger than she was, though Leo wasn’t sure how old that was exactly. Thirty? Thirty-four? Age had become one of the facts they had neglected to exchange, along with where this was going, if anywhere, and whether the night three weeks ago when they had fallen asleep on the roof with their fingers touching counted as a beginning or an accident.

The bus stopped at the cemetery, where two teenage boys boarded carrying a radio and an inflatable dolphin. At the next stop, a dog got on alone. No one seemed surprised. The driver reached back and scratched its head before continuing.

“This country has better public transportation for dogs than we do for people,” Leo said.

Mira smiled but didn’t turn from the window.

He wanted to ask what she was thinking. He wanted, more specifically, to ask whether she had changed her flight. Last night, at dinner, she’d mentioned that Lisbon was cheap in September. “Practically free,” she’d said, passing him a bowl of clams without looking at him. Everyone at the table began advising her where to stay, what to eat, who to email about reading opportunities. Leo had sat with his fork suspended, absurdly wounded by a city he had never been to.

He was flying to Chicago tomorrow. He had a sublet, a deadline, a sister’s baby due in two weeks. His life was waiting with its mouth open.

Mira’s life, it seemed, might simply not.

At the beach, the bus released them into a blare of heat. The stop was nowhere near the beach, as it turned out, but at the top of a long road that unspooled between shuttered villas and dust-coated cactus. Somewhere below them, people shrieked happily.

“You said it was close,” Leo said.

“I said the map implied proximity.”

“The map lied.”

“Mira nodded gravely. “Many institutions do.”

They walked downhill under the enormous noon sun. The bag of peaches dampened Leo’s hand. Mira had tied her hair in a scarf, but strands kept escaping and sticking to her neck. At one point a motorbike came too close, and Leo reached for her elbow to pull her back. His fingers remained there half a second longer than necessary. They both noticed. Neither commented.

“You’re very protective for someone who wouldn’t share the last coffee this morning,” she said.

“You said you didn’t want any.”

“I said, ‘Is that the last coffee?’ There are layers to communication.”

“I’m a poet, Mira. I understand layers.”

“You write prose poems about parking lots.”

“And yet.”

At the end of the road, the beach opened before them: a crescent of pale sand pinned between black rocks, umbrellas blooming in strict rows, children flinging wet handfuls of the world at one another. The sea moved with green light near the shore and darkened beyond the buoys. It was beautiful in the ruthless way of places that have no idea what you are about to lose.

They had planned to meet some of the others there—Anika, Tomas, the Dutch painter whose name Leo still couldn’t pronounce—but no one was in sight. Mira checked her phone.

“No service,” she said.

“Good.”

She gave him a quick look. “Good?”

“I mean, unfortunate. We’ll have to survive alone with our thoughts.”

“That sounds unsafe.”

They found an empty patch of sand near the rocks. Leo spread his towel. Mira spread hers at a precise angle beside it, not touching. Between them they placed the bag of peaches like a chaperone.

For a while, the day was simple. They swam. The first shock of water erased the heat from Leo’s body, and when he surfaced, Mira was already farther out, laughing as a wave lifted her. He followed. They floated on their backs, ears submerged, sky trembling above them. Underwater, the world became a held breath.

Mira stood when the sandbar rose beneath them. Her wet shirt clung to her ribs; her scarf had come loose and streamed beside her like seaweed.

“When do you leave tomorrow?” she asked.

The question arrived so casually that Leo almost missed its danger.

“Eleven-thirty train,” he said. “You?”

“Van at eight. If I go.”

“If?”

“There’s a ferry to Palermo in the afternoon.”

He looked toward shore. A child was trying to bury his father alive.

“You’re going to Palermo?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then Lisbon?”

“I don’t know that either.”

“You say that like it’s a philosophy.”

“You say that like knowing has helped anyone.”

A wave broke between them, splashing Leo in the face. He wiped salt from his eyes. “I’m just asking.”

“No, you’re not.”

He opened his mouth, but she had already turned and swum back toward the beach, her arms cutting cleanly through the water.

On shore, everything irritated them. The sand stuck to the peaches. The water in the bottles had warmed to soup. A vendor came by selling bracelets and would not leave until Mira bought one with a blue bead shaped like an eye.

“For protection,” she said, tying it around her wrist.

“From what?”

She looked at him. “Bad questions.”

They ate in silence. The peaches had, in fact, bruised, and were so ripe they collapsed under their thumbs. Juice ran down Mira’s wrist, over the bracelet. Leo watched her lick it away and felt ashamed of how intensely he noticed.

He wanted to say: Come with me.

He wanted to say: I could come with you.

He wanted to say: I know we made a joke of this, all of it—the walks into town, the cheap wine on the roof, the way you read drafts on my bed because your room had the moths—but I was not joking, or not only joking, and if you tell me this was just a month, I will believe you eventually, but not today.

Instead he said, “You have peach on your chin.”

She wiped the wrong side.

“Other side.”

She wiped again, missed again, then leaned toward him as if presenting evidence.

“Just—” He reached out and brushed it away with his thumb.

For a moment she did not move. His hand hovered near her face. Her eyes were greenish in the beach light, though he had always thought they were brown. Everything about her seemed newly inaccurate.

Then, from the rocks behind them, someone shouted.

A ball struck Leo on the back of the head.

He pitched forward, knocking over the bag of peaches. Two rolled into the sand. One was immediately claimed by a small dog, possibly the same dog from the bus, though Leo could not prove it.

Mira stared at him, then at the dog trotting triumphantly away with the peach in its mouth.

The laugh burst from her so suddenly that she covered her face with both hands. Leo rubbed his head, indignant, until he began laughing too. The ball’s owner, a solemn boy in orange swim trunks, retrieved it without apology.

“Are you injured?” Mira asked when she could speak.

“Gravely.”

“Do you need mouth-to-mouth?”

He looked at her. The joke hung there, glittering, too close to the thing itself. Her smile faltered first.

“I’m going for a walk,” she said.

“Okay.”

“I mean, you can come.”

“Oh.”

He stood too quickly and got dizzy.

They followed the shoreline away from the umbrellas, toward the black rocks. The sand grew coarse, then stony. Beyond the point, they found a smaller cove littered with bottle caps and driftwood. A shrine had been wedged into a crack in the cliff: a plastic Madonna, faded flowers, two candles melted into blue puddles.

Mira crouched to examine it. “Someone loved this place.”

“Or feared it.”

“That’s often the same thing.”

Leo picked up a flat stone and threw it at the water. It sank immediately.

“Terrible,” she said.

“I never claimed to be athletic.”

“You claim lots of things quietly.”

He turned. “What does that mean?”

She stood, brushing sand from her knees. “Nothing.”

“No. Say it.”

She folded her arms. “You act like you’re the reasonable one. Like I’m chaotic because I don’t know where I’ll be next week. But you’ve decided everything and somehow made that my fault.”

“I haven’t made anything your fault.”

“You sulked through dinner because I mentioned Lisbon.”

“I didn’t sulk.”

“You practically became furniture.”

He laughed once, sharply. “What did you want me to do? Congratulate you on maybe leaving for another country without ever—” He stopped.

“Without ever what?”

The cove had grown quiet except for water combing through stones. Out at sea, a white ferry moved along the horizon, slow and indifferent.

“Without ever talking to me,” he said.

Mira’s face changed. Not softened, exactly. Opened, then closed again.

“I talk to you all the time.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Do I?”

“Yes.”

She turned away, looking toward the ferry. “Maybe I don’t want to make some sad little plan. Maybe I don’t want to trade airport times and promises we can fail at. Maybe I wanted one thing in my life not to become logistics.”

“That’s convenient.”

She looked back. “What?”

“Calling it pure because you won’t name it.”

Her eyes flashed. “And what would naming it do? Save it? You live in Chicago. I live out of a suitcase and pretend grants are a personality. We met in a collapsing villa because twelve strangers were paid badly to make art near the sea. It’s not exactly a foundation.”

“It was something.”

“I know.”

The words came out quieter than either of them expected.

Mira looked down at her wrist, at the cheap bracelet with the blue eye. She twisted it once, twice.

“That’s the problem,” she said.

Leo wanted anger then. Anger would have been easier than her sadness, which made him feel as if he had wandered into a room where she was changing. He put his hands in his pockets and found, inexplicably, a peach pit.

“We should go back,” he said.

“Yes,” she said. “We should.”

But the sea had crept higher while they argued. The route around the rocks was now slick, water slapping against stone. They attempted it anyway. Halfway across, Mira slipped. Leo caught her by the wrist, and she caught him by the shirt, and for a few seconds they were both laughing and cursing, soaked to the thighs, neither stable enough to rescue the other.

“This is exactly what I wanted to avoid,” she said, breathless.

“Drowning?”

“Metaphor.”

They retreated to the cove and climbed, badly, over a low section of cliff where thornbushes tore at their legs. At the top they found themselves in the back garden of a closed restaurant. Plastic chairs were stacked beneath a tarp. A cat watched them from an outdoor sink with the expression of a disappointed owner.

A gate led to the road, but it was locked.

“Of course,” Leo said.

Mira tried the latch again. “Maybe it’s decorative.”

“It’s a locked gate, Mira.”

“Some things are conceptually locked.”

He was about to respond when she hoisted herself onto the stone wall beside it.

“What are you doing?”

“Leaving conceptually.”

“Mira—”

But she was already swinging one leg over. Her bag caught on a nail. She froze, half on one side, half on the other, hair falling in her face.

“Don’t,” she said.

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You’re smiling.”

“I am absolutely not.”

“You are smiling with your bones.”

He helped untangle the bag. She dropped to the road with a grunt, then looked up at him.

“Your turn, reasonable man.”

He climbed less gracefully, scraped his palm, and landed beside her in a puff of dust. She took his hand without asking and inspected the cut. Her thumb moved across his palm, removing a fleck of grit.

“You’ll live,” she said.

“Unfortunately for my enemies.”

She didn’t let go immediately. Neither did he.

The walk back to the main beach took twenty minutes. They did not speak much. At a kiosk, Leo bought them lemon granitas from a woman who seemed personally offended by his pronunciation. Mira held the cold paper cup against her forehead.

When they reached their towels, Anika and Tomas had appeared and colonized the area with beer, figs, and news.

“There you are!” Anika cried. “We thought you’d been murdered.”

“Almost,” Mira said. “By geology.”

The afternoon loosened around them. The others talked loudly about residencies they hated and editors who owed them money and whether genius was mostly a scheduling issue. Someone produced a deck of damp cards. Tomas got sunburned in the exact shape of his sunglasses. The Dutch painter fell asleep with a beer in his hand and woke only when it spilled into his armpit.

Leo laughed when he was supposed to. Mira did too. Around them the day performed abundance: light on water, gulls fighting over bread, music from the umbrellas, the smell of salt and frying oil. But beneath it, time continued its small vandalism.

Near evening, the group decided to walk to the port for dinner. Everyone gathered bags and shook sand from towels. Mira searched once, then again.

“My bracelet,” she said.

The blue eye was gone from her wrist.

“It was cheap,” Leo said, then immediately wished he hadn’t.

She looked at the sand. “I know.”

The others were already drifting toward the road. Leo crouched and began searching. After a moment, Mira did too. They sifted through the cooling sand side by side while the beach emptied around them.

“You don’t have to,” she said.

“I know.”

“It’s stupid.”

“I know.”

They found bottle caps, a safety pin, the orange boy’s missing shovel, but not the bracelet. The sun lowered itself into a haze, turning the sea pewter. At last Mira sat back on her heels.

“Okay,” she said. “Let it go.”

Leo continued for another few seconds, then stopped.

They missed the bus to the port.

This was discovered when they reached the stop just in time to see it turning the corner, the dog visible in the rear window, looking back as if it had expected better of them.

Mira began to laugh. Leo, despite himself, laughed too.

“We can walk,” she said.

“To the port? That’s miles.”

“Everything is miles.”

“Another philosophy.”

“I contain several.”

They walked. The road curved along the coast, sometimes close enough that spray touched their faces, sometimes rising above coves where the water had already darkened. Cars passed with their headlights on. Their shoulders brushed once, then again, and eventually they stopped moving apart.

At a bend in the road, they found a bench facing the sea. Neither suggested stopping; both did.

The first stars had appeared, faint and provisional. Below, the ferry they had seen earlier was entering the port, lights strung along its decks.

“I am afraid,” Mira said.

Leo looked at her.

She kept her eyes on the ferry. “That’s all. I know I make it sound like freedom, but mostly it’s fear with better scenery.”

He nodded slowly. “I’m afraid too.”

“Of what?”

“Going home and finding out I made this month up.”

She smiled a little. “You have witnesses.”

“I mean us.”

The word settled between them without drama. Us. Small, reckless, insufficient.

Mira leaned back against the bench. “I don’t know how to do this.”

“Me neither.”

“That’s not comforting.”

“No.”

She turned toward him. “I don’t want to promise you anything tonight. I can’t. I’ll ruin it by morning.”

“I don’t want a promise.”

It was not entirely true, but it was close enough to be generous.

“What do you want?” she asked.

The sea moved below them, endlessly arriving, endlessly leaving. Leo thought of all the answers that would frighten her, frighten him. A future. A plan. A sentence with too much weight.

“This,” he said.

Mira looked at him for a long moment. Then she rested her head on his shoulder.

They sat that way until the ferry finished docking. Until the port became a necklace of lights. Until the damp began to rise from the ground and their clothes cooled against their skin.

When they finally reached the residency, long after dinner, the villa was mostly dark. Someone had left two plates in the kitchen covered with another plate: pasta gone sticky, grilled eggplant, a note from Anika that read, SURVIVORS EAT.

They ate standing at the counter, ravenous and quiet. Upstairs, the hall smelled of salt, wet towels, and turpentine. At Mira’s door, they paused.

In a different story, Leo thought, this would be the moment. A hand on the knob. A kiss that clarified everything or ruined it. Music somewhere. The moon arranging itself helpfully in the window.

But the villa offered only a flickering hall light and Tomas snoring through the wall.

Mira touched Leo’s scraped palm.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

It was not a plan. It was barely a word. Still, he nodded.

“Tomorrow.”

She went into her room. He stood a moment in the hall, listening to the old building shift around him. Then he went to his own room and did not sleep.

In the morning, she left in the early van.

Leo came downstairs at seven-thirty and found her in the courtyard with her suitcase, drinking coffee from a chipped cup. Her hair was wet. She wore the same blue dress she had worn the first night of the residency, when everyone had introduced themselves over too much wine and she had said she was there to write about weather, “unless weather turns out to be about grief, which it usually is.”

“You’re going,” he said.

“For now.”

“Palermo?”

She shrugged. “The ferry exists.”

“That’s a start.”

The driver began loading luggage. Around them, goodbyes flared and vanished: hugs, email addresses, promises no one trusted but needed anyway.

Mira reached into her pocket. “I found this in my bag.”

She opened her hand. The bracelet lay there, the blue eye scratched but intact.

Leo laughed softly. “Protection works in mysterious ways.”

“I thought you should have it.”

He looked at her. “Why?”

“So you can ask bad questions safely.”

He let her tie it around his wrist. Her fingers were cool and quick. When she finished, she did not step away.

“I’ll write,” she said.

“You don’t have to promise.”

“I’m not promising. I’m warning you.”

Then she kissed him, lightly, almost formally, on the mouth. It lasted less than a second. It changed nothing. It changed the shape of the morning.

The driver honked.

Mira picked up her bag, then put it down again and hugged him hard. His arms closed around her. He felt the knobs of her spine, the damp ends of her hair against his cheek, the breath she took before letting go.

“Go write your parking lots,” she said.

“Go write your weather.”

She got into the van. Through the dusty window she lifted her hand, then seemed to reconsider the gesture and pressed her palm flat against the glass. Leo lifted his own hand, the bracelet bright on his wrist.

The van rolled through the gate, past the sour orange trees, down the hill toward the road that led to the beach, the cemetery, the port.

Leo stood in the courtyard until it disappeared.

Later, on the train, he would find sand in his shoes and a peach pit in his pocket. Weeks after that, in Chicago, a postcard would arrive with no return address, only a smudged drawing of a ferry and three words: Weather is holding.

But that morning, there was only the courtyard after departure, the white walls beginning to heat in the sun, and the faint blue eye on his wrist, watching over nothing he knew how to keep.
