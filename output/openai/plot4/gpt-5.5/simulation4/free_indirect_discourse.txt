By the time Mara found the borrowed van, Theo was already in the passenger seat with his bare feet on the dashboard, eating a peach as if he had been waiting there all his life.

“You’re late,” he said.

She checked the time on her phone. Seven minutes. In residency time, seven minutes was nothing. People had missed meals, critiques, entire days. Poets drifted into breakfast at noon with wet hair and guilty faces. Painters appeared at dinner in the same clothes they had worn all week, smelling faintly of turpentine and sleep. Seven minutes was practically early.

“You’re in the wrong seat,” she said.

“I get carsick in the back.”

“You get carsick in cars?”

“Only when I’m not driving.”

The van belonged to the director, a pale blue thing with a dented side door and sand permanently embedded in the floor mats, though the residency sat nearly two hours inland. It had been offered to them for one last beach trip before everyone scattered: to Brooklyn and Berlin, to fellowships and adjunct offices, to apartments where dishes waited in sinks and rent resumed its monthly stalking.

Mara slid into the driver’s seat and turned the key. The engine coughed, considered the proposition, and relented. In the rearview mirror she saw Lila climbing in with a straw hat and three tote bags, Jon carrying a cooler, Sima already taking pictures of the morning light on the gravel drive. The others were coming in a second car. There had been ten of them at the residency in June, then nine when the sculptor left after a fight with his grant foundation, then eight after the novelist’s father died. Now six remained for the beach, which made it feel less like an outing than a remnant.

Theo rolled down the window. “We should get coffee.”

“We just left breakfast.”

“That wasn’t coffee. That was brown water with institutional ambitions.”

Mara told herself not to smile. It was important not to reward him too easily. For four weeks she had been trying to establish a private system of regulation: one smile per three jokes, one question for every two he asked her, no touching unless accidental, no looking when he was looking back. She had succeeded in a general, ornamental way. Anyone observing them—Lila, for example, who observed everything—might have seen nothing except two residents who had fallen into a conversational habit. A convenience. Their studios were across from each other in the old stable. They both worked late. They both hated the clanging dinner bell. They both went walking when their drafts collapsed.

That was all. Except it was not all, and the fact that it was not all had become the third person in every room.

They stopped for coffee in a town so small the café was also a bait shop. The sign read ESPRESSO + WORMS. Theo said it was his ideal literary magazine. Mara bought an iced coffee and a bag of vinegar chips, though it was barely nine in the morning. At the counter, the woman asked where they were headed.

“Blue Point,” Mara said.

The woman paused, one hand on the register.

“Not today, you aren’t.”

“Why not?”

“Bridge is out. Storm last week. County’s fixing it, which means they’ve put up cones and gone home.”

Behind Mara, Theo made a soft delighted noise, as if the day had just improved.

“There’s another way,” the woman said. “Adds an hour. Maybe more if the ferry’s running late.”

“The ferry?” Jon repeated, from behind a display of fishing lures.

“Or you can go to the state beach. Same ocean.”

Same ocean. As if that settled anything.

Blue Point had been Lila’s suggestion, a secluded crescent where the dunes grew tall and the tourists didn’t bother. It had acquired the glow of fate simply by being named aloud at dinner the previous night. Mara had imagined it too completely: the white heat, the first shock of cold water, Theo sitting beside her on a towel, close enough that their shoulders might touch without either of them admitting intent. To go somewhere else now felt like a small moral failure.

“We’ll take the ferry,” Theo said.

Mara turned. “We will?”

He lifted both hands. “Unless you’re afraid of adventure.”

She was not afraid of adventure. She was afraid of the exact opposite: that nothing would happen, that the day would pass pleasantly, safely, and then tomorrow he would fly to Oregon and she would take the train back to Philadelphia and whatever had trembled between them would remain forever unprovable.

“The ferry, then,” she said, and immediately hated how ceremonial it sounded.

They were lost within twenty minutes.

The alternate route wound through salt marsh and private roads that ended abruptly at padlocked gates. Sima’s phone lost service, then Lila’s, then Theo’s, which he held up to the window like a votive object. Jon unfolded a paper map from the glove compartment, the kind that seemed to include every road except the one they were on.

Mara drove past cedar scrub and tilting mailboxes, trying not to appear tense. The van smelled of coffee, sunscreen, peach, and the sour dampness of the cooler. Theo had taken off his sunglasses and was studying the map upside down.

“Left here,” he said.

“That’s a driveway.”

“It has a name.”

“It has a mailbox.”

“Lots of roads have mailboxes.”

She kept driving. Behind them, Jon muttered, “I think we lost the second car.”

They had. When service returned in a single miraculous bar, Lila’s phone filled with messages from the others, who had given up and gone to the state beach. There was some discussion of joining them. Sensible discussion. Democratic discussion. Mara listened while pretending to focus on a tractor ahead of them, its enormous wheels shedding mud.

“Well,” Theo said finally, “we’ve come this far.”

It was not an argument, but it worked on her like one.

The ferry, when they reached it, was not a ferry in the majestic sense. It was a flat metal platform with a peeling booth and a man in a sun-faded cap who looked offended by their arrival. It crossed a narrow inlet at a pace that suggested philosophical reluctance. They parked behind a truck carrying crates of tomatoes. Everyone got out.

The marsh opened around them, green and gold and humming. Egrets picked through the shallows with priestly care. The sky was too large. Mara leaned on the railing and let the wind push hair into her mouth.

Theo came to stand beside her. Not too close. He was skilled at not too close.

“So,” he said, “last day.”

“Not technically. We still have the closing dinner.”

“The closing dinner is not a day. It’s an autopsy.”

She laughed then, not according to system. He looked pleased, which irritated her, which pleased her.

“You’re packed?” she asked.

“Absolutely not.”

“Of course.”

“I work best under threat.”

“You work only under threat.”

“That’s different.”

He turned toward the water. The wind flattened his shirt against him, showing the narrowness of his chest, the slight inward curve of his shoulders. He looked younger in hard sunlight, almost breakable. Ridiculous thought. Men like Theo were never breakable; they moved through life scattering fragments and calling it weather.

At dinner three nights earlier, he had said, “I don’t really believe in permanence.” He had been talking about installations, maybe, or poems, or love—he had that infuriating habit of letting abstraction do the dangerous work. Lila had rolled her eyes. Mara had taken a long drink of wine. Permanence was not the point, she had wanted to say. The point was whether anyone stayed long enough to be responsible for the shape they left behind.

Now he said, “I’ll miss the stable.”

Not her. The stable.

“The mice will miss you,” she said.

“They understand my process.”

“They ate your granola.”

“Part of the process.”

She could have asked him then. What will you miss? Who? She could have made it light, almost cruel. She was good at that when she wanted to be. But the ferry knocked gently against the dock, and the moment passed into the general clatter of reentering the van.

On the other side, the road narrowed and worsened. Sand blew across the asphalt in pale snakes. The van rattled ominously. The beach was still twenty minutes away when the cooler tipped over and flooded the back with melted ice. Jon swore. Lila lifted her bags like a woman escaping a shipwreck. Sima laughed so hard she cried.

Then the van made a sound like cutlery in a disposal and lurched to the shoulder.

For a moment no one spoke.

“That was probably normal,” Theo said.

Steam rose from under the hood.

“That was not normal,” Mara said.

They got out into heat that seemed to press directly on the skull. The road ran empty in both directions. On one side, marsh. On the other, dunes roped off with signs warning them not to disturb nesting birds. Mara opened the hood because she was the driver, therefore, by some ancient law, responsible for combustion. A hot metallic smell rose at her.

“Do you know anything about engines?” Lila asked.

Mara looked into the mysterious arrangement of hoses and caps. “I know this one is angry.”

Theo leaned beside her. “My father once taught me how to change a tire.”

“Is that relevant?”

“No. But it’s something I know about cars.”

They called the residency director, who did not answer. They called the rental agency number listed in the glove compartment, though the van was not a rental, and reached an automated menu with strong opinions about office hours. They called the roadside assistance number on a card that had expired two years ago.

Finally, the man from the ferry drove by in a pickup, slowed, and regarded them with the bleak satisfaction of someone whose worst expectations had been met.

“Overheated,” he said, after looking under the hood for four seconds.

“Yes,” Mara said. “I gathered that from the steam.”

He glanced at her. For a second she thought he might smile, but he only spat into the sand.

“There’s a gas station mile up. They got coolant. Maybe.”

A mile. In the heat. Along a road without shade.

“I’ll go,” Mara said.

“I’ll come,” Theo said too quickly.

Of course he would. Of course he would turn it into something: a pilgrimage, a scene, a final walk under a punishing sun. She wanted to say no. She wanted to say yes before he finished offering. Instead she shrugged.

They set off with two empty water bottles swinging from Theo’s hand. Behind them, the others arranged themselves in the thin strip of shade cast by the van, already looking abandoned.

For several minutes they walked without speaking. The road shimmered ahead. Grasshoppers sprang from the verge. Mara could feel sweat gather at the base of her spine and slide downward. She wished she had worn something more flattering than cutoffs and an old black T-shirt with ink stains near the hem. Then she was annoyed at herself for wishing it. Then annoyed that annoyance also involved him.

Theo kicked a pebble. “This is very us.”

“There is no us.”

The words came out before she could civilize them.

He looked at her.

She kept her gaze on the road. Well. There it was, ugly and small, lying between them like roadkill.

“I meant the group,” he said after a moment.

“Sure.”

“Mara.”

“No, sorry. That was—” What? True? Mean? Pathetic? “The heat.”

“Blame the heat. Classic.”

They walked. The silence now had edges.

She had not planned to say anything. That had been the whole point of her dignity. She would leave with her notes and her revised manuscript and the faint bruise of an almost-affair. She would think of him sometimes, unfortunately. When buying peaches. When hearing someone laugh in the next room. When stuck on a line she could not fix because it was too careful and needed damage. But she would not have been the woman who asked, the woman who made a shape and watched him step away from it.

He said, “I know I’ve been—”

“Don’t.”

“You don’t know what I was going to say.”

“Yes, I do. You were going to say ‘weird.’ Or ‘difficult.’ Or ‘careful.’ Something self-forgiving.”

He laughed once, without humor. “Self-forgiving?”

“You’re very eloquent about your own limitations.”

That landed. She felt it land and wished it hadn’t. His face closed, not dramatically. Just enough.

Ahead, the gas station appeared like a hallucination: two pumps, a garage, a vending machine buzzing with wasps. Mara went inside, grateful for the dimness, the smell of rubber and cold soda. A teenage cashier looked up from his phone as if they were interrupting a private grief.

They found coolant in the back, beside antifreeze and motor oil. Theo paid before she could offer. Outside, they filled the water bottles from a spigot attached to the wall.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

He screwed the cap onto one bottle. “For which part?”

“The accurate part.”

This time he did smile. Barely.

She leaned against the wall. The concrete radiated heat through her shirt. Across the road, the dunes shifted in the wind, though of course they did not shift; they only appeared to. That was the cruelty of distance, or desire. Everything moving until you touched it.

Theo said, “I wasn’t going to say weird.”

“No?”

“I was going to say afraid.”

She looked at him then.

He studied the bottle in his hands. “But that also sounds self-forgiving.”

“What are you afraid of?”

A truck passed, loud enough to erase the first version of whatever he said. Maybe that was mercy.

He waited until it was gone. “Wanting things at the wrong time.”

She could not think of a cutting reply. How disappointing. All her intelligence, and nothing.

“Theo.”

“I know.”

“You don’t even know what I’m going to say.”

“I do.”

And perhaps he did. Perhaps they had been having the same conversation for weeks beneath all the others: under arguments about art, under jokes about residency coffee, under late-night reports from their separate childhoods offered in the blue light of the vending machine outside the kitchen. Perhaps the entire month had been one long approach toward this gas station with wasps at the trash can and coolant in a plastic jug.

He reached out and removed a strand of hair stuck to her cheek.

It was a simple gesture. Almost nothing. She felt it everywhere.

For a second neither of them moved. Then she could have stepped forward, or he could have. There were possibilities, whole branching futures, all of them foolish. A kiss beside a rural gas station. A confession rehearsed too late. A promise neither trusted. Instead the cashier banged on the window and held up Theo’s sunglasses, which he had left on the counter.

They laughed because the alternative was impossible.

By the time they returned, the others had made friends with a woman selling boiled peanuts from the back of her car. Jon had sunburn across one knee. Lila was reading aloud from a pamphlet about piping plovers in a tone of increasing hysteria. The van accepted the coolant with grudging dignity. After twenty minutes, it started.

When they finally reached Blue Point, it was nearly four.

The beach was not empty. Two families occupied the far end with umbrellas and shrieking children. A couple slept under matching hats. Gulls stalked the tideline. But the water was there, enormous and indifferent, and everyone forgave the day at once.

They ran in like people younger than they were. The ocean shocked Mara breathless. Salt filled her mouth. Sima dove cleanly under a wave; Jon yelped; Lila lost her hat and pursued it with grim athleticism. Theo surfaced near Mara, hair plastered to his forehead, eyes bright.

“Worth it?” he asked.

She floated on her back, ears underwater, hearing the muffled boom and drag of the sea. Above her, the sky stretched blank and blue. Worth what? The van, the heat, the argument, the almost-touch? The whole month? The leaving?

“Ask me later,” she said.

They stayed until the light changed. The families packed up. The sleeping couple woke and kissed with the weary efficiency of long practice. Mara sat on her towel, damp and salted, eating vinegar chips with sandy fingers. Theo lay beside her, not touching, one arm thrown over his eyes.

The others wandered down the beach to look at tide pools. No one announced leaving them. No one had to. Lila, subtle as a tax notice, glanced back once and then continued walking.

Mara watched a sandpiper stitch the shore with its feet.

Theo said, “My flight’s at eleven.”

“I know.”

“Right.”

“You told everyone at breakfast.”

“I did?”

“Twice.”

He lowered his arm and looked at her. In the low sun, his face was all planes and shadows. She wanted, suddenly and sharply, to remember it incorrectly. To make him less beautiful later. To give him flaws he did not have or enlarge the ones he did: cowardice, vanity, a tendency to hide inside charm. But memory would do what it wanted. It always had.

“I don’t want to make this into something it can’t be,” he said.

There. The sentence. Balanced, mature, unbearable.

Mara nodded. She could be balanced too. She could be so mature she vanished.

“No,” she said. “God forbid.”

He flinched, and she was glad, and sorry.

The tide pulled at the sand. A gull cried like a hinge.

Then Theo sat up. “I’ll miss you,” he said.

She stared at the water.

It was not enough. It was exactly what she had wanted. It came too late. It came while he was leaving. It came without plan, without remedy, without even the decency of being useless, because already it changed something. Already the day rearranged itself around those words.

She brushed sand from her knee. “I’ll miss you too.”

He exhaled as if he had been carrying something heavy and had set down only half of it.

When he kissed her, it was not dramatic. No orchestral surf, no collapsing world. He simply leaned in, and she let him, or leaned too, and his mouth was warm and tasted faintly of salt and peach. One kiss, then another, slower. His hand hovered at her shoulder before resting there, tentative, as though asking permission of bone.

She thought: this is a mistake.

She thought: finally.

Free indirect thoughts okay. Continue.

When the others returned, they were sitting apart again, but not as far apart as before. Lila looked at Mara and said nothing, which was the closest she came to tenderness.

The drive back was quieter. Everyone was exhausted by sun and mishap. Jon fell asleep against the window. Sima scrolled through photos, deleting most of them with ruthless little taps. Lila hummed along to a radio station that dissolved into static every few miles.

Theo sat in the passenger seat, feet properly on the floor now. Once, in the dark, his hand found Mara’s on the gearshift. Only for a moment. A private punctuation. Then a pair of headlights came around a bend, filling the van, and he withdrew.

Back at the residency, the gravel drive glowed under the porch lamps. Moths beat themselves against the light. The old house looked both familiar and already lost, as if departure had begun without them.

Everyone unloaded towels, empty bottles, the cooler now smelling strongly of warm fruit. There were jokes about the van, about the ferry man, about Lila’s hat, which had been recovered but not emotionally. Then people drifted toward showers, studios, beds.

Mara stood by the van with sand drying on her ankles.

Theo lingered on the other side of the open door.

“So,” he said.

She almost laughed. After everything, so.

“So,” she said.

They could go to his studio. Or hers. They could extend the day into night, make it harder, make it memorable in the obvious way. Tomorrow they could part with the stunned politeness of people who had taken more than they were able to keep.

He seemed to be thinking this too. She could see it move across his face: want, caution, want again.

Mara was tired of caution only when it belonged to him. Her own she considered wisdom.

“I should shower,” she said.

“Me too.”

Neither moved.

Then he stepped forward and kissed her forehead. A strange choice, almost old-fashioned, almost worse than the other kisses. It suggested care. It suggested restraint. It suggested an alternate universe in which time had behaved.

“Goodnight, Mara.”

“Goodnight.”

In her room, she did not turn on the light. Moonlight lay in a pale rectangle on the floor. Her suitcase stood open by the bed, half-filled, reproachful. From down the hall came the uneven music of pipes, doors, someone laughing softly into a phone. Tomorrow all of this would be anecdote. The broken bridge, the ferry, the overheated van, the gas station, the beach reached too late. Theo would become a person she mentioned carefully, or not at all.

She sat on the bed and tasted salt at the corner of her mouth.

It had been foolish. It had been insufficient. It had been, despite every intelligent objection, something.

Outside, the cicadas screamed in the dark as if summer were not ending, as if noise could hold a place open, as if any of them could stay.
