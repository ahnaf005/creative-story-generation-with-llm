**The Last Beach**

On the last Friday of the residency, the sea did not appear where it was supposed to.

Mira stood at the edge of the road with her tote slipping down her shoulder, staring past the scrubby fields and the low stone walls at a horizon that had promised blue on the map but offered only more road, more dust, more white heat lifting off the asphalt in wavering sheets. Beside her, Leo unfolded the printed directions for the third time, though there was no reason to believe they would reveal anything new. The paper had softened from sweat and sea plans, its corners gone furry.

“It says fifteen minutes from the bus stop,” he said.

“We’ve been walking for forty.”

“Maybe we’re slow walkers.”

She looked at him. He had taken off his sunglasses to read, and without them his face seemed too open, almost boyish, though he was thirty-seven and given to quoting dead poets before breakfast. A bead of sweat ran from his temple into the dark curl near his ear. He did not wipe it away. He was enjoying this, she realized, or enjoying the story it would become: the final beach trip, two residents lost among the goats and olive trees, the sea hiding itself like a prankster god.

She wanted not to be irritated. It was, after all, the last excursion, the last time they would be together without anyone else from the residency interrupting to ask what they were working on, what they were avoiding, which deadline they had murdered most recently. Tomorrow night there would be the closing dinner at the long table under the fig tree, speeches and photographs, the director kissing cheeks, the laundry room emptying of borrowed shirts and paint-stained towels. On Sunday, Mira would fly back to Chicago. Leo would take the ferry north, then some train, then another ferry. He had explained the route to her once, tracing it on the table with the handle of a spoon, as though distance were something that could be persuaded into elegance.

Now they were lost.

“You were in charge of directions,” she said.

“I was in charge of optimism.”

“That was my mistake.”

He grinned and folded the paper badly, creating a new crease through the name of the village. A truck passed, carrying crates of peaches. The driver honked, not angrily, just to mark them as figures in the landscape: woman with hat, man with towel, neither where they belonged.

Mira adjusted the strap of her sandal. A blister had risen on her heel, a small, shining betrayal. She had chosen the sandals that morning because Leo had once said they looked like something an actress would wear in an Italian film, which was a stupid reason to choose anything, especially footwear. She was thirty-four, not a girl in a movie, not even convincingly relaxed. The hat she wore was too big; the tote contained two peaches, sunscreen, a notebook she pretended not to have brought, and a bathing suit she was beginning to suspect she would never use.

They had been at the residency for six weeks in a converted convent in the hills. Twelve artists, writers, translators, and one composer who worked exclusively at night and made everyone feel as if the building were haunted by a meticulous appliance. Mira had come to finish a sequence of essays about weather and grief. Leo had come to translate a cycle of poems from a language he described as “almost extinct, but not in a tragic way.” In the first week she had found this unbearable. In the second week she had found him funny. By the third, she had learned the rhythm of his steps on the stone stairs and could identify his cough from across the courtyard.

It was not a romance. That was important. Or it had become important to insist upon, internally if nowhere else. There had been wine, yes, and late conversations on the terrace, and the night of the power outage when they had sat in the kitchen eating olives from the jar while everyone else wandered around with phone flashlights. There had been the one almost-touch, when he leaned past her to retrieve a bottle opener and the side of his hand grazed her waist. Nothing had happened. Nothing was a country people moved through all the time, building towns there, raising flags.

Leo turned in a circle, shading his eyes.

“I think I hear waves,” he said.

“That’s wind.”

“Wind can be waves if you believe.”

“I don’t.”

“Tragic.”

They walked on.

The road dipped, narrowed, and became a track. Dust coated Mira’s feet. The air smelled of thyme and hot stone. Somewhere in the brush, insects clicked with mechanical devotion. Leo offered to carry her tote, and she said no too quickly. He accepted this without drama, though he glanced at the bag as if it had hurt him personally.

At last they saw a sign: **Spiaggia**, painted blue on a plank nailed to a crooked post. An arrow pointed toward a gap in the bushes.

“There,” Leo said, with the solemn triumph of a man who had done nothing.

The path descended sharply. Mira slipped once and caught herself on a branch, scraping her palm. Leo reached for her, but by then she was upright. Too late, always too late, or perhaps she moved too quickly to give anyone the satisfaction of rescuing her. He asked if she was all right. Fine, she said. Fine, fine, fine; the national anthem of women who had carried themselves too long.

The sea, when it finally appeared, was not the wide gentle beach they had imagined from the residency kitchen, with umbrellas and a little bar selling cold beer. It was a cove of dark stones cupped between cliffs, wild and nearly empty. The water shone a bruised blue, deepening to cobalt beyond the rocks. Three German hikers were leaving as Mira and Leo arrived, their calves heroic, their backpacks bristling with equipment. One of them pointed at the water and said something that sounded like a warning.

“What did he say?” Mira asked.

Leo, who did not speak German, nodded wisely. “Welcome, probably.”

“He looked worried.”

“Germans often do. It’s part of their charm.”

The beach was beautiful in an inconvenient way. No shade except under an overhang where someone had left cigarette butts and a deflated soccer ball. No sand, only stones that shifted treacherously underfoot. No other people now, which should have felt like luck but instead enlarged the silence between them.

They laid their towels side by side with a gap that seemed casual and exact. Mira took off her dress, trying not to perform the act, which of course made it feel even more like a performance. Her bathing suit was black, practical, cut lower than she remembered. Leo looked out to sea with exaggerated courtesy. That was worse than looking. She wanted to laugh, to tell him she did not mind, to ask whether he had always been this careful or whether she had made him so. Instead she busied herself with sunscreen.

“Do you want—” he began.

“No, I’m fine.”

He held up the tube. “I meant some.”

“Oh.” She took it. Their fingers did not touch.

She rubbed lotion on her arms, her neck, the tops of her thighs. She could feel him beside her doing the same, the faint slick sounds, the coconut smell, the absurd intimacy of two adults preparing their skin for damage. He asked if she would put some on his back. She hesitated only half a second, which was enough time for an entire alternate life to flare and vanish.

“Sure,” she said.

He sat in front of her on the towel, shoulders slightly hunched. His back was pale where his shirt had hidden it, narrow but strong, the spine a delicate ridge. She squeezed sunscreen into her palm and spread it over him, briskly at first, then slower despite herself. There was a small mole beneath his left shoulder blade. A childhood scar near his ribs. She had not known his body contained such ordinary facts. It seemed indecent to learn them now.

“Cold,” he said.

“Sorry.”

“It’s all right.”

Was it? His voice had altered, dropped into a register she had heard only twice before: once when he read her a poem in the library because he said the English failed unless spoken aloud, and once at two in the morning when he told her his marriage had ended not with betrayal but with fatigue. That had seemed sadder to her. Betrayal at least had architecture: doors, rooms, someone entering where they should not. Fatigue was fog.

She finished and patted his shoulder, lightly, like a nurse dismissing a patient.

“There,” she said.

He turned his head, not quite enough to look at her. “Thank you.”

They went into the water.

The stones hurt. Mira picked her way down like an old woman, arms lifted for balance. Leo splashed ahead, then yelped.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“What happened?”

“A sea urchin may have expressed an opinion.”

“You stepped on one?”

“No, no. We’re negotiating.”

She laughed despite herself, and the laugh loosened something. The water reached her knees, then her hips, cold enough to steal her breath. She plunged in before she could reconsider. For an instant the world vanished into blue pressure and the thunder of her own body. When she surfaced, Leo was floating on his back, eyes closed, his hair darkened and sleek.

“You see?” he said. “Worth the pilgrimage.”

She would not give him that. “Maybe.”

They swam out past the first shelf of rock. The cove opened around them, the cliffs rising like weathered walls. From the water, the shore looked far away, their towels small flags of surrender. Mira floated on her back and watched gulls tilt in the hard white sky. She could almost forget the airport shuttle booked for Sunday morning, the unfinished essay on her laptop, her apartment with the radiator that clanged like a trapped animal. She could forget that Leo had a life full of people whose names she had learned incompletely: a sister in Lisbon, an ex-wife named Clara, a friend called Pip or Pippa who had loaned him a coat. She had no rights over the inventory of him. She had no claim.

A sharp sting sliced across her forearm.

“Ow.”

Leo righted himself. “What?”

Another sting, this time on her thigh. She looked down and saw nothing but blue, which made it worse.

“Something stung me.”

Then he swore. “Jellyfish.”

The swim back became undignified. Mira tried to move calmly and failed, slapping the water, imagining translucent bells pulsing all around her with malicious grace. Leo kept saying, “Slowly, slowly,” while not going slowly at all. By the time they reached the rocks, they were both laughing and cursing. She stumbled, and this time he caught her elbow. She let him. Only because of the jellyfish, obviously. Only because the rocks were slick.

On shore, the stings rose in red threads along her skin. Leo inspected them with grave attention.

“Don’t say it,” she warned.

“I would never.”

“I know what you’re thinking.”

“That I should pee on you?”

“Leo.”

“I was thinking that folk medicine is often misunderstood.”

She threw a pebble at him. He dodged theatrically, then produced from his bag a crumpled paper napkin and a bottle of water. He poured water over her arm. Useless, probably. Kind. Worse than useful.

His fingers hovered near the mark on her thigh. “May I?”

She nodded.

He touched the skin around the sting, not the sting itself, as if reading heat. The contact was slight. It moved through her disproportionately. Ridiculous. She looked at the cliffs, the abandoned soccer ball, anywhere else. He withdrew his hand.

“You’ll live,” he said.

“Generous prognosis.”

“I’m known for hope.”

They ate the peaches, which had gone warm and soft in the tote. Juice ran down Mira’s wrist. Leo gave her his napkin; she refused; he gave it anyway. The afternoon settled over them. They spoke of safe things: the composer’s nocturnal thumping, the director’s belief that all conflict could be solved with lentils, the translator from Montreal who had cried over a broken espresso machine and later claimed it was conceptual.

Then the safe things ran out.

Leo leaned back on his elbows. “What time do you leave Sunday?”

“Shuttle comes at seven.”

“So early.”

“Yes.”

“Mine leaves at ten.”

She nodded. The difference of three hours felt like an insult arranged by fate.

“You’ll be back in winter,” he said. “Chicago winter. Character-building.”

“Mostly slush-building.”

“You’ll send me pictures.”

Would she? Was that what they were promising now, pictures of weather? Snow as a socially acceptable form of longing. “Maybe,” she said.

He heard the hesitation. Of course he did; he heard punctuation in poems written by dead people. He sat up and began turning a pebble between his fingers.

“I’d like to stay in touch,” he said.

There it was, the small practical phrase under which whole doomed empires sheltered. Stay in touch. As if touch were the thing they had managed; as if it could be kept, like receipts in a drawer.

“Me too,” she said, because anything else would be cruelty or truth, and she was tired.

He smiled, but not quite. “You say that like you’re agreeing to a committee assignment.”

“I’m bad at departures.”

“Everyone is bad at departures.”

“No. Some people are elegant. They write notes. They hug at the correct pressure. They say, This has meant so much, and don’t sound like they’re resigning from a job.”

“And you?”

“I make jokes until I disappear.”

“That explains the hat.”

She touched the brim. “The hat is sincere.”

“The hat is a barricade.”

Wind lifted the edge of her towel. She pressed it down with her palm. He had come too close to something, or she had. The afternoon had that dangerous clarity she hated, when every object seemed outlined for memory: the salt drying on his collarbone, the peach pit near his knee, the crease between his brows he got when he was deciding whether to be brave.

He said, “Mira.”

“No.”

He blinked. “I haven’t said anything.”

“I know.”

A gull cried overhead, harsh and unimpressed.

He looked away first. Good. Or not good. The space between them filled with all the sentences he had not said and she had preemptively refused. I like you. I’ll miss you. Come with me. Don’t go. None of them practical, all of them adolescent, all of them capable of ruining the one thing they still had: this cove, this afternoon, the fact that until sunset they could pretend the future had missed the bus.

After a while he lay back and covered his face with his arm.

“I was going to say you have sunscreen on your ear,” he said.

She laughed. It came out too loudly, almost a bark. “Liar.”

“Yes,” he said, not moving his arm. “A little.”

They did not swim again. The jellyfish had claimed the water. They dozed in broken intervals, waking whenever stones shifted beneath them or the sun moved cruelly onto an exposed patch of skin. Mira dreamed briefly that the convent had floated out to sea with all their suitcases inside, the director standing at the gate waving a ladle.

When the light began to soften, Leo checked his phone.

“We should go if we want the last bus.”

The phrase had a ceremonial gloom. Last bus. Last beach. Last chance to say nothing in particular.

They dressed with the awkwardness of people who had not undressed together but had somehow become aware of the possibility. Mira’s dress stuck to her damp skin. Leo shook stones from his sandals. He patted his pockets, then froze.

“What?” she said.

He patted them again, more urgently.

“What?”

“The keys.”

“What keys?”

“To my room. And the studio. I put them—” He looked at the towels, the bags, the stones, the entire hostile beach. “I put them somewhere.”

Mira closed her eyes. Of course. A final misadventure required an offering.

They searched. They lifted towels, unpacked bags, inspected the crevices between stones. Leo apologized every ninety seconds until she told him to stop apologizing or she would bury him there. The sun lowered. Shadows pooled under the cliff. The last bus, no doubt, was already preparing to leave without them, driven by a man with no interest in narrative satisfaction.

At last Mira found the keys inside one of her sandals.

“How did they get there?” Leo said.

“You tell me.”

“I have no memory of this.”

“Convenient.”

“You saved us.”

“I saved your poems from spending the night alone.”

He took the keys from her palm. This time their fingers touched. Neither of them moved for a second. Such a small contact, hardly anything. She had touched strangers more intimately on crowded trains. Still, the moment opened under her like deep water.

Then he slipped the keys into his pocket.

“Thank you,” he said.

They climbed the path in the amber light. Mira’s blister had become a fact of existence. Leo walked behind her, close enough that once, when she paused, he nearly bumped into her. At the top, they saw the bus at the far bend in the road, already moving.

“No,” Leo said, and began to run.

Mira ran too, tote banging against her hip, hat clutched in one hand. The bus did not slow. Leo waved both arms. Mira shouted, though what she shouted was not language. The bus rounded the bend and disappeared.

They stood panting in the road.

“Well,” Leo said finally.

She bent over, hands on knees, laughing. It was terrible, all of it. The blister, the jellyfish, the lost keys, the vanished sea and vanished bus, the impossible tenderness of a man she had known for six weeks and would soon know mostly through glowing rectangles, if at all. Terrible and funny. She laughed until her eyes watered.

Leo watched her, then started laughing too.

They walked back toward the village, hoping for a taxi, a miracle, a farmer with a generous truck. The sky turned lavender. Their shoulders brushed once, then again. Neither adjusted course.

After a long silence, Leo said, “You know, if we miss dinner, they’ll eat all the fish.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“I don’t want to sit at that table tonight.”

He glanced at her. “No?”

“No.”

They walked another few steps.

“There’s probably a bad café in the village,” he said.

“Probably closed.”

“A vending machine.”

“Broken.”

“A bench.”

“Infested with ants.”

“Perfect.”

She smiled in the dimming light. Ahead, the first lamps of the village flickered on, one by one, as if someone were making a path for them, though of course no one was. No one had arranged any of this. That was the problem and the mercy. They had only the road under their sore feet, the night gathering, the residency waiting behind the hills with its packed suitcases and unsaid things.

Leo offered his arm then, not gallantly, not jokingly. Just offered.

Mira looked at it. She could refuse; she was accomplished at refusal. She could keep the day intact in its almostness, preserve the clean ache of what had not happened. Instead she took his arm.

Only until the village, she told herself.

Only until Sunday.

Only.
