On the last Friday of the residency, Mara woke to the sound of someone knocking badly on her door.

Not impatiently—badly. Like the person was trying to communicate apology through the wood.

She rolled out of bed, still wearing the shirt she’d painted in the day before, and opened it to find Eli in the corridor with two paper cups of coffee and a canvas tote slipping down his shoulder. The hallway smelled of turpentine, burnt toast, and the sea just beyond the dunes. Somewhere down the hall, the composer from Berlin was playing three notes on the piano over and over, as if trapped in a thought.

“I brought breakfast,” Eli said.

“You brought coffee.”

“I brought the idea of breakfast.”

Mara took one of the cups. “It’s eight.”

“Yes,” he said. “The tide, apparently, waits for no one.”

They had been saying they would go to the beach since the first week of the residency, when all twelve of them had arrived carrying duffel bags, unwieldy ambitions, and the thin optimism of people selected for something. The center occupied an old converted convent on the coast, pale stone with rusting balconies, its studios facing either the sea or the parking lot depending on your luck or artistic merit, which everyone pretended was not a factor.

Mara had come to paint water without painting the sea. Eli had come to finish a novella about a man who disappeared during a municipal snowstorm. They had met on the first evening when Mara spilled red wine into his lap during introductions. He had looked down at the spreading stain on his beige trousers and said, “Finally, a plot.”

After that, their days began accumulating around each other without permission. They smoked outside the laundry room though neither of them really smoked. They sat across from each other at dinner and failed to disguise it as coincidence. She listened to him read drafts in the courtyard, his voice low and suspicious of every sentence. He came to her studio and stood too close to her canvases, as if proximity might reveal what she was trying to hide.

Nothing had happened. Almost nothing. Once, after a party in the printmaking studio, they had walked back through the herb garden under a moon so large it seemed theatrical. Eli had touched the back of her wrist and said, “You have blue paint here.” She had looked down. There was no paint. Neither of them had mentioned it.

Now there were three days left.

“Who else is coming?” Mara asked.

“Everyone who claimed they were coming has stopped answering messages. So either no one, or all of them in a sudden burst of guilt.”

She went back inside to find her swimsuit, though she disliked swimming and owned a swimsuit in the same theoretical way Eli owned a breakfast. When she returned, he was examining the postcard she’d taped to her door: a black-and-white photograph of a woman walking into fog.

“You packed?” he asked.

“Don’t be vulgar.”

“My train is Monday at ten.”

“I know.”

The words landed between them with the soft thud of a dropped towel.

Outside, the August morning was already bright enough to seem accusatory. They borrowed two rusted bicycles from the shed, though only one had a functioning brake. Eli insisted Mara take it because he had, in his words, “made peace with speed.” The road to the beach curved past fig trees and low white houses, then dipped toward the sea. The air smelled of salt and diesel and sun-warmed fennel.

Mara rode ahead, her dress ballooning around her knees. Behind her, Eli sang fragments of songs he half remembered and got mostly wrong.

At the market near the harbor, they stopped for provisions. The shop was narrow and crowded with inflatable beach toys, postcards, peaches, newspapers, sunscreen, anchovies, hats, and a cat asleep in a crate of onions. Eli bought nectarines, a loaf of bread, two bottles of mineral water, and a packet of biscuits shaped like animals.

“You’re thirty-four,” Mara said.

“And still capable of wonder.”

At the counter, he patted his pockets and went pale.

“No wallet?” she asked.

“No wallet.”

“You invited me.”

“I invited you spiritually.”

Mara paid while the old woman behind the counter watched them with the flat interest of someone who had seen every possible configuration of desire and foolishness come through her shop.

Outside, Eli said, “I’ll pay you back.”

“No, you won’t.”

“I absolutely will.”

“You still owe me for the wine you said was communal but turned out to belong to the choreographer.”

“That was a misunderstanding of labels.”

They ate one nectarine each before getting back on the bicycles. Juice ran down Mara’s wrist. Eli looked at it and then away.

The beach they had chosen was not the near one, crowded with families and plastic umbrellas, but a cove forty minutes away, recommended by Nina the ceramicist, who had described it as “beautiful, if you survive the descent.” They left the bicycles chained to a road sign and followed a path down through scrub and rockrose. Cicadas screamed invisibly around them. Halfway down, Eli slipped on loose gravel and sat down abruptly.

Mara tried not to laugh. “Are you injured?”

“Only narratively.”

“Can you walk?”

“I may need to be carried.”

“By whom?”

He looked up at her, squinting against the light. “A kind stranger. Strong. Emotionally available.”

She laughed then, and he smiled in that quick private way of his, the way that always made her feel she had accidentally opened a door.

The cove was almost empty. Two teenagers slept under a striped towel on the far side, and an elderly man in a straw hat stood waist-deep in the water like a saint awaiting instructions. The sea was blue in several languages. It darkened near the rocks, brightened over sand, flashed silver where the wind touched it.

They claimed a flat place beneath a cliff. Mara spread her towel. Eli emptied the tote with ceremony: bread, fruit, biscuits, one of Mara’s sketchbooks that he had apparently picked up by mistake, and a tube of ultramarine paint.

“Why do you have that?” she asked.

“I thought it was sunscreen.”

“It says ultramarine.”

“In my defense, that sounds protective.”

Mara put the paint into her bag. “You’re a danger to yourself and others.”

“I’ve been told.”

He said it lightly, but something in his tone made her glance at him. He was looking out at the water, his hair lifting in the wind. Over the past month she had learned the shapes his silences took. There was the silence of composition, when a sentence formed behind his eyes. The silence of irritation, thin and cool. The silence after someone at dinner asked too casually whether his book had sold well. And another silence, more difficult, which arrived whenever the future came near.

She took off her dress and felt instantly self-conscious in her black swimsuit. Eli busied himself with opening the biscuits.

“You’re not swimming?” she asked.

“I’m building suspense.”

The water was cold enough to erase thought. Mara waded in up to her thighs, then her waist, gasping. The elderly man nodded at her solemnly, as if she had passed an exam. Behind her, Eli approached in stages, making exaggerated faces.

“This is unreasonable,” he called.

“It’s the sea. File a complaint.”

He dove all at once, badly, with a splash that made the teenagers stir. When he surfaced, he looked shocked and younger.

They swam out to where the bottom vanished. Mara floated on her back, ears submerged, sky trembling above her. Underwater, the world was all muffled pulse. When she stood upright again, Eli was near enough that she could see the drops of water clinging to his eyelashes.

“I finished a chapter last night,” he said.

“That’s good.”

“It’s terrible.”

“That’s your process.”

“My process is despair interrupted by coffee.”

“Then it’s going beautifully.”

He smiled, but it fell away. “My editor wants the whole thing by October.”

“And will you give it to her?”

“I don’t know.” He kicked slowly, keeping himself afloat. “I keep writing people into rooms and then not letting them leave.”

“Maybe they don’t want to.”

“Everyone wants to leave eventually.”

Mara looked toward the shore. Their towels lay bright and temporary on the sand. “Do they?”

He didn’t answer. A wave lifted them both and set them down again.

Later, on the beach, they ate bread torn with sandy fingers. Eli read aloud the ridiculous fortunes printed on the animal biscuits, which turned out not to be fortunes at all but nutritional information in three languages. Mara sketched the cliff edge, though she kept glancing at him when he wasn’t looking. He had a bruise purpling on his hip from the fall. She wanted to touch it. She wanted, with equal strength, to pack her things and leave before wanting became humiliating.

By noon the heat pressed down heavily. The teenagers left. The old man disappeared without either of them noticing. A dog arrived alone, shook seawater over Eli’s notebook, stole half the bread, and escaped up the path.

Eli chased it for twelve futile steps before stopping, hands on hips. “That dog has no respect for literature.”

“It knows what it likes.”

He examined the notebook pages, holding them by the corners. Blue ink bled across his handwriting. “Maybe it improved them.”

Mara looked at the ruined pages. “I’m sorry.”

He shrugged. “I remember most of it.”

But she knew this was untrue, or not entirely true. She knew the particular misery of losing work—not the work itself, exactly, but the version of yourself who had made it. He sat down beside her, quieter now, and laid the notebook in the sun.

“Maybe this is a sign,” he said.

“What kind?”

“That I should become a person who sells umbrellas. Or repairs boats. Something with clear outcomes.”

“You’d be terrible at clear outcomes.”

“Probably.”

A gull cried overhead. Mara shaded her eyes. “After Monday, where do you go?”

“Paris for two nights. Then Berlin. Then maybe Prague, if my sublet hasn’t fallen through. Then home for my mother’s surgery.” He scratched at a drying line of salt on his arm. “You?”

“New York. Then upstate to see my sister. Then I don’t know.”

“You never say ‘home.’”

She watched a line of foam unmake itself on the sand. “Neither do you.”

“That’s because I’m mysterious.”

“That’s because you’re evasive.”

He laughed once. “And you’re not?”

“I am private. It’s more elegant.”

“Ah.”

The small word irritated her. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“No, what?”

He leaned back on his elbows and looked at her. “Sometimes I think you use leaving as proof that nothing matters.”

Mara felt heat rise into her face. “That’s rich.”

“It’s not an accusation.”

“It has the shape of one.”

“Fine.” He sat up. “Maybe I mean I don’t know what I’m supposed to do with you leaving.”

“With me leaving?” She laughed, too sharply. “We’re both leaving. That was always the arrangement.”

“I know.”

“We’re here for six weeks. We make work. We drink cheap wine. We have overly serious conversations in courtyards. Then we go.”

“I know.”

“So what did you expect?”

He looked down at his hands, spread open on his knees. “I don’t know.”

The wind moved over them. Mara heard, far above, a car passing on the coastal road. She wished suddenly for the others—the choreographer, Nina, the composer with his three eternal notes—to arrive noisily and save them from the precision of this moment.

Instead there was only Eli, wet-haired and sunburning, and the sea dragging itself repeatedly to shore.

“I don’t want to be another residency anecdote,” he said at last.

She tried to answer lightly. “You’re much too clumsy. You’ll be a residency legend.”

He did not smile.

Mara looked away first. It felt like losing, though she did not know what contest they were in.

For a while they said nothing. Eli rescued his notebook pages from the sun and weighted them with stones. Mara pretended to draw, pressing so hard the pencil tore the paper. The afternoon gathered and loosened. Families arrived on the far side of the cove, spreading towels, calling children by names that rang brightly off the cliff.

When the sky began to soften, Eli stood. “We should go before the path gets dark.”

“Yes,” Mara said.

They packed badly. Sand infiltrated everything. Mara couldn’t find one sandal until Eli discovered it under the towel, occupied by a crab the size of a coin. They both screamed, then laughed with disproportionate relief. The laughter did what apologies could not. It made them young again for a minute, foolish and forgiven.

The climb back was worse than the descent. Eli carried the tote; Mara carried the wet towels. Near the top, they stopped to catch their breath. Below them, the cove had become a blue bowl holding small, moving figures. The sun hovered above the water, swollen and orange.

Mara said, “I don’t think nothing matters.”

Eli turned toward her.

She kept her eyes on the sea. “I think too many things matter. That’s the problem.”

He was quiet. Then he said, “Yes.”

It was not an answer, but it was close enough.

At the road, they found that one bicycle had a flat tire.

“Of course,” Mara said.

Eli crouched beside it. “Maybe if we believe in it.”

“It’s dead.”

“We could ride double.”

“On the bicycle with no brake?”

He stood. “Adventure.”

“Obituary.”

So they walked, pushing the bicycles beside them as evening cooled the road. Their shoulders occasionally touched. Neither moved away. Cars passed with small gusts of warm air. The sky turned lavender, then blue, then the deep transparent color just before dark. Lights appeared in the village and along the harbor. Somewhere someone was grilling fish.

At the market, the old woman was pulling down the metal shutter. She saw Eli pushing the wounded bicycle and said something in the local dialect that made Mara laugh though she understood only the tone.

“She says I look like a man defeated by simple machines,” Mara translated.

“You don’t speak the dialect.”

“I speak old women.”

He bought two beers this time with coins found in the bottom of the tote, exact to the cent. They drank them sitting on the harbor wall with their feet above the rocks. Fishing boats knocked gently against their ropes. The moon rose small and late.

“I’m sorry about earlier,” Eli said.

Mara picked at the label on her bottle. “Me too.”

“For which part?”

“For whichever part was mine.”

He nodded. “That seems fair.”

A group of residents passed on the road behind them, returning from dinner, their voices lifted with wine. Someone called their names. Eli raised his beer but did not stand. Mara felt the fragile privacy of the day closing around them again.

“When you’re in Berlin,” she said, “will you finish the book?”

“Probably. Eventually. Badly.”

“Send me pages.”

He looked at her.

“If you want,” she added.

“I want.”

The harbor water slapped softly against stone.

“And you?” he asked. “Will you paint the sea after all?”

“No.”

“No?”

“I think I’ve been painting around it.”

“That sounds like you.”

“It does.”

He smiled. “Send me pictures of what you don’t paint.”

She laughed quietly. “All right.”

They sat until the beers were empty and the mosquitoes found them. Then they resumed the walk back to the residency, slower now, the broken bicycle ticking beside them like a small mechanical insect.

The convent glowed at the end of the road, windows lit in uneven squares. From the open kitchen came the sound of dishes, laughter, someone arguing about grant applications. Ordinary life, already continuing without them.

At the foot of the stairs, Eli stopped. Mara’s room was on the second floor, his on the third. The stairwell smelled of damp stone and basil from the pots in the courtyard. For weeks they had parted here with jokes, borrowed books, careless goodnights stretched slightly too long.

Now neither of them spoke.

Eli reached into the tote and pulled out her sketchbook, warped slightly from the day. “This is yours.”

She took it. Their fingers touched. It was nothing. It was the oldest thing in the world.

“Goodnight, Mara,” he said.

She wanted to ask him to come in. She wanted him to ask. She wanted to preserve the ache precisely as it was, unspoiled by fulfillment or refusal. She wanted Monday to be fictional.

Instead she said, “Goodnight.”

He climbed two steps, then turned back. “Today was—”

“I know,” she said.

He nodded, accepting this incomplete sentence as if it were generous. Then he went up, his footsteps fading.

Mara stood in the stairwell long after he disappeared. In her room, she rinsed salt from her skin and found sand in the bed, in her bag, between the pages of her sketchbook. On the last page, beneath a rough drawing of the cliff, Eli had written something while she wasn’t looking.

Not a sentence. Not even a proper note.

Just a line from the nutritional information on the animal biscuits, copied in his narrow, slanting hand:

May contain traces.

Mara sat on the edge of the bed and read it again. Then she laughed once, softly, and pressed the page closed.

Outside, the sea moved in the dark, arriving and leaving, arriving and leaving, as if it had never promised to stay.
