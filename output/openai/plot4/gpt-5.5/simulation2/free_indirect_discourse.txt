**The Last Low Tide**

On the morning of the last beach trip, the residency van refused to start.

It made a sound like an old man clearing his throat, then another sound like an apology, and then nothing. The six of them stood in the gravel lot behind the converted convent with towels over their shoulders and canvas bags at their feet, watching Niko, the program assistant, pump the gas pedal as if violence might resurrect the engine.

Mara had known, somehow, that the day would begin like this. Of course the van would fail. Of course their final outing, the promised communal farewell to the sea, would sag immediately under the weight of inconvenience. It had rained all night, a hard vertical rain that drummed the roof tiles and filled the courtyard with the smell of wet stone. Now the sky had cleared into a pale, slippery blue, but the gravel was still dark, the cypresses dripping, everything shining with the look of having narrowly survived.

Theo stood beside her, barefoot in his sneakers, his hair still damp from the shower. He held a paper cup of coffee in one hand and a straw hat in the other. He had bought the hat in town their second week, from a woman who insisted it was made by her uncle, though the tag inside said Vietnam. Since then he had worn it ironically and then, at some point, without irony, which was the way things happened with him.

“Maybe it’s a sign,” he said.

Mara did not ask of what. If she asked, he would say something theatrical: that the gods disapproved of artists at leisure, or that the sea had revoked its invitation. Or worse, he might say something simple and true, that perhaps they were not meant to go, and then she would be annoyed with him for pretending not to mean anything by it.

“It’s a van,” she said. “It has one job.”

He smiled into his coffee. “You’re very hard on objects.”

She was. She had always been. Objects should perform. Doors should shut, pens should write, zippers should not betray a person in public. People were more complicated, unfortunately, and could not be handled by warranty.

Niko got out of the van and announced that they would go by bus. There was a stop at the bottom of the hill, and if they hurried, they could catch the 10:12 to San Felice, and from there it was only a fifteen-minute walk to the beach.

Only fifteen minutes. This was said in the bright tone people used when they meant thirty.

They gathered their things. Mara had packed a towel, a notebook she would not write in, a dented bottle of sunscreen, two peaches from breakfast, and the blue swimsuit she had not yet decided whether to wear. It was the last full day of the residency. Tomorrow, everyone would scatter: Berlin, Toronto, Lisbon, São Paulo, New York. Mara would fly back to Chicago by way of Rome and resume her actual life, which had been waiting patiently like a dog tied outside a shop.

For four weeks she had lived in a room that used to belong to a novice nun. White walls, narrow bed, desk under the window, crucifix shadow still faint on the plaster where someone had taken it down. She had come to finish a series of essays about grief and architecture and had instead written pages and pages about weather, oranges, church bells, and Theo’s hands.

No, not about his hands. About a man washing paint from his hands at the outdoor tap. About gesture. About attention. About the intimacy of watching someone believe he was unobserved.

They walked down the hill in a loose group, sandals slapping, bags bumping hips. The road curved through olive groves. The sea appeared and disappeared between branches, a strip of hammered silver.

Theo fell into step beside her, though he had been walking ahead with Amara, the sculptor from Ghana, who laughed easily and touched people’s arms when she spoke. Mara had no reason to dislike Amara and did not dislike her. She disliked only the way Theo leaned toward her laughter, as if warmth were a climate he could enter at will.

“Did you pack the peaches?” Theo asked.

“Yes.”

“Both?”

“One was for you?”

“You said last night you didn’t like peaches.”

“I said these peaches were mealy.”

“And yet you packed them.”

“For emergencies.”

“Good. I intend to become an emergency around noon.”

There it was again, that lightness that could be flirtation or could be merely Theo being Theo, generous and careless with his brightness. In the first week, she had mistaken it for interest. In the second, she had decided interest was beside the point. In the third, after the evening in the laundry room, she had become less sure.

The laundry room had smelled of detergent and mildew. She had been trying to rescue her black dress from a machine that would not unlock. Theo had come in with a basket of damp sheets, and together they had pressed buttons, unplugged the machine, plugged it back in, cursed the manufacturer, and finally sat on the floor waiting for the cycle to end by some mysterious internal logic. It was nearly midnight. The fluorescent light buzzed above them. He had told her about his daughter then, not in the rehearsed way he spoke of her at dinner—seven years old, liked dinosaurs, lived with his ex in Montreal—but with sudden specificity: how she corrected his French, how she slept with one sock on, how he feared becoming a voice on the phone. Mara had wanted to touch his shoulder. She had not. When the machine clicked open at last, they both startled, as though caught.

After that, he had sometimes found her in the mornings. Coffee on the terrace. A walk to the bakery. Once, a cigarette he did not smoke, held between his fingers until it burned down to ash because he had forgotten the performance required maintenance. Nothing had happened. But nothing had become a country they occupied together, with its own weather, its own guarded borders.

At the bus stop, they learned the 10:12 had left at 10:07.

“It’s Italy,” Niko said, wounded. “That never happens.”

The next bus would come in forty minutes. Everyone groaned extravagantly. Mara sat on the low stone wall and took off her sunglasses to clean them on the hem of her shirt. The day was warming. Cicadas revved invisibly in the grass.

Theo sat beside her, close enough that their knees nearly touched, though there was space elsewhere on the wall. Nearly touched: the pathetic unit of measurement by which she had lately lived.

He tilted his hat over his eyes. “If we die here, tell them I was brave.”

“You were felled by public transportation.”

“A modern tragedy.”

Across the road, an old woman in a black dress watered geraniums with a green plastic can. The water flashed in the sun, scattering diamonds on the leaves. Mara wondered what the woman saw when she looked at them: foreign adults dressed like children, carrying towels, speaking too loudly, leaving soon. The residency must be used to this. Every month a new group arrived full of ambition and digestive issues, fell in love with the light or each other, then vanished.

What arrogance, to believe their particular vanishing mattered.

The bus, when it came, was already crowded. They climbed aboard and swayed in the aisle among market bags, teenagers, a man transporting a caged rabbit. Theo gave his seat to an elderly woman and ended up pressed behind Mara as the bus lurched through hairpin turns. His hand gripped the rail above her. His forearm hovered near her cheek. She could smell soap, coffee, and the mineral scent of skin in heat.

Ridiculous. She was forty-one years old. She had survived worse than proximity. She had survived her father’s long illness, her marriage’s polite collapse, two academic searches, a biopsy, a winter when she ate standing over the sink because sitting down felt too much like surrender. She could survive twenty minutes of Theo’s arm.

Then the bus braked suddenly, and his body knocked into hers. His free hand caught her waist.

“Sorry,” he said, low.

“It’s fine.”

But his hand remained for a second longer than accident required. Or did it? Accident had elastic rules. By the time she decided to register it, he had withdrawn.

San Felice was crowded with vacationers and vendors selling inflatable dolphins, striped umbrellas, fried dough twisted in paper cones. The walk to the beach took them through streets that smelled of sunscreen, basil, exhaust, and hot stone. Someone bought cherries. Someone else needed a bathroom. Niko lost the path and led them briefly into a marina, where yachts sat blank and white as teeth.

By the time they reached the public beach, all the best spots had been claimed. The sand was coarse and littered with bottle caps. A family had erected a fortress of umbrellas nearby, and children ran shrieking between towels, pursued by a grandmother with a spray bottle.

“Paradise,” Theo said.

Mara laughed despite herself.

They found a place near the rocks. Towels unfurled, bags dropped, shirts lifted over heads. Mara changed behind a towel held by Amara and emerged in the blue swimsuit, feeling both exposed and foolishly proud, as if she had built the body herself from difficult materials. Theo was looking out at the water when she turned around. Of course he was. Or perhaps he had looked away quickly. She was becoming a novelist of microscopic events, assigning motives to eyelashes.

The sea was colder than it looked. They entered it in stages, everyone making noises of complaint and ecstasy. Mara dove under before she could reconsider. The cold closed over her scalp, her ears, her eyes. For a moment there was only green light and the muffled churn of water. She surfaced gasping.

Theo was a few feet away, slicking his hair back. Without the hat he looked younger and more tired.

“You’re braver than I am,” he said.

“You followed me in.”

“Under protest.”

They swam out past the first line of swimmers, toward the buoys. The others remained nearer shore, splashing, calling. Mara floated on her back and watched gulls wheel overhead. The water lifted and lowered her. Her ears filled, emptied, filled.

If she said something now, the sea would take it. She could say: I wanted this month to be about work, and instead it became about waiting for you to knock on my door. She could say: last night when you sang badly in the kitchen, I almost kissed you over the sink. She could say: are you also pretending not to know?

Theo floated beside her. Their fingers brushed once under the water, an accidental grazing that struck through her like voltage. Neither of them moved closer. Neither moved away.

“Do you think you’ll come back?” he asked.

“To the residency?”

“To any of it.”

Any of it. The sea, the convent, the dinners under the pergola, the hot little rooms, the borrowed time. The version of herself who had existed here, sharpened by desire and idleness.

“No,” she said. “Probably not.”

He nodded. “Me neither.”

The sadness of this was disproportionate and therefore embarrassing. People were always leaving places. They had arranged their lives around leaving places. The whole structure of the residency was departure disguised as opportunity. Come here, be transformed, then vacate the room by ten.

A shout came from shore. Amara was waving both arms. At first Mara thought she was calling them back for lunch. Then Niko pointed frantically toward the rocks.

Theo squinted. “Is that my hat?”

It was. The straw hat, which he had apparently left too close to the waterline, was drifting beyond the rocks with stately independence.

“Oh no,” Mara said. “Your tragic hat.”

“It’s not tragic yet.”

He began swimming toward it.

“Theo, don’t be stupid.”

But he was already moving, strong and determined, and she followed because apparently stupidity was contagious. The hat bobbed near a cluster of dark rocks where waves broke in white bursts. It seemed always within reach and always just beyond. Theo lunged, missed, laughed, lunged again. A wave slapped him sideways.

“This is how men die,” Mara called.

“For fashion!”

At last he seized the brim, triumphant, just as his foot struck a submerged rock. His expression changed—surprise first, then pain.

Mara reached him. “What happened?”

“Nothing. Rock. I’m fine.”

Blood ribboned from his heel into the water.

“You’re not fine.”

“It’s very cinematic though.”

She put his arm over her shoulder, and they made their awkward way back, half-swimming, half-hopping through the shallows. On shore, everyone gathered around with the solemn excitement people reserve for minor injuries. Someone produced tissues. Someone else had antiseptic wipes. Niko, pale with responsibility, suggested a clinic.

“It’s a cut,” Theo said. “I’ve had worse.”

Mara knelt in the sand and held his foot steady while Amara poured bottled water over the wound. The cut was shallow but dramatic, bleeding more than it deserved. Theo winced and tried to joke, but Mara could feel the tension in his leg, the involuntary flinch each time the water hit.

“Hold still,” she said.

“Yes, doctor.”

“I mean it.”

He looked at her then, properly. The beach noise seemed to flatten around them—the children, gulls, vendors, waves—all pressed into a single bright surface. His foot was in her hands. Sand clung to his ankle. Such intimacy, absurdly obtained. A bleeding heel, a runaway hat. This was what the universe offered instead of clarity.

After the wound was cleaned and bandaged with a ridiculous amount of gauze, he was ordered to remain on the towels. The hat, damp and misshapen, sat beside him like a rescued animal.

Mara gave him one of the peaches.

“My emergency,” he said.

“It’s mealy.”

“So am I.”

They ate in silence. The peach was warm, bruised near the pit, juice running down Mara’s wrist. Theo watched the water. His bandaged foot rested on her towel because there had not been room, or because neither of them had moved it.

Around midafternoon, clouds gathered over the headland. At first no one took them seriously. The sky had been performing all day; why shouldn’t it add a little drama? But the wind shifted. Umbrellas began to flap. A paper plate skidded across the sand. Then rain came suddenly, fat drops that darkened towels and sent families into shrieking motion.

They packed badly. This was the way of wet departures: nothing folded, everything stuck with sand, someone always missing a shoe. Mara’s notebook got damp at the edges. Niko shouted that there was a café near the road. Theo tried to stand, grimaced, and pretended he had not.

“Lean on me,” Mara said.

“I can walk.”

“Yes, and I can fly. Lean.”

He obeyed. His weight settled against her side, warm and solid. They crossed the beach slowly while rain needled their backs. The others hurried ahead, bright towels over their heads, laughing. Mara and Theo lagged behind like survivors of a small war. His arm was around her shoulders. Her hand circled his waist. There was no way to make this ironic.

By the time they reached the café, they were soaked. The place was crowded with refugees from the storm, all steaming clothes and damp hair. They found two chairs under the awning while the rest squeezed inside to order coffees and beer. Rain sheeted off the striped canvas. The sea had turned pewter.

Theo removed his ruined hat and set it on the table. It had lost all shape.

“Now it’s tragic,” Mara said.

He smiled faintly. “It had a good run.”

For a while they watched the rain. His shoulder touched hers. The contact was continuous now, no longer accidental, and therefore harder to understand. Perhaps injury had absolved them. Perhaps weather had. Perhaps they were both simply tired.

“I leave at six tomorrow,” he said.

“I know.”

Of course she knew. She knew everyone’s departure times with the obsessive accuracy of a doomed air-traffic controller. Theo’s taxi was at six. Amara’s at nine. Hers at eleven-thirty. By noon the convent would be stripped of them. New sheets, new keys, new strangers arriving Monday with their hopes in wheeled luggage.

“My daughter starts school next week,” he said. “First grade.”

“That’s big.”

“Yeah.”

He turned the coffee cup in front of him, though it was empty. His fingers worried the rim. Those hands again. Paint still under one thumbnail, a stubborn green.

“I didn’t expect…” he began.

The rain filled the pause.

Mara waited, hating herself for waiting. Say it or don’t. Do not make her stand at the edge of the sentence forever.

He looked out toward the beach. “I didn’t expect to like being here so much.”

Coward. Or gentleman. Or merely a man saying the largest thing he could safely say. She felt an unreasonable tenderness toward him then, and an equally unreasonable desire to slap the empty cup from his hand.

“Me neither,” she said.

He laughed once, softly. “That’s not what I meant.”

No, it wasn’t. The words opened between them, small and bright, and immediately began to close.

Inside the café, someone called Mara’s name. Amara was waving a receipt, asking if she wanted an espresso. Mara shook her head. When she turned back, Theo was watching her with an expression too tired to defend itself.

“What did you mean?” she asked.

There. The sentence had been spoken. Not elegantly, not bravely, but spoken.

He looked down at the hat. Rain tapped the awning. A child cried somewhere inside. The whole world had become interruption, witness, excuse.

“I mean,” he said slowly, “that this month would have been easier if you were less—”

He stopped.

“Less what?”

“Less you.”

It was terrible. It was perfect. It explained nothing and admitted everything. She felt her face grow hot, though the rain had cooled the air.

“That’s a very residency thing to say,” she said.

“I know. I hate myself.”

“You should.”

They both laughed, quietly, with relief or grief. His knee pressed against hers under the table. She did not move.

What would happen if she kissed him? The awning would not collapse. The sea would not boil. The others might see, yes, and then there would be looks, speculation, a final dinner sharpened by gossip. Tomorrow he would leave at six regardless. A kiss would not revise his life in Montreal or hers in Chicago. It would become another object to carry home, fragile and inconvenient, wrapped badly in memory.

But not kissing him would be an object too.

The rain softened. People began to emerge from the café and test the sky. Niko announced that if they wanted the five o’clock bus, they had to leave in ten minutes. Of course. Time, having lounged all month like a cat in the sun, now sprang up and demanded obedience.

Theo stood carefully. Mara reached for him, but he balanced on his good foot and smiled.

“I can manage.”

“Yes,” she said. “You’re very heroic.”

They made their way to the bus stop in the damp afterglow of the storm. The road smelled of wet dust and crushed pine. Their group was subdued now, sunburned and tired, carrying bags heavier with seawater. Theo walked beside her, limping slightly. Once, when the path narrowed, his hand brushed hers. This time his fingers caught, held.

Only for a few steps.

Then the road widened, and he let go.

At the stop, the bus was late. Naturally. Everyone complained without conviction. The sky cleared in strips, revealing a low gold sun. Mara stood apart from the others near a wall covered in bougainvillea. Her towel dripped onto her sandals. In her bag, the notebook pages would be curling. Good. Let them curl. Let everything bear evidence.

Theo came to stand beside her.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“For the hat?”

“For being obscure.”

“You weren’t obscure.”

“No?”

“No.”

He nodded, accepting this. The bougainvillea petals were papery and impossibly bright, magenta against the wet wall. One had fallen onto his shoulder. She lifted it off before she could decide not to. Her fingers grazed the damp fabric of his shirt.

He looked at her, and for one suspended second the day gathered itself: the dead van, the missed bus, the crowded aisle, the cold sea, blood in the water, rain on canvas, his unfinished sentence, her ridiculous heart still insisting, even now, on the possibility of a door.

Then the bus appeared around the curve, exhaling smoke.

Of course it did.

On the ride back, they did not sit together. An old man occupied the last two adjacent seats with a basket of figs on his lap, and Mara ended up near the front beside Niko, who fell asleep immediately, mouth open. Theo stood in the aisle halfway back, one hand gripping the rail, the ruined hat dangling from his wrist. Through gaps between passengers, she could see him sometimes: profile, shoulder, hand. Not enough to make a story. Enough to make one anyway.

At the convent, the evening bells were ringing. Everyone dispersed to shower, nap, salvage wet belongings. The final dinner would be at eight. There would be speeches, wine, promises to visit, photographs in which they would look happier than they felt. Mara climbed the stairs to her room and closed the door.

Her suitcase lay open on the floor, half-packed, reproachful. She took off the blue swimsuit and hung it over the chair. Sand fell from the towel in a small gray heap. In the mirror, her shoulders were pink, her hair wild with salt. She looked like someone who had spent the day being foolish. She looked alive.

On the desk, her notebook had warped from the rain. She opened it carefully. The last page of notes had blurred, ink feathering into blue shadows. A sentence about church bells had dissolved completely. Another, written two mornings ago, remained legible at the top of the page: *The problem with temporary places is that they reveal the permanent self.*

How pompous. How possibly true.

A knock came at the door.

She stood very still. The knock came again, softer.

When she opened it, Theo was in the hallway, showered, hair damp, white shirt untucked, bandage fresh on his heel. He held the straw hat in both hands. It had been reshaped badly, its brim uneven, its crown dented.

“I thought you might want to say goodbye to it,” he said.

She looked at the hat. Then at him.

“That hat and I were never close.”

“No. But you were there at the end.”

The hallway smelled of soap and old plaster. From downstairs came the clatter of plates, someone laughing, a chair dragged across tile. Life continuing, vulgar and punctual.

Theo’s smile faded.

“I leave early,” he said.

“I know.”

“I didn’t want…” He stopped, shook his head. “I don’t know what I didn’t want.”

There was no time now to become different people. No time to be brave in a way that would not also be selfish. She understood this, and hated understanding it. Understanding had ruined many things in her life. It had never once kissed her in a hallway.

Mara stepped forward and put her arms around him.

For a moment he was surprised. Then he held her, carefully at first, then not carefully. His face turned against her hair. His body gave off the clean warmth of someone recently washed and still tired from the sea. She felt his hand at the center of her back, the other between her shoulder blades. No kiss. Not yet, not after, perhaps not ever. But the embrace lengthened past politeness, past comfort, into something with a pulse.

This, then. This was the thing they would have. Not nothing. Not enough. Something.

When they separated, he kept his hands lightly on her arms.

“Less me,” she said.

He smiled, and the sadness in it was plain. “Impossible.”

Downstairs, someone called that dinner was ready.

They walked together to the stairwell, not touching. At the top step, Theo paused.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “I’ll be terrible at goodbyes.”

“Good. I hate competent people.”

He laughed. Then he began the descent, limping slightly, carrying the ruined hat.

Mara followed a step behind. Through the tall window over the stairs, she could see the last of the sunset burning over the cypress trees, the sky washed clean after the storm. By morning the gravel would be dry. The rooms would empty. The sea would go on throwing itself at the shore, indifferent and exact.

For now, there was dinner. There was wine. There were speeches to endure and photographs to resist. There was the long table under the pergola, still damp from rain, where everyone would sit too close together because it was the last night and because leaving made people briefly generous.

Mara touched the wall as she went down, feeling the old stone cool beneath her palm. She would remember this, unfortunately. She would remember all of it: the mealy peach, the blood ribboning in green water, the hat like a small drowned animal, the almost and the not quite, his hand finding hers for only a few steps on the road after rain.

How little had happened.

How entirely it had happened.
