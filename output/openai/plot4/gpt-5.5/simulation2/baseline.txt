**The Last Tide**

On the morning of the final beach trip, Mara woke to the sound of a suitcase being dragged down the hallway.

The residency house was never quiet, not exactly. Even in the small hours there were floorboards complaining, kettles clicking off, someone’s door easing shut with the discretion of a secret. But luggage had a different sound: blunt, terminal. It struck each wooden seam like a countdown.

She lay still and listened. Two days left. Maybe three, if you counted the morning shuttle to the train station, though no one did. Departures began before anyone left. They began in the careful way people stopped buying groceries.

Downstairs, someone laughed too loudly.

Mara rolled onto her back and stared at the ceiling, where a crack branched like a river delta above her bed. She had meant to sketch it in her notebook weeks ago, when the house still seemed strange and abundant and everyone’s time there stretched ahead of them in generous folds. Instead she had drawn Eitan’s hands holding a chipped blue mug, Eitan’s shoulders hunched over a typewriter he insisted on using though the ribbon was nearly dead, Eitan asleep in the hammock with a book collapsed over his face.

Not that she would ever show him.

She dressed quickly: swimsuit under a linen shirt, shorts, sandals that were already surrendering at the straps. In the kitchen, the counter was crowded with the trip’s provisions: bread, peaches, cucumbers sweating in a plastic bag, a jar of olives, two bottles of warm white wine, and a watermelon so enormous it looked less like food than an assignment.

“Good, you’re awake,” said Nila, the residency coordinator, without looking up from her clipboard. “Can you help load?”

Mara took the watermelon.

Outside, the van sat idling in the gravel drive, its side panels dusty with pollen and salt. The residency house rose behind it in pale yellow stucco, shutters open like eyelids. Beyond the orchard, the sea was invisible but somehow present, a pressure in the air.

Eitan was tying a stack of straw mats to the roof rack with a seriousness that suggested he had once been betrayed by rope.

“You’re doing that wrong,” Mara said.

He glanced down, squinting against the sun. “Good morning to you too.”

“It’s going to come loose on the highway and decapitate a cyclist.”

“There are no cyclists on the highway.”

“Not after today.”

He smiled, but only briefly, and the smile did not quite reach the part of him she had learned to watch. The space between his eyebrows. The hinge of his jaw. Small weather signs.

They had been careful for weeks. That was the word Mara used privately: careful. They took walks to the old lighthouse but did not hold hands. They stayed up after the others went to bed, talking in the courtyard under the orange tree, but sat on opposite benches. If their knees touched under the dining table, one of them shifted away after a second too long.

Once, drunk on cheap red wine and the strangeness of being briefly freed from their real lives, he had brushed a crumb from the corner of her mouth with his thumb. Neither of them had mentioned it. Neither had forgotten.

The van filled slowly with residents, towels, hats, bags of food, someone’s portable speaker, someone else’s bad mood. There were nine of them left now: painters, poets, a composer, a translator of medieval court documents. A month ago there had been fifteen. Each departure had made the group both closer and less possible, like a room losing walls.

Mara ended up in the back row beside Eitan because Nila believed in “organic arrangements,” which mostly meant no one wanted the middle seat over the wheel well. As the van lurched down the drive, Eitan’s knee knocked hers.

“Sorry,” he said.

“It’s a van. It happens.”

“Still.”

She looked out the window. Rows of olive trees flashed past, their leaves silvering in the wind. On the radio, a man sang mournfully in a language Mara didn’t know. The air-conditioning worked only in theory, so all the windows were open, whipping hair into mouths, ferrying bits of conversation backward.

“Have you packed?” Eitan asked.

“Half.”

“That means no.”

“It means I’ve made piles.”

“Ah. The first stage of grief.”

“What about you?”

He tapped the backpack at his feet. “Everything I need.”

“For six weeks in Berlin?”

“For the next twelve hours.”

She turned to him then. “You really aren’t going back to New York first?”

He shook his head. “Flight from here tomorrow night. Then Berlin.”

“Then the fellowship.”

“Then the fellowship.”

“Then?”

He shrugged in a way she hated. Not because it was careless, but because it was practiced. “Then we’ll see.”

Mara wanted to say: I don’t believe in people who say that. She wanted to say: I know where I’ll be, and it is not where you’ll be, and I resent the geography of it. She wanted to say: ask me to visit.

Instead she said, “You’ll need more than one shirt.”

“I own two.”

“Romantic.”

He laughed, and for a moment the air between them cleared.

The first misadventure happened twenty minutes later, when the van made a coughing sound like an elderly animal and rolled to a stop beside a field of sunburned grass.

Nila got out and opened the hood with the confidence of someone who had never fixed an engine but was prepared to supervise its emotional recovery. Everyone climbed out after her, stretching and shading their eyes. The road was empty in both directions. Heat trembled above the asphalt.

“Maybe it’s out of gas,” said Tomas, the composer.

Nila gave him a look. “The tank is full.”

“Then it’s spiritually out of gas.”

Mara wandered to the roadside, where tiny yellow flowers grew through cracks in the ditch. Behind her, the others discussed engines with escalating ignorance. Eitan came to stand beside her, hands in his pockets.

“This feels like a metaphor,” he said.

“Everything feels like a metaphor to writers.”

“I’m not sure I’m a writer anymore.”

She glanced at him. “Since when?”

He picked a burr from his shorts. “Since I stopped writing.”

“You’ve been writing.”

“I’ve been producing pages. That’s different.”

Mara knew better than to reassure him too quickly. She had learned that his doubts were not locks requiring her key. They were rooms he sometimes invited her to sit in.

She nudged a pebble with her sandal. “At least you produce pages. I’ve spent three weeks painting the same window and making it worse.”

“That blue one?”

“You noticed?”

“I notice many things.”

There it was again: the small opening, the dark undercurrent. She could step into it. She could make it impossible to pretend later.

Behind them, the engine turned over and failed with theatrical finality.

“I also notice,” Mara said, “that we may die here before seeing the sea.”

He looked at her for a second, then smiled. “There are worse places.”

She wanted to ask, Worse than with me? But that was the kind of sentence that turned air into weather.

Eventually a farmer appeared in a pickup and spoke to Nila in rapid local dialect, then to the engine, then to the sky. He found a loose connection, tightened it with a tool produced from nowhere, refused money, accepted two peaches, and drove away. The residents cheered as if a war had ended.

By the time they reached the beach, the sun was high and the parking lot was full. The “secret cove” Nila had promised turned out to be popular with families, teenagers, elderly couples under striped umbrellas, and one dog determined to dig through the planet.

“It was secret in May,” Nila said defensively.

They carried everything down a path between wild rosemary and scrub pine. The sea appeared in pieces: first a flash of blue through branches, then a sheet of light, then the whole bright, impossible expanse. Mara felt it in her chest. No matter how many times she had seen the ocean, it retained the force of arrival.

They claimed a patch of sand near the rocks. Towels unfurled. Sunscreen circulated. The watermelon was placed in the shade like a relic. Someone opened the first bottle of wine and discovered there was no corkscrew.

“We are artists,” Tomas said. “We improvise.”

They attempted a shoe, a key, a penknife, and a method involving a stick that nearly resulted in tragedy. Finally Eitan pushed the cork into the bottle with the handle of a paintbrush from Mara’s bag.

“That was my good brush,” she said.

“It has entered a new phase of its career.”

The wine came out flecked with cork and tasting faintly of wood varnish. They drank it anyway from paper cups that collapsed at the rim.

Mara went swimming alone.

The water was cold enough to erase thought. She plunged under and came up gasping, hair slicked back, skin alive. Around her, children shrieked in three languages. Farther out, beyond the rope line, a motorboat dragged a red inflatable raft in reckless circles.

She floated on her back and let the sky occupy her. For the first time in days, she felt free of the small humiliations of wanting. The sea held her without asking what came next.

When she returned to shore, Eitan was sitting on her towel, reading the paperback she had brought and abandoned.

“That’s mine,” she said, dripping over him.

“I’m aware.”

“You hate that writer.”

“I’m reevaluating.”

“You’re sitting on my towel.”

“I’m guarding it.”

“From whom?”

He looked past her. “The dog.”

The dog in question was asleep under a stranger’s umbrella.

Mara wrung out her hair. “You didn’t swim.”

“I will.”

“You always say that.”

“I require the right moment.”

“You mean you’re afraid of cold water.”

“I am respectful of cold water.”

She sat beside him on the sand, close but not touching. He kept reading, or pretending to. A drop from her hair landed on his forearm. He didn’t move.

For lunch they ate bread torn by hand, tomatoes salted with seawater because someone had forgotten the salt, cucumbers cut unevenly with a borrowed knife, olives that rolled into the sand and were still deemed edible. The watermelon split badly under Tomas’s enthusiastic rock assault, collapsing into sweet red ruin. Everyone ate with sticky fingers, juice running to their wrists.

Afterward, heat and wine softened the group into silence. Nila slept under a hat. The translator read medieval court documents on her phone. Two painters argued lazily about whether yellow could be tragic.

Mara and Eitan walked to the tide pools.

They told no one they were going; they simply stood at the same time and drifted toward the rocks, as if drawn by separate but compatible forces. The sand grew hot underfoot. Small crabs vanished into crevices. The beach noise thinned behind them.

At the far end of the cove, the rocks formed shallow basins where minnows flickered like thoughts. Mara crouched beside one and watched a tiny anemone open and close its red fringe.

“When do you leave?” Eitan asked.

“Day after tomorrow. Morning train to Paris, flight to Chicago.”

“You’re going back to the school?”

“In September.”

“And before that?”

“My sister’s having a baby. I promised I’d help.”

“You’ll be good at that.”

“What, babies?”

“Helping.”

She laughed softly. “That sounds like a diagnosis.”

He stepped across a wet rock and nearly slipped. Mara grabbed his wrist. For a second his weight came toward her, warm and startled. They steadied together, their bodies close enough that she could see the flecks of gold in his brown irises.

“You saved my life,” he said.

“I saved you from an embarrassing ankle injury.”

“I’ll dedicate the next book to you.”

“You said you weren’t writing.”

“I’ll dedicate my silence.”

His wrist remained in her hand. Neither of them looked down.

The tide breathed against the rocks. Behind them, someone’s radio thumped faintly. Mara could feel the pulse under his skin, quick and ordinary and unbearable.

Eitan said her name.

Not dramatically. Not like a confession. Just her name, as if checking whether she was still there.

She let go.

A gull screamed overhead, vulgar and perfectly timed.

“We should go back,” Mara said.

He nodded. “Before they eat all the sand olives.”

On the return, they found the path blocked by a family arranging a photograph on the rocks: grandparents, parents, children, all squinting into the sun while the father shouted instructions no one followed. Mara and Eitan waited, then took an alternate route over a flat shelf slick with algae.

This was the second misadventure.

Mara slipped first. Her foot shot forward, her arms windmilled, and she sat down hard in a tide pool with a splash that soaked her shorts and dignity. Eitan tried to help, began laughing, apologized, then slipped himself and landed beside her with less grace and more profanity.

For a moment they sat in six inches of water, stunned.

Then Mara started laughing. Not politely, not attractively, but helplessly, from the gut. Eitan laughed too, his head thrown back, knees bent, one hand braced in the pool. The sound of it broke something in her—not painfully, but like a fever breaking. She laughed until her ribs hurt.

A small boy from the photographing family pointed at them.

“Are you okay?” he called.

“No,” Eitan called back solemnly. “We have become marine life.”

The boy considered this, then nodded.

They returned to the towels wet, scraped, and bright with embarrassment. The others applauded. Nila insisted on disinfecting Mara’s knee with something that smelled like industrial regret. Eitan submitted his elbow for treatment and made a face.

“Brave,” Mara said.

“I’m very close to death.”

“You’re very close to needing a bandage with cartoon bears.”

“I hope they have bears.”

They did not have bears. They had plain gauze and tape that immediately collected sand.

In the late afternoon, clouds gathered beyond the headland. At first no one believed in them. The sky above remained blue, the water glittering. But the wind shifted. Umbrellas began to turn inside out. A child’s inflatable dolphin escaped and tumbled down the beach with three adults in pursuit.

“We should think about leaving,” Nila said.

Artists, as a group, were bad at leaving. They collected cups, lost sandals, shook towels too near other people’s faces. The portable speaker disappeared and was found inside the watermelon bag. The corked wine bottle spilled in the sand. Someone realized they had not taken a group photograph and insisted on doing so at once, though half the group was damp, sunburned, or visibly done with memory.

They lined up with the sea behind them. Mara stood at one end, Eitan at the other. At the last second, Tomas said, “No, no, you two switch, the light is weird,” and somehow they ended up side by side.

Nila set the timer and ran into frame. Everyone smiled.

As the camera blinked, Eitan’s hand brushed Mara’s. Not even a touch, really. A question in passing. She did not move away. Their little fingers hooked for the briefest instant, hidden between their bodies, while everyone said cheese in different accents.

Then the camera clicked, and the moment vanished into the general evidence that they had been there.

The rain began on the drive back.

Not rain, at first, but fat exploratory drops that darkened the windshield and evaporated. Then a sudden, biblical sheet. The van’s wipers squealed in protest. The open windows were slammed shut too late, leaving everyone damp and laughing and irritated. The smell of wet towels, sunscreen, wine, and human fatigue thickened the air.

Mara sat beside Eitan again. Outside, the landscape blurred into green and gray. Thunder rolled somewhere inland.

The others grew quiet one by one. Nila drove with fierce concentration. Tomas fell asleep with his mouth open. The translator rested her forehead against the glass.

In the dimness of the back row, Eitan took Mara’s hand.

This time there was nothing accidental about it.

His palm was warm, slightly rough from the rocks. He did not look at her. She did not look at him. Their hands rested between them, concealed by a damp towel and the gathering dusk, while the van climbed the road toward the residency house.

Mara understood then that neither of them would say what this was. To say it would be to make it smaller, or larger, or responsible for itself. It had lived in the temporary country of the residency, under permissions granted by distance and ending. It could not survive customs.

Still, she turned her hand and interlaced their fingers.

At the house, everyone scattered into the rain with bags over their heads. The mats on the roof rack had survived. The watermelon rind had not. Sand poured from everything. In the kitchen, they deposited the remains of the day: half a loaf, three peaches, wet napkins, an empty jar of olives. The residency dog, who belonged to no one and everyone, sniffed the offerings and looked disappointed.

People went off to shower. Doors closed. Pipes groaned in the walls.

Mara stood at the sink rinsing sand from her sandals. Eitan came in carrying the ruined paintbrush.

“I washed it,” he said.

She took it. The bristles were stained faintly red from wine. “It’s destroyed.”

“It has character.”

“It has a drinking problem.”

He leaned against the counter. His hair was still damp from rain, curling at the temples. There was a strip of gauze on his elbow. He looked younger than he had that morning, or simply less defended.

“Mara,” he said.

She set the brush down.

The kitchen light hummed overhead. From upstairs came the muffled thud of a suitcase being closed.

He looked at her, and she saw the sentence arrive and fail in him. Maybe he saw the same in her. The wanting was there, plain now, no longer clever enough to disguise itself as banter. But so was the train schedule, the flight, Berlin, Chicago, the lives that had loaned them to this place and were now calling them back.

She could have crossed the kitchen. He would not have stopped her. That was the terrible part.

Instead she picked up a peach from the counter, the last unbruised one, and handed it to him.

“For the road,” she said.

He looked at the peach in his palm. Then he laughed once, quietly—not because it was funny, but because it was what they had.

“Thank you.”

“Don’t pack it at the bottom.”

“I’m not an amateur.”

“No. You own two shirts.”

He tucked the peach into his pocket with exaggerated care.

They stood there another moment, close enough to alter the air around each other.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“Tomorrow.”

But tomorrow was already crowded: final readings, studio cleanouts, addresses exchanged with people they would not write to, polite promises, the awkward choreography of endings. Tomorrow would not belong to them.

Eitan seemed to know this. He opened his mouth, closed it, then stepped forward and kissed her cheek.

It was not enough. It was almost too much.

His lips were warm and brief against her skin. When he pulled back, he did not apologize.

“Goodnight,” he said.

“Goodnight.”

Mara watched him leave the kitchen and climb the stairs. Halfway up, he paused as if he might turn around. He didn’t.

Later, in her room, she packed in earnest. Clothes in one pile, books in another, sketches between cardboard sheets. She found the drawing of Eitan’s hands holding the blue mug and considered tearing it out. Instead she placed it at the bottom of her suitcase, under everything.

Rain tapped the shutters. Somewhere down the hall, someone was crying softly or laughing into a pillow; in the residency house, the two sounds were often indistinguishable.

Mara sat on the bed and touched the place on her cheek where he had kissed her. Already it was only skin.

In the morning, the sea would still be there beyond the orchard, indifferent and shining. The beach would hold no trace of them: no footprint, no spilled wine, no hidden clasp of fingers in a group photograph. The tide was efficient that way.

But for now, in the brief hour before sleep, she let the day remain whole. The stalled van, the cork in the wine, the cold water, the algae-slick rocks, their laughter in the tide pool, his hand finding hers in the rain.

A month ago, she had arrived with two suitcases and a plan for her work. She would leave with fewer certainties and a ruined brush.

Also this: a small, unfinished thing that had not become a story, or a love, or a mistake.

Not yet, anyway.

Or maybe never.

Outside her window, the storm moved out over the dark sea. Mara turned off the light and listened until she could no longer tell whether what she heard was rain, or leaves, or the last tide withdrawing from the shore.
