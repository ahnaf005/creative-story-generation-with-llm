**Low Tide Inventory**

By the time Mara found the rental car in the gravel lot behind the residency house, Julian had already managed to offend the GPS.

“It keeps insisting the ocean is north,” he said, holding his phone at arm’s length, as if the device might be made reasonable by distance.

“The ocean is north.”

“Yes, but not emotionally.”

He was leaning against the driver’s side door in yesterday’s linen shirt, sleeves rolled to the elbows, hair still damp from a shower he must have taken while she was searching the communal kitchen for the cooler. He looked irritatingly awake. The others had left notes—gone to the studio, gone to town, gone to revise my life choices—and the big house had the sour, hollow air of a party after the music stopped.

Their last beach trip. How ceremonial that sounded, how childish. As if the residency were a summer camp and not six weeks of adults pretending their work mattered enough to excuse their behavior.

Mara lifted the cooler into the back seat. “You’re driving.”

“I thought you were driving.”

“I drove last time.”

“You nearly killed a cyclist.”

“He emerged from fog like a symbol.”

Julian smiled, and there it was, that small tilting of the day toward him. She had spent weeks developing immunity to it and had failed in private, repeatedly. It was probably chemical. It had to do with sleep deprivation, shared wine, proximity to someone else’s sentences. Nothing more serious than weather.

He opened the passenger door for her with exaggerated gallantry. “Fine. I’ll drive us north, against my emotional convictions.”

They had been at the coastal residency since early June: twelve artists in a once-grand house smelling of eucalyptus, mildew, and old grant money. Mara had come to finish a collection of essays about disappearance. Julian was translating a dead poet whose work, he claimed, resisted him with admirable hostility. They had become friends first by accident, then by schedule. Morning coffee, late kitchen sightings, walks to the post office neither of them needed. Friends, yes. Everybody used the word easily. The composer from Berlin had called them “the inseparables,” which made Mara want to separate immediately, dramatically, perhaps by sea.

Now there were three days left. On Monday she would fly to Chicago, to her sublet with its unreliable radiator and the editor who had begun writing emails in the tone of a disappointed aunt. Julian would go to Lisbon for two months, then somewhere else after that, always somehow en route. He had no permanent address; he turned this into a charm. Mara suspected permanence had rejected him first.

The car smelled faintly of hot plastic and French fries. Julian adjusted the seat, the mirrors, the radio, then somehow turned on the windshield wipers, which squealed dryly across the glass.

“Good start,” Mara said.

He ignored her and backed out with grave concentration.

The morning had that washed-clean brightness the coast produced after fog. Pine trees leaned toward the road. Beyond them, flashes of water appeared and vanished like a secret being badly kept. Mara held the paper map on her lap because she had insisted on bringing it, a gesture she now regretted. Maps implied competence. Hers was upside down.

“Do you know where we’re going?” Julian asked.

“Of course.”

“You’re holding Portugal.”

She rotated the map. “I was appreciating scale.”

He laughed, not loudly. He never laughed loudly. This was one of the things people mistook for depth. Or perhaps it was depth, she didn’t know. He could be very quiet and then say something so precise it felt less like observation than trespass. On their third night, when everyone had been drunk on the porch and boasting about failure, he had said to her, You always make a joke right before you tell the truth. She had hated him for a full hour.

They were going to a beach someone at the residency had recommended, a place past the municipal beaches and their kettle-chip umbrellas, past the seafood shacks and souvenir stores. “You have to climb down,” the photographer had said, “but it’s worth it.” Everything worth it seemed to require a descent.

For twenty minutes they followed the GPS, which led them through a subdivision of identical beige houses and then into the parking lot of a veterinary clinic.

“You have arrived,” Julian’s phone announced.

A woman in scrubs watched them from the entrance while walking a dachshund in a cone.

“This can’t be right,” Mara said.

“Maybe the beach is inside the dog.”

He parked under a pepper tree and leaned over to look at the phone. His shoulder brushed hers. Not even a brush; a suggestion. She sat very still, hating the body for being such a loud, provincial instrument. It recorded everything. Skin, warmth, the citrus soap he used, the frayed seam at his cuff.

“Wrong Arroyo Lane,” he said.

“There are two?”

“There are apparently seven. Humanity was a mistake.”

The dachshund stared at them with wet judicial eyes.

They recalibrated. They drove back the way they had come, past a fruit stand where Julian abruptly decided they needed peaches. He turned too sharply into the dirt pullout, sending the cooler sliding across the back seat with a thud.

“Jesus.”

“Fruit,” he said, as if invoking a sacred category.

The stand was unattended except for a boy of about twelve reading a graphic novel beneath an umbrella. There were peaches, apricots, strawberries in green baskets, jars of honey with handwritten labels. Mara chose three peaches, then put one back. Why was she counting fruit as if the afternoon had a fixed arithmetic? Two people, two peaches. Sensible. Modest. Not lush.

Julian bought six.

“We’re not overwintering,” she said.

“We might be delayed by plot.”

“By your driving, certainly.”

The boy took Julian’s cash and gave him change without looking up. Back at the car, Julian rinsed a peach with water from his bottle and handed it to her. She bit into it. It was warm from the sun, overripe, collapsing immediately, juice running down her wrist.

“Oh no,” she said.

“What?”

“It’s perfect.”

He watched her with an expression that was almost too attentive, then looked away toward the road. Good. Yes. Look away. They had become skilled at these small rescues.

The correct Arroyo Lane was forty minutes north, after a one-lane bridge and a sign warning of falling rock. They parked beside two other cars in a dirt clearing above the cliffs. The sea spread below them, bright and hard, throwing back the sun. A path led down through ice plant and scrub, narrower than promised.

Julian took the cooler. Mara carried towels, her bag, and the paper map, which she folded and shoved into a pocket for no reason except loyalty. The path descended in switchbacks. Dust lifted around their shoes. Halfway down, Julian slipped, recovered, then pretended he had meant to perform a brief experimental dance.

“Convincing,” Mara said.

“I studied movement in Berlin.”

“You told me you were mugged in Berlin.”

“It involved movement.”

She laughed and then, immediately, felt the approaching end of laughter, the drop after it. That had been happening all week. Every pleasure came edged with inventory: last breakfast with him in the kitchen, last argument about whether soup could be breakfast, last time he would stand in her studio doorway and say nothing until she asked what he wanted and he said, To see if you were winning.

At the bottom, the beach was smaller than it had looked from above, a crescent of pale sand held between dark rocks. There were tide pools near the northern end and a family far down by the water with a red umbrella. Otherwise it was empty. Worth it, then. Of course. The world kept making beauty available at inconvenient times.

They set their towels near a boulder striped with mineral veins. Julian opened the cooler: bread, cheese, olives, a jar of mustard, seltzers, the excessive peaches.

“No knife,” Mara said.

Julian stared into the cooler. “I brought a corkscrew.”

“We don’t have wine.”

“In my defense, I was prepared for a different emergency.”

They tore the bread. They broke the cheese into uneven chunks with their fingers. Mustard was applied with the handle of a spoon, then with the spoon itself, then abandoned as too ambitious. Sand entered everything. An olive rolled down Mara’s thigh and disappeared beneath the towel. Julian tried to catch it and knocked over a seltzer, which foamed into his lap.

“This is elegant,” he said.

“It’s very French.”

“The French have knives.”

“The French have lower expectations.”

They ate anyway. The bread was tough, the cheese sweating oil, the peaches bruising in their paper bag. Above them gulls hung in the air, making occasional remarks.

After lunch, Julian went to investigate the tide pools. Mara remained on the towel, knees drawn up, watching him crouch among the rocks. He had a long, careful way of moving when he forgot himself. In company, he could be loose, performative, all slanted wit and disappearing cigarettes. Alone—or thinking he was alone—he became deliberate. He touched nothing at first. Then he leaned over a pool and pointed, though no one was beside him to see.

She should go over. Or not. It was pleasant here, sun on her shins, wind worrying at her hair. She could be a person who stayed where she was. She could be someone with boundaries, a shoreline, municipal regulation. But then he turned and called, “Mara, come see this,” and there went sovereignty.

The tide pools held miniature weather: anemones green as bottled glass, hermit crabs dragging borrowed houses, tiny fish flickering like thoughts. Julian pointed to a starfish tucked beneath a ledge.

“Do we still call them starfish?” he asked.

“Sea stars.”

“Of course. We corrected the stars.”

She crouched beside him. The rock was slick. Their shoulders nearly touched again, and this time neither moved. Wind, water, the family’s distant shrieks, a gull complaining overhead. All of it seemed arranged to cover silence, and yet silence pressed up through everything.

He said, “Are you ready to go back?”

“To the house?”

“To your life.”

Her life. As if it were a coat checked at the door, waiting to be reclaimed, sleeves cold from disuse. “Not especially.”

“No?”

“I haven’t finished what I came here to finish.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

There he was, trespassing again. She picked at a bit of seaweed on the rock. “Are you?”

“Ready?”

“To keep fleeing under the name of travel.”

He smiled faintly, but it didn’t hold. “Not especially.”

Well, then. There they were, two citizens of Not Especially, crouched over marine invertebrates.

A wave struck the outer rocks and sent spray over them. Mara flinched; Julian put a hand out, not quite on her back, just there in the air behind her, useless and devastating. If he touched her, things would simplify horribly. If he didn’t, they would continue like this, elaborately unsimple, which might be worse.

She stood too quickly. Her sandal slipped. For one suspended second she understood exactly how foolish she would look falling into a tide pool in front of the man she was not in love with. Julian caught her by the forearm.

“Careful.”

“I’m fine.”

“You’re bleeding.”

A thin line of red had opened on her ankle where the rock had scraped her. It was nothing. A scratch. Naturally it felt symbolic, because she was a writer and therefore professionally unwell.

Back at the towels, he rummaged in his bag and produced a packet of tissues, a receipt, a pen cap, and finally a bandage folded inside a hotel sewing kit.

“You carry bandages in sewing kits?”

“I contain multitudes, many of them stolen from hotels.”

He knelt to clean the cut with water. This was excessive. This was nothing. His fingers were cool around her ankle, his head bent in concentration. She looked out at the water so he would not see her face rearrange itself into something honest. Ridiculous, to be moved by first aid. Ridiculous, to want his hand to remain there after the bandage was smoothed down.

“There,” he said.

“Am I going to live?”

“I would never make medical guarantees.”

He sat back on his heels. For a moment he did not move away. The sun had shifted; his face was half shadow. He looked as if he might say something, and Mara braced herself for any number of disasters: I’ll miss you. Come to Lisbon. This has been lovely. You’re my dearest friend. Each one equally impossible.

Instead the red umbrella family began shouting. Their beach ball had escaped and was bounding down the sand toward the rocks, pursued by a child with no sense of mortality. Julian stood, grateful perhaps for the interruption, and sprinted after it. He reached the ball just before it hit the tide pools, scooped it up, then bowed to the child, who accepted it with royal indifference.

Mara watched him jog back, breathing hard, hair windblown. Misadventure suited him. Or maybe she had decided to make everything suit him because departure required evidence.

In the late afternoon the fog began gathering offshore, a gray wall advancing with bureaucratic patience. The air cooled. They swam anyway, because it was the last beach trip and because neither wanted to be first to suggest leaving.

The water was brutal. Mara shrieked despite herself; Julian laughed once, loudly this time, shocked into it. They dove under together and came up gasping, hair in their eyes, teeth flashing with cold. For a few minutes they were only bodies in water, no residency, no Monday, no rooms to empty or drafts to salvage. A wave lifted her; another spun him sideways. He reached for her hand, or she reached for his—later she could not honestly assign agency. Their fingers locked briefly under the surface.

Then a larger wave broke between them and tore them apart.

On shore, they wrapped themselves in towels and sat close enough that their shoulders touched plainly now, without plausible deniability. The fog swallowed the horizon. The family packed up and climbed the trail, leaving behind a plastic shovel, which Julian noticed and ran after them to return. Of course he did. He was infuriatingly good in minor moral tests.

When he came back, Mara was packing the cooler. Sand stuck to the peaches. One remained intact, improbably unbruised. She held it out to him.

“Your provisions.”

He took it but did not eat. “Stay tonight,” he said.

She looked at him.

“At the house,” he added, too quickly. “I mean, don’t work. Don’t hide in your studio. Come sit on the porch after dinner.”

It was both less and more than she had thought he might say. Stay tonight. At the house. On the porch. With everyone? Without? Language, cowardly instrument, always providing exits.

“I wasn’t hiding.”

“You were absolutely hiding.”

“I was revising.”

“You were revising in a locked room with the lights off.”

“That’s my process.”

He smiled. “Come anyway.”

She wanted to ask: And then what? Would they sit among the others while moths battered themselves against the porch light? Would he tell the composer some story about a train in Hungary while Mara memorized the way his hands moved? Would they wait until everyone drifted to bed? Would they stand in the dark hallway outside their separate rooms, each carrying the unbearable dignity of restraint?

The peach rested in his palm. He turned it once with his thumb.

“You come,” she said finally. “To my studio. I’ll show you the essay.”

His expression changed so slightly that anyone else might have missed it. But she had become expert in Julian’s weather. “The disappearance one?”

“They’re all the disappearance one.”

“I’d like that.”

Good, then. They had made a plan sufficiently ambiguous to survive contact with fear.

The climb back up was worse than the descent. The cooler bumped against Julian’s leg; Mara’s ankle stung. Twice they had to stop and pretend to admire the view. Fog moved over the water below, erasing the beach in increments. By the time they reached the car, her lungs burned and his shirt clung damply to his back.

The car would not start.

Julian turned the key. The engine clicked once, then offered a silence so complete it seemed philosophical.

“No,” Mara said.

He tried again.

“No.”

“It may respond to encouragement.”

“It’s a rental car, not an orchid.”

He got out, opened the hood, and stared into the engine with the solemn incomprehension of a man examining an autopsy.

“Do you know anything about cars?” she asked.

“I know they are emotionally oriented.”

They had no service. Of course they had no service. The fog thickened, and the dirt clearing emptied of all other vehicles because sensible people had left before the weather became a plot device. Mara sat on the hood while Julian walked in widening circles holding his phone aloft. Once he climbed onto a boulder, slipped, and caught himself with a curse so sharp she laughed before she could stop herself.

“I could have died,” he called.

“You could have gotten one bar.”

Eventually, by standing near the road with one arm extended like a tragic statue, Mara found service enough to call the rental company. A tow truck would come. Approximately ninety minutes. Possibly two hours. Did they feel safe? The operator asked this in a tone suggesting safety was a menu option they might select.

They waited in the car with the doors open, because the interior smelled now of wet towels, cheese, and failure. Fog beaded on the windshield. Julian split the last peach with his hands, twisting until it came apart raggedly. He gave her the larger half.

“Why do you keep doing that?” she asked.

“What?”

“Giving me the better piece.”

He looked at his own half, then hers. “Do I?”

“Yes.”

“I hadn’t noticed.”

Liar, perhaps. Or worse, not liar.

She ate the peach slowly. It was cooler now, less sweet than the first, and fibrous near the pit. Julian leaned back in the driver’s seat, eyes closed. He looked tired at last.

“I don’t want to do the goodbye,” he said.

The words entered the car and sat there, plain and homely. Not a confession. Not quite. But something.

Mara stared through the open door at the fog. There were things she could say that would make the moment smaller, safer. Nobody does. We still have three days. Don’t be dramatic. She had a whole bright toolbox of evasions.

Instead she said, “Me neither.”

He opened his eyes. She could feel him looking at her and refused to turn because turning would require choices for which she had not dressed appropriately. Her wet swimsuit was cold beneath her clothes. Her bandaged ankle pulsed. In the glove compartment, the rental agreement trembled slightly in the wind.

“We could be bad at it,” he said. “The goodbye.”

“We are bad at most things.”

“True.”

“We got lost at a vet.”

“We forgot a knife.”

“You attacked yourself with a boulder.”

“The car died of grief.”

She smiled despite herself. Then the smile faded, not unpleasantly. Maybe this was all they would get: a dead car, a shared peach, fog enclosing them with almost theatrical tenderness. Maybe desire did not always arrive demanding to be resolved. Maybe sometimes it remained atmospheric, like weather one carried elsewhere in the lining of a coat.

Julian reached across the space between the seats and took her hand. No wave came to separate them. His palm was slightly sticky with peach juice. Hers was cold.

Mara let him hold it. That was the astonishing thing: not the touch, but that she allowed the moment to continue without editing it into irony. The fog pressed close to the windows. Somewhere below, invisible now, the tide was coming in over the pools, covering the sea stars, the anemones, the little borrowed houses of crabs.

When the tow truck finally appeared, its yellow lights turning the fog into a cheap miracle, Julian released her hand only after the driver knocked on the window.

“Battery,” the driver said five minutes later, as if delivering a moral diagnosis. He jump-started the car, lectured them about headlights, and accepted Julian’s thanks with professional indifference.

They drove back carefully, the radio off. The road unspooled through the fog; eucalyptus branches emerged and vanished. Neither of them mentioned the hand-holding. This restraint felt less like cowardice now than preservation. Speech could bruise things. Speech had its uses, yes, but silence had been maligned.

At the residency house, lights glowed in the downstairs windows. Someone was playing piano badly and with conviction. Dinner smells drifted out: garlic, tomatoes, burnt bread. Ordinary life, offensively ongoing.

Julian parked but did not turn off the engine immediately.

“Studio later?” he said.

“Yes.”

He nodded. They sat another moment, facing the lit house.

Mara knew Monday would come. Rooms would be stripped of their temporary selves. Suitcases would open their black mouths. Email addresses would be confirmed, promises made too casually or too intensely. Chicago would be waiting, and Lisbon, and whatever versions of them existed without this coast, this house, this borrowed car smelling of sand.

But not yet. Not quite yet.

She opened her door and stepped into the damp evening. Julian lifted the cooler from the back seat, and together they walked toward the house, carrying towels stiff with salt, leftover cheese, a map folded wrong, and the last unspent hours between them—awkward, luminous, impossible to keep.
