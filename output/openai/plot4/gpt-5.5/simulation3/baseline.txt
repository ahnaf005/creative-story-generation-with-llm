**The Last Tide**

On the last Friday of the residency, Mara and Theo missed the bus to the beach because Theo insisted on buying peaches from a man who had arranged them in pyramids beside the road.

“They won’t taste like this in Berlin,” he said, selecting each one as if auditioning fruit for a private performance.

“You don’t live in Berlin.”

“I will in two weeks.”

“You’ll live near Berlin,” Mara said. “In a town you can’t pronounce.”

“I can pronounce it.”

“Then say it.”

Theo looked at the peach in his hand and smiled. “I can almost pronounce it.”

The bus groaned away from the stop in a veil of dust, its rear window reflecting the white afternoon. Mara watched it disappear around the bend toward the coast.

“You see?” she said. “This is what happens when you romanticize produce.”

Theo held out a peach. “Consolation?”

It was warm from the sun, flushed with gold and red. She took it because she was hungry, because she liked the way his long fingers hesitated before letting go, and because tomorrow everyone would begin leaving the residency house in waves—painters with taped-up canvases, poets with hangovers, composers with hard drives in their socks. Theo on the six a.m. train north. Mara the next afternoon south, back to a city where her plants had probably died and her old life waited with the patience of a creditor.

They had been at the residency for six weeks, lodged in an old convent on a hill above a Mediterranean town whose name Mara still felt she was mispronouncing. There were twelve artists in all. At first, she and Theo had orbited the same conversations without touching them. He was a photographer who claimed not to photograph people; she was a novelist who had spent most of the residency failing to write a novel about a woman who disappeared from her own life and was not missed for three days. Theo’s studio was across the hall from hers. At night she heard him open and close drawers, pace, play a single piece of music over and over until she began to dream in its rhythm.

They became friends in the third week after a power outage trapped them in the kitchen with a pot of half-cooked rice and two bottles of local wine. Someone had lit candles. Someone had found a guitar. At some point Mara and Theo were sitting under the open window, sharing burnt rice scraped from the bottom of the pot and laughing too quietly for the size of the room.

After that, they took walks down into town. They bought oranges and notebooks and strange, salty pastries. They read near each other in the courtyard and pretended coincidence had arranged it. Once, during a storm, Theo put his raincoat over both of their heads as they ran from the laundry shed, and Mara spent the rest of the evening unable to recall anything except the brief damp warmth of his shoulder against hers.

Nothing had happened. This fact had become its own event.

Now the bus was gone, and the beach waited twelve kilometers away.

“We could go tomorrow,” Mara said.

“We leave tomorrow.”

“I leave tomorrow. You leave at an hour designed by sadists.”

“Then we walk,” Theo said.

“In this heat?”

“It’s downhill.”

“And back?”

He looked toward the road, then back at her. “We’ll be transformed by the sea. Stronger. Braver. Less argumentative.”

“You’ve mistaken the sea for a cult.”

But she started walking.

The road curved through terraces of olive trees, their leaves flashing silver in the wind. Grasshoppers flung themselves from the verge. Above them, the sky was an enamel blue, hard and polished. Theo wore a straw hat he had bought from a market stall in town, too small for him and slightly crooked. Mara had told him it made him look like an amateur smuggler. He had thanked her solemnly.

For the first half hour they spoke easily. About the residency director’s habit of appearing silently behind people at breakfast. About the Austrian sculptor who had built a chair no one could sit on and then accused everyone of philistinism. About the novel Mara had not written.

“It’s not nothing,” Theo said when she described it that way.

“It is mostly nothing.”

“Most things are mostly nothing until they aren’t.”

“That sounds like something you’d put under one of your photographs.”

“I don’t put words under photographs.”

“Because you know they’d be things like that.”

He laughed. Mara had come to know several versions of his laugh: the polite one he used at dinner, the sudden boyish one when surprised, the soft exhale that seemed intended only for himself. This was the second kind. She treasured it, which annoyed her.

After an hour, the road forked. The left side descended toward a village of white houses. The right side, according to Theo’s phone, promised a shortcut to the beach.

“Your phone has lied before,” Mara said.

“It has made bold suggestions.”

“It once led us to a private goat enclosure.”

“The goats were hospitable.”

“They chased us.”

“They escorted us out.”

The shortcut turned out to be a path, then a suggestion of a path, then a stretch of scrubland where thorny bushes clutched at Mara’s skirt. They pushed through wild rosemary and pale dry grass. The sea appeared in glimpses between rocks, impossibly blue and still far away. By the time they admitted they were lost, Theo had a scratch on his cheek and Mara had one sandal held together with a strip of linen torn from the hem of her shirt.

“I’m beginning to suspect,” she said, balancing on a stone, “that transformation is painful.”

Theo crouched at her feet, trying to retie the sandal. “You say this as if you didn’t choose the path.”

“I chose to follow you.”

“That was your first mistake.”

“Yes.”

He glanced up. The word had come out too plainly. For a second they were both very still. His hand rested lightly around her ankle, and the heat seemed to gather there.

Then a goat bleated from somewhere behind them, indignant and close.

Mara jerked, nearly kicking him in the chest. Theo fell backward into a bush with a cry that was more surprise than pain. A small brown goat emerged from behind a rock, chewing and looking at them with bored contempt.

Mara began laughing. She tried to stop when she saw the burrs clinging to Theo’s shirt, but the sight of him sitting in the shrubbery, defeated by a goat the size of a laundry basket, dissolved her.

“You’re cruel,” he said, though he was laughing too.

“You should photograph it. The dignity of rural life.”

“I only photograph landscapes.”

“This is a landscape with an opinion.”

They found the path again by following the goat, who seemed offended by their dependence and frequently paused to glare back at them. It led them to a narrow lane that finally descended toward the coast. They arrived at the beach in late afternoon, sweaty, scratched, and quiet.

It was not the grand beach they had imagined. No long crescent of white sand, no empty expanse waiting for revelation. Instead there was a small cove cupped between black rocks, its sand coarse and gray, scattered with seaweed and bottle caps. A family occupied one end beneath an orange umbrella. Two teenagers smoked on the rocks. A dog slept in the exact center of the beach as if claiming it.

“It’s perfect,” Theo said.

Mara looked at him.

“It’s terrible,” he amended. “But perfect.”

They changed behind separate boulders. Mara’s swimsuit was black, practical, older than some of the residency fellows’ careers. When she stepped out, Theo was already in the water up to his waist, shoulders pale and angular, hair darkened and flattened by the sea. He waved once, then dove.

Mara waded in slowly. The water was shockingly cold, wrapping her calves, her thighs, her ribs. She swore under her breath. Theo surfaced nearby, gasping.

“Braver yet?” he asked.

“Never speak to me again.”

He splashed her, lightly. She splashed him back, less lightly. A brief, childish war followed. They chased each other through the shallows until Mara slipped on a submerged rock and went under. The world became bubbles and salt. When she surfaced, coughing, Theo was beside her.

“Are you all right?”

His face was so nakedly concerned that she wanted to make a joke and could not. Water ran from his lashes. His hand hovered near her arm, not touching.

“I’m fine,” she said.

“You’re bleeding.”

A thin red line marked her shin. Nothing serious. A scratch. He guided her to a flat rock at the edge of the water, and she let him, though she did not need guiding. He knelt and poured water from his cupped hands over the scrape.

“You’re very calm in a crisis,” she said.

“This is not a crisis.”

“What is it?”

He looked up at her. For a moment, Mara thought he might answer differently. Might say: This is the problem. This is what we have been practicing not to name. This is the last day and your leg in my hand and six weeks wasted being careful.

Instead he said, “A minor injury caused by poor swimming technique.”

She kicked water at him with her good leg.

They lay on towels as the sun lowered and the family under the orange umbrella packed up noisily. The teenagers left. The dog, after a long philosophical struggle, moved three meters and slept again. Theo cut peaches with a pocketknife and handed slices to Mara, juice running down his wrist.

“What will you do first when you get home?” he asked.

“Apologize to my basil. If it’s still alive.”

“And if it isn’t?”

“Bury it with honors.”

He smiled. “Then?”

“Answer emails. Avoid my editor. Sleep in my own bed and hate how quiet it is.”

“The convent isn’t quiet.”

“At night it is. Under all the noises.”

She did not say: except for you across the hall. Except for your drawer opening, your chair scraping, your footsteps like questions.

Theo stretched on his back, one arm over his eyes. “I think I’ll miss the bells.”

“You complained about the bells every morning.”

“I can miss what I complained about.”

“That’s almost your entire personality.”

He lowered his arm and turned his face toward her. “Will you miss anything?”

The safe answers lined themselves up: the view, the food, the time, the illusion of being a person without obligations. Mara picked up a pebble and pressed it into the sand.

“Yes,” she said.

The word sat between them, inadequate and immense.

Theo watched her, and the beach seemed to empty further around them, the world drawing back to give them room. Mara could hear the water dragging gently at the shore, the clink of rigging from boats beyond the rocks, a child shouting somewhere distant and inland.

He said, “Mara—”

The dog chose that moment to rise, shake itself violently, and steal Theo’s remaining peach.

“Hey!” Theo scrambled up. The dog trotted away, delighted, peach clenched in its jaws.

Mara laughed so hard she had to lie down.

“You encourage theft,” Theo said, returning without the peach.

“You were robbed because you were vulnerable.”

“I was robbed because you failed to establish dominance.”

“You’re the one who believes in landscapes. Negotiate with it.”

He sat beside her, knees drawn up, smiling despite himself. The interrupted moment did not return. Or perhaps it had only changed shape, becoming one more object they would carry away separately: almost, almost.

They stayed until evening gathered. The sky softened from blue to lavender to a bruised rose near the horizon. The sea darkened. When the air cooled, Mara put on her shirt, now uneven at the hem from the sandal repair, and Theo shook sand from the towel with excessive seriousness.

The problem of getting back remained.

“There may be an evening bus,” Theo said.

“There may also be a helicopter if we believe hard enough.”

They climbed the road to the village above the cove. It consisted of one closed café, several sleeping cats, and a bus sign bent at an angle suggesting surrender. According to the posted schedule, the last bus had departed forty minutes earlier.

“This is your fault,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“You admit it?”

“I admit nothing legally.”

They sat on a low wall beside the road. Theo’s phone had little battery and no signal. Mara’s had died sometime between the goat and the sea. The village smelled of dust, jasmine, and fried fish from an unseen kitchen. A woman in a blue apron emerged from a doorway, looked them over, and spoke rapidly in Italian.

Theo answered in what Mara believed was Spanish with decorative hand gestures. The woman frowned, then laughed, then pointed down the hill, up the hill, at the sky, at her watch, and finally at an elderly man smoking beside a small red car.

Five minutes later, they were folded into the back of the car among crates of tomatoes, while the elderly man drove them toward the town at a speed that suggested he had made peace with mortality long ago.

Theo sat pressed against Mara from shoulder to knee. There was nowhere else for either of them to go. The road twisted in the dusk. Tomatoes rolled against their ankles. The driver sang under his breath to the radio, which played static interrupted by bursts of opera.

Mara kept her hands clasped in her lap. Theo’s arm was warm against hers. At each curve, they leaned into each other and then away, apologizing at first, then not. She could see the pulse in his throat. She wondered whether desire was always so ordinary when examined closely: a series of accidents mistaken for signs, a shortage of space, the smell of sun on someone’s skin, the knowledge that soon there would be nothing to decide.

The car dropped them at the edge of town. They tried to pay, but the old man refused with theatrical offense and gave them each a tomato instead. The residency house was still two kilometers uphill.

“Do you think,” Theo asked, holding his tomato, “this day is trying to become symbolic?”

“It’s trying too hard.”

They walked the final stretch in darkness. The hill was steeper than Mara remembered. Lights glowed above them from the convent windows. Somewhere, dinner would be ending. Plates cleared, wine poured, the last night beginning its predictable collapse into speeches and promises to visit. Tomorrow, all the temporary intimacies of the residency would be packed into suitcases and dispersed.

Halfway up, Theo stopped. Mara took three steps before realizing and turned back.

“What?”

He was looking toward the sea, invisible now beyond the black slopes. “I don’t want to go back yet.”

She knew he meant the house. She knew he did not mean only the house.

“We don’t have to,” she said.

They left the road and climbed into a small olive grove where the ground was pale with fallen leaves. The town below shimmered in fragments. The moon had risen, thin and low. They sat with their backs against an old tree, close but not touching.

For a while neither spoke.

Finally Theo said, “In Berlin—near Berlin—I have a studio for three months. Then maybe Prague. Maybe nothing. I don’t know.”

“I know.”

“I’m not good at—” He stopped.

“At what?”

He laughed once, softly. “Staying, apparently.”

Mara leaned her head back against the bark. “I’m very good at staying. I stay long past usefulness. In cities. Relationships. Drafts. Conversations.”

“This conversation?”

“Possibly.”

He turned to her. In the dimness his expression was difficult to read, which made it easier and harder at once.

“I wanted to kiss you,” he said.

Mara felt something in her chest lift and ache. The sentence should have been thrilling. It was thrilling. It was also already sad, because of the tense he had chosen.

“When?”

He looked away. “Several times. Most times.”

She closed her eyes briefly. “Why didn’t you?”

“Because you didn’t seem to want me to.”

“I did.”

The leaves stirred overhead. From the convent came the faint sound of laughter, a bottle breaking, applause. Theo breathed in as if he had been struck.

“Oh,” he said.

It was such a small word to hold six weeks.

Mara looked at him then. “And now?”

He met her eyes. “Now you leave tomorrow.”

“You leave first.”

“Yes.”

A practical fact. A coward’s refuge. A mercy.

He raised his hand, then let it fall. She wanted to take it. She wanted not to have to. The space between them was tiny, ridiculous, impassable. She thought of the beach, the dog stealing the peach, the old man’s tomatoes, the goat escorting them toward the sea. All day the world had been interrupting them, or rescuing them, and now at last there was no one to blame.

Mara reached for his hand.

Theo laced his fingers through hers with such care that she nearly cried. They sat like that beneath the olive tree, holding hands in the dark, saying nothing. It was not enough. It was exactly enough. His thumb moved once across her knuckles. She memorized the motion with the doomed greed of a traveler memorizing a room before leaving it forever.

After a long time, he said, “If I kiss you now, will it make tomorrow worse?”

“Yes.”

He nodded.

She waited.

Then he leaned in, and she met him halfway.

The kiss was gentle at first, almost formal, as if asking permission again and again. Then it deepened, and with it came the hunger they had spent weeks disguising as wit, as timing, as walks to town for oranges. Mara touched his face where the goat path had scratched him. He laughed against her mouth, and she did too, because even this could not escape the day’s absurdity.

When they drew apart, the night had not changed. The convent lights still shone. The sea was still hidden. Departure remained exactly where it had been.

Theo rested his forehead against hers. “I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be.”

“For waiting.”

“For not waiting longer.”

He smiled sadly. “Which is it?”

“Yes,” she said.

They returned to the house close to midnight. In the courtyard, the others were singing badly. Someone had put flowers in a wine bottle. Someone cried into the Austrian sculptor’s shoulder while he looked both moved and alarmed. Mara and Theo stood at the edge of the light, their hands no longer touching.

“There you are!” called Lina, the residency director, appearing as always from nowhere. “We thought the sea had taken you.”

“It considered us,” Mara said.

Theo held up the tomato. “But we returned with provisions.”

The night unfolded around them. There were toasts, embraces, badly exchanged addresses. Mara drank wine and felt Theo’s presence across the courtyard as if he were a second moon. They did not sit together. They did not need to, or did not dare.

At three in the morning, Mara woke to the sound of Theo’s suitcase wheels in the corridor.

She had not meant to sleep. She had meant to wait, to say goodbye properly, to be dignified or reckless. Instead she opened her door barefoot and half-dreaming as he was passing.

He stopped.

The corridor was gray before dawn. His hat was in his hand, his backpack on one shoulder. For a moment they simply looked at each other, stripped of beach and wine and borrowed courage.

“Your train,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Will you make it?”

“If the taxi comes.”

“Does it know about you and transportation?”

“I’ve tried not to mention it.”

She smiled, then didn’t.

Theo set down his bag. He came to her, and they embraced. Not dramatically. Not like people in films who have been granted violins and rain. He held her carefully, his cheek against her hair, and Mara gripped the back of his shirt with both hands.

“I’ll write,” he said.

She knew he meant it. She also knew meaning it was not the same as doing it, and doing it was not the same as anything.

“Send photographs,” she said.

“I don’t photograph people.”

“Then send landscapes with opinions.”

His laugh moved through her.

Outside, a car horn gave a short, impatient beep.

They pulled apart. Theo picked up his suitcase. At the end of the corridor he turned once, as people do when they cannot help obeying old choreography. Mara raised her hand. He raised his.

Then he was gone.

Later, after the sun had climbed and the house filled with the ugly music of departure—zippers, footsteps, promises—Mara packed her own suitcase. On her desk she found one of the peaches, slightly bruised, and beside it a small photograph.

It showed the cove at dusk: the gray sand, the black rocks, the blurred shape of the dog asleep near the waterline. In the foreground, at the edge of the frame, was Mara’s towel, a corner of her torn shirt, her hand resting open on the sand. Not a portrait, exactly. Almost an accident.

On the back, Theo had written one sentence.

*Most things are mostly nothing until they aren’t.*

Mara laughed, though it hurt. She put the photograph inside her notebook, between the unwritten pages of her novel. Then she took her suitcase downstairs and stepped into the bright, indifferent day.
