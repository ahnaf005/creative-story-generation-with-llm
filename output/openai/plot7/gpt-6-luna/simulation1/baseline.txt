### The Night Nursery

At 2:13 in the morning, Mara woke to the sound of a baby crying in the wrong key.

It wasn’t a loud cry. It was thin and questioning, like a note played on a glass rim. She opened her eyes. The maternity ward was washed in blue light, and the rows of bassinets stood along the wall like little boats tied to a dock. Beyond the window, the city had disappeared into fog.

Her daughter slept beside her.

Mara leaned over the bassinet. The baby’s face was scrunched, her mouth a small red O. A white cap covered her dark hair. On her wrist, beneath the hospital band, the name was written in black marker: **BABY GIRL—MARA VENN**.

“Hello, June,” Mara whispered.

She had chosen the name months ago, before she knew what June would look like. Her husband had wanted Celia. Mara had said June, because the name felt like a door opening. They had agreed to decide after the birth. The birth had happened six hours ago, after seventeen hours of labor and an emergency operation. By the time the baby arrived, Mara had been too tired to argue. Her husband had gone home to shower and bring her a clean shirt. He was supposed to return before dawn.

The baby stopped crying.

Mara lay back. The room hummed with the soft, mechanical breathing of machines. Somewhere, a nurse laughed. Somewhere else, someone sobbed.

Then the glass note came again.

She sat up. On the far side of the room, a woman in the next bed was staring at her own bassinet.

“Did you hear that?” Mara asked.

The woman turned. She had a narrow face and a dark braid spread over one shoulder. Her name, according to the card at the foot of her bed, was Mrs. Sato.

“I hear it all night,” she said. “It’s the baby they haven’t brought back yet.”

“Which baby?”

Mrs. Sato looked down at her hands. “I don’t know. Mine, I suppose.”

A nurse came through the door, pushing a cart. She was young and wore a badge that said **LUCY**, though the name seemed somehow provisional, like a name she’d borrowed for the shift.

“Everyone all right?” Lucy asked.

“Yes,” Mara said.

Mrs. Sato said nothing.

Lucy checked the bassinets, first Mara’s, then Mrs. Sato’s. She glanced at the cards and smiled the bright, tired smile of someone repeating a sentence she had repeated many times.

“They’re all where they should be.”

“Mine was crying,” Mara said.

“They do that.”

“It was a strange sound.”

“Newborns are full of surprises.” Lucy touched the baby’s cap, then moved on.

Mara watched her go. At the end of the corridor, Lucy turned left. The maternity ward was a straight corridor, but for a moment her footsteps seemed to recede in both directions.

Mara shut her eyes.

When she opened them, the clock read 2:13.

She pressed the call button. It took several minutes for anyone to come. During that time, she heard a woman speaking softly from the bed across the room.

“Have you seen my son?” the woman said.

Mara looked over. The bed had seemed empty before. Now there was a woman sitting upright in it, her hair wet and dark against her cheeks. A bassinet stood beside her.

“Which one is yours?” Mara asked.

The woman blinked. “The one with the yellow blanket.”

All the babies had white blankets.

“Do you know your name?” Mara asked.

The woman considered. “I knew it downstairs.”

A second woman emerged from the bathroom, one hand holding the wall. She wore a hospital gown and no slippers. Her face was pale, but she was smiling.

“Is it morning?” she asked.

“Not yet,” Mara said.

“Thank God.” The woman looked at the dark window. “I have to get home before my mother notices.”

She climbed into the empty bed and pulled the blanket over herself.

Mara pressed the call button again. This time, the nurse arrived at once, a broad-shouldered woman with silver hair and no badge.

“I’m sorry,” Mara said. “I think there are people in here who shouldn’t be.”

The nurse looked around. “There are mothers in here who gave birth tonight.”

“Yes, but—”

“All of them?”

The question was so reasonable that Mara stopped.

The nurse adjusted the blanket at Mara’s feet. “Try to rest. You’ve had a difficult day.”

“I need to see my husband.”

“He’ll be here in the morning.”

“He said before dawn.”

The nurse checked the window. “It is before dawn.”

Mara looked again. The fog had swallowed the city, and there was no sign of the sun.

The nurse left. The room shifted around her—the beds, the bassinets, the women sleeping with their hands curled toward their babies. Mara’s body ached in a deep, unfamiliar way. She could feel the stitches pulling when she breathed. She wanted June in her arms.

When she reached for the bassinet, it was empty.

She stared at it, unable to understand the small, neat fact of absence.

“June?”

A soft cry rose from somewhere down the hall. The glass note, clear as a spoon against a jar.

Mara swung her legs over the side of the bed. The floor was cold beneath her feet. She wrapped herself in the blue robe hanging on the chair and stepped into the corridor.

At the nurses’ station, a woman in a green gown was counting the chairs.

“Seven,” she said. “There were eight before.”

“What are you doing?” Mara asked.

“Looking for my husband.”

“Are you sure he was a chair?”

The woman gave her a careful look. “I’m sure he was here.”

At the far end of the corridor, the double doors stood open. Beyond them, Mara could see another corridor, though she was certain there was supposed to be a wall there. A sign overhead read **POSTPARTUM**, and below it another sign read **ARRIVALS**.

The baby cried again.

Mara followed the sound.

She passed a room where two women sat side by side, each holding a baby. One woman murmured, “That’s not his nose,” while the other said, “It will be by morning.”

A nurse wheeled a bassinet past her, its wheels squeaking at regular intervals. The baby inside slept with one fist beside its cheek. Mara reached out, almost touching the blanket.

“Not that one,” the nurse said.

“Where is mine?”

The nurse kept walking. “You’ll know.”

At the next doorway, Mara found Mrs. Sato. She was standing in the middle of a small room with no windows. Her braid hung loose, and she held a baby wrapped in a yellow blanket.

“I found him,” she said.

“That isn’t your blanket.”

Mrs. Sato looked down. “No, I suppose not.”

“Is he yours?”

“I don’t know.” Her eyes shone. “He’s warm.”

Mara understood. Warmth could be enough for a moment. A baby’s weight against a chest. The tiny movements, the animal certainty of breath.

Mrs. Sato handed the child to a nurse who had appeared behind them. “Take him to his mother,” she said.

The nurse nodded and vanished into the corridor.

“Maybe I should go back,” Mara said.

“Maybe,” said Mrs. Sato.

But she didn’t turn around. She seemed to have forgotten what she was returning to.

A door opened nearby. Inside, the room was bright and ordinary: white walls, a chair, a bassinet. A baby lay in it, awake and quiet. Its face was long and solemn. A dark curl showed beneath its cap.

Mara knew it was not June.

June’s hair had been fine and black, with a small, distinct swirl at the crown. This baby had a curl that fell over its forehead. June had a little crease under her left eye, visible when she frowned. This baby’s eyes were wide and smooth.

The baby wore a wristband. Mara leaned closer.

**BABY BOY—ELISE R.**

The child looked up at her.

She should have called for the nurse. She should have picked him up only to read the label more clearly. She should have left him untouched.

Instead, she slid her hands beneath the blanket and lifted him.

He was heavier than June. His body settled against her with a weight that seemed to answer a question she hadn’t known she was asking. He smelled of milk and warm cotton. His fingers opened and closed against the front of her robe.

“Hello,” she said.

His mouth worked silently. For a second she felt an unreasonable, almost frightening certainty: she had been expected here. Not by this child, exactly, but by the space he occupied in the world.

A woman spoke behind her.

“That’s mine,” she said.

Mara turned. The woman stood in the doorway, barefoot, with a hospital band around her wrist. Her eyes were red-rimmed but calm.

“Elise?” Mara asked, reading the band.

The woman nodded. “I think so.”

“I’m sorry. I thought—”

“I know.” Elise came nearer. She didn’t reach for the baby. “He looks different when he’s over there.”

Mara glanced down at the child. “He isn’t mine.”

“No,” Elise said.

The baby made a small, contented sound.

Mara waited for Elise to take him. Elise looked at him, then at Mara, as if trying to remember the proper order of things. At last she touched the baby’s cheek with one finger.

“Keep him warm,” she said.

The words settled between them.

“What’s his name?” Mara asked.

“Do you know?”

“No.”

Elise shook her head. “Neither do I.”

They stood together, two strangers in the bright little room, until the fluorescent lights flickered. When they steadied, Elise was gone.

Mara carried the baby back to her room.

The woman in the green gown was asleep beneath the blanket. Mrs. Sato sat beside her bed, humming. Mara returned to her own bed and lowered the baby into the bassinet.

He began to cry.

The sound was ordinary, a thin, human cry. It moved through Mara like a needle passing through cloth. She lifted him again, and he settled against her chest. His heartbeat fluttered under her palm.

She told herself she would find the nurse in the morning. She would explain the bracelet. She would ask them to bring June. The thoughts arrived in the right order, but none of them seemed urgent. She was tired enough to accept that morning could be a place they had not reached yet.

The clock read 2:13.

Then, all at once, it read 5:46.

A nurse appeared with a clipboard. It was Lucy, the young nurse from before. Daylight made her look more solid.

“Your husband is here,” she said.

Mara looked toward the doorway. A man stood there holding a paper cup of coffee and a clean shirt. He looked exhausted and delighted.

“You’re up,” he said. “How’s June?”

Mara looked down at the baby in her arms.

The baby’s dark curl rested against her wrist. His eyes were closed. The band around his ankle was turned inward, hidden by the blanket.

“She’s fine,” Mara said.

Her husband frowned at the infant, then at her. “That’s a boy.”

“Is it?”

He came close enough to see the band. His face changed as he read it.

“Mara.”

“I know,” she said.

He looked toward the nursery, then back at the baby. He was already reaching for the call button.

Mara could have let him press it. She could have told him everything: the women in the corridor, the room with no windows, Elise’s calm, the impossible time on the clock. She could have explained that she had held the wrong child and understood, with no reason at all, that the wrongness did not feel like a mistake.

Instead, she touched the baby’s cheek.

His eyes opened. They were dark and unfocused, yet she felt seen.

Her husband stood very still. “We have to find her.”

“Yes,” Mara said.

But she did not give the baby back.

The nurses came. There were forms and questions, quick footsteps, the squeak of wheels. Someone checked the bracelet and someone else checked the room. No one asked why the baby was in Mara’s arms. No one seemed to know which direction to go.

When they finally brought a wheelchair, her husband helped her sit. He carried the baby, carefully, with one hand under the head. Mara kept a finger curled around the child’s small fist.

They rolled past the nursery. The glass window reflected the corridor, the pale morning, the chairs lined along the wall. For an instant Mara thought she saw Elise standing among the bassinets, watching them go.

Then the doors opened.

Outside, the fog had lifted. The sky was the color of a new bruise, softening toward pink. Her husband held the baby close, still reading the name on the bracelet, still waiting for Mara to tell him what to do.

Mara took the child from him.

She knew he wasn’t hers. She knew June was somewhere behind them, or ahead, or perhaps waiting under a different name. And yet, when the baby’s head settled beneath her chin, a quiet happiness rose inside her—not triumph, not relief, but something old and unearned, as if she had been forgiven for a thing she could not remember doing.

The baby sighed.

Mara stepped into the morning with him in her arms.
