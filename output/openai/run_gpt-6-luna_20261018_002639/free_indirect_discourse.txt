### The Empty Flat

In the dream, the flat was always on the third floor.

Daniel knew this because the stairs were steep and narrow, and because he had to count them. Thirty-two, with a landing halfway up where a dead plant sat in a cracked blue pot. At the top was a door painted the yellowish white of old teeth. He never knew how he had got the key. He only knew the flat was his.

Inside, there was no furniture. The rooms were bright, though no sun showed through the tall windows. A cold draft wandered from room to room. Sometimes Daniel heard a tap running in the kitchen. Sometimes he heard Rachel calling from somewhere he could not see.

He woke before he found her.

The dream came often enough that he’d stopped telling himself it meant nothing. Not that it meant anything, either. Dreams were just the mind dragging its furniture around in the dark. Still, there was the flat again. The empty rooms. Rachel’s voice, never quite saying his name.

He woke before dawn on a Wednesday and lay very still beside Isabel, listening to the radiator click. Her back was turned to him. The sheet had slipped from one shoulder. She had been warm a moment ago, or he had been dreaming that too.

He had been trying to tell her for weeks. Each time he found a reason not to. It sounded stupid. It sounded disloyal. It sounded like asking her to take care of an old ghost he’d invited into their bed.

By breakfast, he was tired of keeping it to himself.

“I keep dreaming about Rachel,” he said.

Isabel lowered her coffee cup. “All right.”

“And a flat. Some place I’ve never seen before. Empty. I’m looking for her, but I never find her.”

Isabel waited. She was good at waiting; it could feel like kindness or a test. He hoped it was kindness.

“I thought you were going to tell me you’d dreamed you were a dentist,” she said. “That’s what I usually dream when I’m stressed.”

“Do you?”

“I have a lot of teeth to work with.” She smiled, then let the smile go. “Does it bother you?”

The obvious answer was yes. The less obvious answer was that he didn’t know what bothered him: Rachel in the dream, the empty flat, or the way he had woken and found Isabel beside him and felt, for an instant, that he was in the wrong life.

“It’s not that I miss her,” he said.

“I didn’t ask if you missed her.”

Right. He had put the word there himself, between them. “No. I know.”

Isabel turned her cup slowly on the table. “Maybe you miss who you were when you were with her.”

That sounded plausible. It also sounded like something printed on the wall of a therapist’s office. Daniel looked at the butter dish instead.

Their marriage had ended without a catastrophe. No one had left for someone else, or been caught with a secret account. There had been years of small withdrawals: evenings spent at separate ends of the sofa, questions answered with “fine,” plans postponed until the plans stopped being plans. When Rachel said she wanted to leave, he’d been surprised, though he had watched her go quiet for a long time. He had thought quiet was the same as staying.

“What about Simon?” he asked.

Isabel’s expression changed. “What about him?”

“You said he wanted to live in Lisbon.”

“He did. He wanted to live in Lisbon, and Berlin, and maybe on a boat. He liked the idea of a life more than the life.” She shrugged. “I was twenty-eight. I thought if I were patient enough, he’d decide to live in one place and with me.”

“Did he?”

“No.” She gathered the plates. “But I did learn I don’t want to be somebody’s waiting room.”

He felt the remark land, not quite a blow. “Is that what I’m doing?”

“I don’t know. You haven’t been talking about Rachel. You’ve been dreaming about her, apparently. There’s a difference.”

“Is there?”

“Probably. I’m not an expert.” She stood with the plates in her hands. “I just don’t want to have to compete with the best version of a marriage that’s already over.”

He was left with the coffee cooling between his hands. There it was, then. The difficult thing, said without drama. He wanted to tell her she wasn’t competing. But it might be true that he had kept Rachel carefully polished in his mind—not as a person, exactly, but as the keeper of all the things he’d failed to say at the right time.

That evening they talked again. Not all at once, and not in the grand way people talked in films, laying their histories out under soft light. They talked while Isabel folded laundry and he hunted through the drawer for a missing sock. They talked in small pieces.

He told her about Rachel’s habit of leaving cupboard doors open. Isabel told him Simon had once bought a one-way ticket to Lisbon for her birthday, as if a surprise could stand in for asking. Daniel asked if she had loved him. She said yes, then, after a pause, “Or I loved the part where I thought he might become someone else.”

It was a relief to hear that love could be true and still be mistaken. He had always imagined the past as a row of locked doors, each one containing a final answer.

“Do you ever wonder,” he said, “if we’re just repeating things?”

“Of course. But if we were repeating things exactly, you’d have to start leaving cupboard doors open.”

“I could.”

“Don’t. I’d take it as a sign of moral decline.”

She leaned her shoulder against his. He let himself lean back. It was not a solution, but the contact felt like a small decision made in the present.

The next Saturday, Daniel went looking for a roll of tape. They had a drawer in the hall that held batteries, takeout menus, a screwdriver, several keys to unknown locks. The tape wasn’t there, of course. Instead, he found it in the pocket of an old canvas jacket that had been folded beneath a stack of scarves.

A ring of keys slid into his hand.

There were three: one long and brass, one small and silver, and one with a blue plastic tag, its lettering nearly rubbed away. He turned the tag to the light.

RIVER CLUB — LOCKER 14.

For a second, he was back on the water.

It had been late May, the first summer he and Isabel were together. They’d gone to the river because she’d seen a sign for canoe hire and said it looked fun. He had not been sure. Canoes seemed like things designed to expose a person’s weaknesses: balance, coordination, the ability to steer without arguing.

Isabel had claimed the back seat. “You’re taller,” she’d said. “You can do the hard bit.”

The hard bit, it turned out, was steering. They’d spent the first half-hour drifting into reeds and apologizing to swans. Isabel laughed so hard she had to put down her paddle. Daniel remembered the sound of it, startling and unguarded. At the bend in the river she’d reached across the canoe to point out a kingfisher, and the boat rocked.

“Don’t move,” he’d said.

“I’m not moving.”

“You’re leaning.”

“I’m looking.”

“Look from the middle.”

She’d sat back, grinning. “You’re very bossy for a man who’s going in circles.”

They had got better as the afternoon went on. They moved together, not perfectly, but well enough to glide. They stopped beneath the willow branches and ate squashed sandwiches. Isabel had one mustard smear on her wrist. He’d thought, with a suddenness that embarrassed him, that he could be happy here. Not happy forever. Just happy now, with her, on that bit of river.

How had he forgotten the key? The club had lent them the locker for their life jackets. He’d put the keys in his jacket and then, apparently, carried them through two moves and three winters. The flat in his dreams had no furniture, no trace of anyone. But this memory did. Isabel’s laugh. The paddle dripping. A mustard smear.

He took the keys to the kitchen, where Isabel was reading.

“Look what I found.”

She took the blue tag. “Oh! Locker fourteen. I wondered where those went.”

“You remember?”

“I remember you saying you’d put them somewhere safe.” She looked up at him. “I think I said, ‘That’s how things get lost.’”

“You were right.”

“I often am.”

A small silence. He almost said that he’d been thinking about Rachel again. Instead, he tried to give the memory its proper name.

“Do you remember that first time we went canoeing?”

“Of course. You were terrified of the swans.”

“I was respecting them.”

“You were paddling backward.”

“I was adapting.”

She laughed, and something in him loosened. Not a knot coming undone, exactly. More like a hand opening.

“There’s a canoe place by the old lock,” he said. “We could go this weekend.”

Isabel watched him carefully. “Is this because you’re trying to prove something?”

He had been ready to say no. He let the answer wait. “Maybe I’m trying to remember something. But not something with Rachel.”

“Okay,” she said. “I’d like that.”

On Sunday the river was high and brown, carrying leaves and the occasional twig. They hired a canoe from a wooden shed near the lock. Daniel wore the old jacket, though the keys stayed in his pocket this time. Isabel sat in front. The river opened around them, broad and unhurried.

“You steer,” she said.

“You said I was bad at it.”

“You were. It’ll be interesting to see if you’ve improved.”

He put the paddle in the water. The canoe turned slowly, then straightened. Isabel dipped her paddle in on the other side, and they moved forward.

They didn’t talk much at first. There was the work of keeping a line, the soft slap of water against the hull. Daniel watched the back of Isabel’s coat rise and fall with each stroke. It wasn’t the back of Rachel’s coat. He didn’t have to remind himself of that. Isabel was here, in front of him, real and capable of being irritated if he steered them into a branch.

At the bend, the current carried them under a willow. Isabel lifted her paddle.

“Careful,” he said.

She glanced back. “I’m only looking.”

“Look from the middle.”

“Still bossy.”

“Still going in circles.”

She laughed, and he felt the sound settle into the quiet places of him. The flat would probably return. Dreams were stubborn; they didn’t take advice. But perhaps he could stop searching every empty room for the person who wasn’t there.

The canoe drifted into the open water. Isabel reached back with one hand, and he took it briefly, keeping his other hand on the paddle.

The river moved beneath them. For once, he let the past come along without asking it to steer.
