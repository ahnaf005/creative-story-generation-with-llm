On the last morning of the residency, Mara and Eli set out for the beach with a paper map, two coffees, and the conviction that the bus would come.

It did not.

They waited beneath a crooked sign until the coffees went cold. Then Eli proposed they walk, though the sea was “probably only a little farther,” which turned out to mean three miles past a farm where a goat ate the corner of Mara’s map.

By the time they reached the shore, the tide was retreating. Mara took off her shoes and ran toward the water. Eli stayed behind to guard their bags, though there was nothing in them worth guarding except a notebook full of drawings he had never shown her.

A gust lifted the notebook from the open bag. It skittered down the beach. Eli chased it, slipped on wet sand, and landed hard on one knee. Mara laughed until she saw the blood. Then she knelt beside him, hands hovering, unsure whether to touch.

“You’re very graceful,” she said.

“I’ve been told.”

She cleaned the cut with the corner of her scarf. His knee trembled beneath her fingers, or perhaps her own hand did. Neither mentioned it.

They spent the afternoon building a crooked shelter from driftwood that collapsed whenever the wind shifted. They ate crushed biscuits and watched a dog steal someone’s sandwich. Every so often one of them began to say something, then let the waves take over.

At dusk, Eli asked what time her train left in the morning.

“Eight.”

“Mine’s at nine.”

They both looked toward the station, though it was miles away and hidden beyond the dunes.

That evening, back at the residency, they packed in separate rooms. Mara found a small shell in her coat pocket and put it on the windowsill. Across the courtyard, Eli’s light stayed on long after hers went out.

In the morning, they met at the gate with their bags. He offered to carry hers. She said it wasn’t heavy. He nodded, as if she had answered a different question.

They walked together to the road where their buses would split off. The goodbye was brief, almost ordinary. Mara reached for him, then changed the gesture into a tug at his collar, straightening it.

“Your knee,” she said.

“Your map,” he replied.

She still had it. The goat’s bite had removed the part showing the way back.

Then the first bus came. Mara climbed aboard, and through the fogged window she watched Eli lift his hand—not waving, exactly, but holding it there.

The bus pulled away. The sea was somewhere behind them, already making its own version of what had happened.
