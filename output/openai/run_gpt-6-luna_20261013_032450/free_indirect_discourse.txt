By the time Mara found the car keys, the others had already left for the beach.

“They’re in the fridge,” Theo said, from the doorway.

Of course they were. The keys had fallen from her tote sometime after she’d put the oranges in there, which had seemed sensible at the time. The residency kitchen smelled of cold coffee and seaweed, though the sea was twenty minutes away.

Theo held the passenger door open while she climbed in. He had packed nothing but a towel and the paperback he’d been pretending to read all week. Mara had brought sunscreen, two peaches, a sketchbook, and a bottle of water that leaked onto her lap before they reached the highway.

“Prepared,” he said.

“Always.”

They drove with the windows down. Theo’s elbow rested on the sill, his hand opening and closing in the wind. In four days he would fly to Berlin; in six, Mara would take the train home. Everyone at the residency spoke about departure as if it were a practical matter, like washing dishes. She was good at practical matters. She had not yet asked him whether he planned to call.

At the beach, the parking lot was full. They left the car half a mile away and carried their things over the dunes. Mara’s sandal snapped on the path.

“Don’t laugh,” she said.

“I’m not.”

He was. Not much, though. He crouched and fixed the strap with a twist of wire from his pocket, which he must have been carrying for some inexplicable reason. His fingers brushed her ankle. It would have been easy to say something. It was always easy, apparently, to say nothing.

They found a narrow strip of sand between a family with a bright orange tent and a couple asleep under matching hats. Mara laid out the towel. The wind took one corner and flung it over Theo’s head.

“Very elegant,” she said.

“I’m going to remember this beach as hostile.”

She wanted to tell him she would remember it as the last one. That sounded too much like a request.

They ate the peaches, which were bruised from the drive. Juice ran down Mara’s wrist. Theo passed her the water bottle, now empty, as if it might still be useful.

A wave came farther up the shore than either of them expected and soaked their shoes. Theo jumped back, laughing, and Mara stayed where she was. The cold water closed around her feet. For a moment she could imagine staying: another week of dinners, another walk to the studio, Theo’s music through the thin wall at night.

But the tide did not care what she imagined. It withdrew, dragging sand from beneath her heels.

On the drive back, Theo asked what time her train was on Friday.

“Ten fifteen.”

“I could come to the station.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

She looked out the window. The coast slid past in flashes between the trees. He could come. He could not. She could ask him to. The road was full of exits, each one clearly marked.

At the residency, he carried her broken sandal inside and set it by the door.

“See you at dinner?” he asked.

“Probably.”

He nodded, then went down the hall to his room. Mara stayed in the kitchen, listening to his footsteps fade. The fridge hummed around the keys. She put them on the counter this time. Then she went to her room and began packing, though there were still four days left.
