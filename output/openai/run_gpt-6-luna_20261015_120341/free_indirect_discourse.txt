### The Chair in the Water

At four o’clock, the rain stopped, but the gutters kept running.

Jonah stood beneath the pharmacy awning and watched the couple cross Bell Street. They were not young, though not old enough to have become invisible to one another. The man carried a paper sack against his chest. The woman walked beside him with her hands in the pockets of a blue coat. They stepped around a puddle at the same moment, not touching, not looking down. A little coordination like that. Nothing worth watching.

Still, Jonah watched them.

They stopped at the corner to let a delivery van pass. The woman said something. The man smiled, and she did too, a second later, as if the smile had taken the longer route. Then they continued toward the river.

It was only the rain that had made him notice them. People moved differently in rain: heads bowed, shoulders hunched, everyone intent on getting home. These two seemed to have nowhere urgent to be. Their quietness irritated him. Or no, not irritated. It made him want to know what they knew.

He had seen the chair that morning, dumped beside the bins on Claremont Avenue. A small wooden chair, the kind meant for a kitchen, with a curved back and arms polished by years of hands. Someone had burned it almost completely black. One leg was gone. The seat had split down the middle, and the wood around the crack curled up in brittle flakes. It looked as though it had been sitting in a fire, waiting patiently for somebody to come back for it.

Jonah had stopped to look at it. Why not? There was nothing else to do. He had imagined the fire—an accident, a spiteful neighbor, a cigarette dropped into a pile of laundry—and then felt foolish for giving the chair a history. He’d gone on, leaving it by the bins. There were always things abandoned in the city. People too, if you knew where to look.

The couple reached the next block. Jonah left the pharmacy awning and followed.

Not close. He wasn’t stupid. A bus shelter gave him cover, then a newsstand, then a row of parked cars. He could stop whenever he liked. They were simply going his way.

The man wore a gray scarf tucked neatly into his collar. The woman’s blue coat had a darker patch at the shoulder where the rain had soaked through. She carried no bag. He wondered whether she had left one at home or whether she owned so little she needed none. Her shoes were flat and sensible. She walked with a small inward turn of the left foot.

The man glanced back once, at a sound behind them. Not at Jonah. Of course not. He had nothing to be afraid of.

They turned down Mercer Street, then cut through a passage beside a shuttered bakery. Jonah knew this part of town well enough to know when he didn’t know it. The houses here leaned toward one another, their brick faces stained by soot and rain. There was a narrow canal at the far end, usually hidden behind a railing and a line of bins. Today the railing was already half underwater.

The couple stopped at a red door set between two shopfronts. The man took a key from his pocket. The woman waited, her face turned toward the street. She looked directly at Jonah.

Not at him, exactly. At the place where he was. Her eyes were dark and very still. The look lasted only a second, but the second opened out, became a room he could not leave. Then the key turned, the door swung inward, and they disappeared.

Jonah stood under the awning opposite, listening to the rain start again.

He knew he should go. He had followed them here. That was enough. More than enough. He was not the sort of person who needed to see what came next.

But the red door had no curtains in the upper window, and a pale light had appeared behind the glass. He could see the woman move through the room, then vanish. A moment later the man crossed the window carrying the paper sack. Their shadows slid over the wall. They were home. That was all. There they were, behind the glass, performing the little rituals everyone performed: setting down the groceries, taking off wet coats, turning on lamps. They would eat. They would sleep. In the morning, they would leave again.

Nothing worth watching.

Jonah began the walk back. He made it as far as the canal before the storm drain overflowed.

Water came foaming through the iron grates, carrying leaves and bits of paper. It ran over the pavement and around his shoes. The canal had risen above its stone banks and was beginning to spread through the narrow street. Jonah stepped onto the low wall, then onto the broad sill of a shop window. The current was not strong, but it was full of little things: a plastic bottle, a glove, a child’s red ball that bobbed away toward the red door.

The quickest route out was back the way he’d come. He could go home, put the kettle on, and tell himself he had been foolish. He could watch the news and hear them talk about the river as if the river were a problem belonging to someone else.

Instead, he turned toward Claremont Avenue.

He was careful, moving along the higher pavement, one hand against the walls. The water reached his ankles, then his shins. The rain blurred the streetlights, each one stretched into a trembling yellow line. He thought of the chair by the bins. He would have to pass it. He had already decided that.

There it was, halfway down the block, no longer beside the bins. The current had wrenched it loose and carried it into the road. It rolled slowly on its side, struck a parked car, then swung around. One of its blackened arms caught against the wheel. The chair’s curved back seemed to bow beneath the rain. A ragged strip of wood hung from the seat.

Jonah stopped.

At this distance, in the water, it looked less like furniture than the remains of something that had tried to stand and failed. He could see the pale wood beneath the char, a thin line of it showing where the arm had broken. It reminded him of the woman’s face at the window—no, that was absurd. She had been ordinary. She had worn a blue coat and sensible shoes. He had no right to make her into anything.

The chair came free of the car and drifted on.

Jonah followed it.

By the time he reached Mercer Street, the water was at his knees. It had closed over the canal railing. The red door stood above it on two shallow steps, the house’s lower windows shining out over the flood. Jonah moved into the shadow of a doorway opposite and watched.

A lamp burned behind the glass. The man crossed the room and drew the curtains, then seemed to reconsider. He opened them again, just a little. Perhaps the room was too warm. Perhaps the rain made him uneasy. The woman appeared behind him, carrying a white bowl. She set it down on a table and stood looking out.

Jonah could see the room clearly now. A couch. A narrow table. A lamp with a crooked shade. There was a chair beside the window, its wooden back curved like a crescent. The woman moved toward it and sat.

No. It wasn’t the same chair. This one was polished, intact, golden in the lamplight. A chair like the burned one, perhaps. A chair like any chair.

She sat very straight, her hands resting on the arms. Her shoulders were a little hunched, as if she were listening for something in the walls. The man sat on the couch behind her. He spoke. She didn’t turn.

Outside, the ruined chair drifted into the street. It bumped against the step and settled there, one leg lifted out of the water. Its charred back pointed toward the window.

The woman leaned forward in her chair. In the room’s warm light, her blue coat hung from the wall, its dark wet patch shining. She was wearing a pale sweater. Jonah could see the line of her shoulder, the turn of her head, the small inward tilt of her left foot beneath the hem of her skirt.

The charred chair shifted in the current. Its broken arm knocked against the stone step. Once. Twice.

The woman lifted her head.

Across the street, Jonah held his breath. The man had stopped speaking. He looked at her, then followed her gaze toward the window.

The woman rose from the chair and went to the glass. She put one hand against it. Jonah thought of her palm on the wet street, touching the invisible distance between them. She looked past the reflection of the lamp and into the flood.

The burned chair lifted, turned, and pressed its back against the red door. Its black curve lined up with the woman’s shadow in the window. For one long moment, the two shapes fitted together: the broken chair outside, the woman inside, both bowed beneath the same dark arch. Then the current tugged at the chair, and it slid away from the steps.

The woman remained at the glass.

Jonah felt the water pull at his knees. It was rising faster now, carrying grit and branches, tugging at his trouser legs as though someone beneath the street had taken hold of him. He should move. There was still time to get back to higher ground. The woman was watching a flood; that was all. The chair was only wood. The room was warm. The man was there with her.

She brought her other hand to the glass.

Jonah understood then—not what she was, or what had happened to her, but why he had followed. She had been waiting for someone to notice the place where she was. Someone to stand outside and see the shape she made.

The chair bumped against the far curb and turned slowly in the water, its scorched seat facing upward like a dark, empty face.

In the window, the woman smiled.

Jonah could not tell whether the smile was meant for him. He watched it until the flood reached his waist, until the lamplight shivered and broke across the water, until the red door and the woman and the chair all leaned together in the glass. Then the current caught his coat and pulled him away from the wall. The street slid sideways. The window climbed above him.

For a little while he could still see the woman standing there, one hand pressed to the pane.

Then the water closed over the sill, and the light went out.
