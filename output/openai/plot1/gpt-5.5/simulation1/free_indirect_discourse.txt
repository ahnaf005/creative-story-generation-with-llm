**The Chair in the Rain**

By the time the rain began in earnest, he had already noticed the chair.

It lay on its side beside the curb at the corner of Fane and Larch, where the pavement dipped and collected leaves, oil, cigarette ends, all the small dark things the city rinsed from itself. At first he thought it was an animal. Some large black dog struck down and left there, stiff-legged, its back arched. But when he drew closer, umbrella angled against the wind, he saw the splintered rungs, the curved back, the webbed seat burnt clean through. A kitchen chair. One of those old wooden ones with a little dignity left in it, even ruined.

Someone had set fire to it. Not entirely. The legs were charred halfway up, as though it had stood in flames and then been kicked over before it could finish dying. The rain had slicked it black. In the hollow where the seat should have been, water trembled.

He stopped longer than he needed to. People went around him, irritated, their collars turned up. A bus hissed past and threw dirty spray over the chair. Still it remained what it was, a burned chair in the rain, and not worth thinking about.

Not worth thinking about at all.

He went on.

He had no special destination. That was the truth of it, though if anyone had asked he might have invented an errand—bread, batteries, a prescription, something small and respectable. He had left his room because the walls were sweating and the air smelled of old carpet. Because the afternoon had become unbearable. Because from his window he could see only the blank flank of the building opposite, its bathroom windows glowing one by one as evening came on, each lit square proof that other people had arranged themselves into lives.

The streets were filling. Gutters ran brown. In the distance, a siren rose and fell, then was smothered by rain.

He first saw the couple outside the shuttered florist’s.

There was nothing remarkable about them. Later, this seemed the remarkable thing, that they had possessed so perfectly the camouflage of the ordinary. The man was tall and narrow, with a navy raincoat shining at the shoulders. He carried a paper bag against his chest, careful not to crush whatever was inside. The woman beside him wore no hat. Rain had darkened her hair and flattened it against her cheeks. She walked with her hands in the pockets of a green coat, her head slightly lowered, as though listening to something underground.

Husband and wife, probably. Or almost. They had that way of not touching and yet remaining connected, as if some invisible cord passed between their elbows. The man said something. The woman turned her face toward him. She did not smile, exactly; her mouth altered by a degree. It was enough. He felt, watching from beneath his umbrella, a small internal shift, a latch lifting.

There. That was all. There they were.

He slowed.

The couple passed the florist’s windows, where faded paper daffodils sagged behind glass. The man leaned close to the woman to speak over the rain. She shrugged. Ordinary. Entirely ordinary. But his eyes clung to them with the greedy relief of someone finding a lighted window after a long walk through fields.

Perhaps it was the way they moved together without ceremony. Perhaps it was the bag, held so carefully. Perhaps it was only that the woman’s wet hair had separated into black strands against her pale cheek, and for an instant she had looked not young or old but weathered, as if she had been standing in the rain for years before the man came along and claimed her.

He followed them.

Not followed. That was too deliberate a word. He happened to be walking the same way. Cities were full of coincidences; people flowed along the same channels. He kept several paces behind, then half a block, then nearer again when the crowd thickened. If they looked back—though why would they?—he would become a man with an umbrella, a face among other faces, his shoes wet through, his errands private.

The rain increased. Shop awnings drummed. Water slid over the curb and spread across the street in rippled sheets. At intersections, cars nosed through slowly, their tires sending up fans of gray water. The drains must have been clogged. Always the city was surprised by weather, as if rain were an innovation, as if water had no ancient claim.

The couple crossed Market Street against the light. The man reached back without looking, and the woman took his hand for the crossing only, releasing it on the far curb. That brief clasp struck him with unexpected force. There was the whole history of them in it, surely: quarrels in kitchens, sleep-warmed backs, the passing of cups, the old wounds pressed and released. He imagined their apartment—not grand, but warm. A table by a window. A lamp with a pleated shade. Books doubled on shelves. Two mugs in the sink. A plant refusing to die.

He had a room with a hot plate and one chair.

No. He would not think about his room.

They turned onto Larch.

He hesitated at the corner, because there, half-submerged now in the gutter, was the burned chair. He had circled back without noticing. The rainwater had risen around it so that it seemed to be floating, though one broken leg still snagged on the curb. Its charred back gleamed. The hollow seat held a trembling pool that overflowed with each new gust.

The woman noticed it too.

She stopped.

The man took two steps before realizing she was no longer beside him. He turned, called something. She did not answer. She stood in the rain looking down at the chair. Her face, which had been ordinary—tired, damp, slightly impatient—went still.

He, the watcher, halted behind a parked van.

Now what? Now she would say, What a shame, or Some people, or Nothing lasts. She would lift her shoulders and continue. But she did not. She stared at the burned chair with a concentration so intimate that he felt embarrassed, as though she had been caught reading a letter meant only for her.

The man came back. He touched her sleeve. She flinched—not much. Enough.

Then she laughed.

It was not loud enough to carry properly through the rain, but he saw it in her throat, the white flash of her teeth. The man did not laugh. He looked at the chair, then at her. She said something. He shook his head. She bent slightly, as if to touch the ruined back, but the man caught her wrist. Not roughly. Carefully. The way one might stop a sleepwalker.

The watcher felt his own wrist tighten.

There was something here. Of course there was. One did not stop in the rain for a burned chair unless it had spoken. One did not laugh like that—soundless, open-mouthed, almost delighted—unless some private door had blown wide.

The couple moved on.

He followed again, no longer pretending to himself that he had business in that direction. If he turned away now, the rest of the evening would close around him like wet cloth. He would return to his room and sit on his one chair and hear water crawling through the pipes. Better to walk. Better to know where they went. Not for any wicked reason. He meant them no harm. He was only curious, and curiosity was a hunger more refined than hunger.

They lived in one of the old brick houses near the canal, where the front steps rose steeply from the pavement and basement windows crouched behind iron grates. The neighborhood had been fashionable once, then poor, then fashionable again in patches, according to the whims of people who liked exposed beams and proximity to decay. The canal ran behind the houses, black and swollen, licking at the stone embankment.

Number 17. He saw the man take keys from his pocket. The woman stood one step below, looking back down the street, not at him—surely not at him—but at the rain, the gutters, the water rushing past the tires of parked cars. Her face lifted. For a moment the streetlight caught her eyes and made them shine as though filled to the brim.

Then she went inside.

The door closed.

He should go home.

Instead he crossed the street and stood beneath the plane tree opposite. The branches gave little shelter. Rain struck the leaves and gathered in drops that fell cold down his neck. The front windows of Number 17 were curtained in gauze, but not entirely. Through the lower panes he could see light bloom, then deepen. Shadows moved.

He told himself he would wait only until his cigarette was finished, though he did not smoke. An old phrase from men in films. He stood with his hands in his pockets and watched.

The front room appeared in fragments. A lamp lit. The man entered carrying the paper bag, now darkened by rain. He set it on a table. The woman came in after him and removed her green coat. Under it she wore a black dress or blouse—some dark garment that made her head seem disembodied, pale and wet-haired. The man said something, gesturing toward the kitchen. She did not answer. She stood in the center of the room, arms hanging loosely, water dripping from her hair onto her shoulders.

Take off your shoes, he thought absurdly. You’ll ruin the floor.

The man disappeared. The woman remained. She turned slowly and looked toward the window.

He stepped back behind the tree.

Impossible that she could see him. The glass held the room’s light; outside was rain and dark. Still, her gaze seemed to pass over him, not searching, not startled, but recognizing. As if he belonged there. As if he had always been expected outside in the weather.

The rain became heavier, then changed character. It was no longer falling in drops but in thick slanting cords. Water began to gather over the pavement, first in shining patches, then in a continuous trembling skin. At the end of the street, someone shouted. A car alarm chirped and died. The canal, behind the houses, made itself known with a low, pushing sound.

He remembered notices pasted near the bridge that morning: FLOOD WARNING. He had glanced at them and felt only the mild contempt one reserves for official anxiety. The city exaggerating again. Men in yellow vests longing for catastrophe. But now water was rising over the curb. It slipped around his shoes, cold through the leather.

Inside, the man returned with a towel. He placed it around the woman’s shoulders. She did not lift her hands to hold it. He rubbed her hair awkwardly, tenderly. The watcher could not look away. There was something indecent in tenderness when seen from outside, something that accused the one who watched.

The woman suddenly caught the towel and pulled it from his hands. She said something sharp. The man stepped back. His face, turned briefly toward the window, was tired, not angry. Very tired. He picked up the paper bag and took from it a round loaf of bread, then a small bottle, then a bunch of white flowers wrapped in cellophane.

Flowers. From the closed florist? No, perhaps from somewhere else. For dinner, then. An apology. An anniversary. A ritual to keep the house from sinking.

The woman laughed again.

This time he heard it faintly through the glass and rain. Not laughter after all, maybe. A bark. A gasp. She pointed to the flowers, then to herself, then toward the street. Toward the corner where the chair lay burning no longer, drowning now.

The man shook his head. He reached for her. She backed away.

Water climbed over the watcher’s ankles.

He should move to higher ground. Anyone sensible would. Already the street had become a shallow stream, current tugging along the gutter. A plastic bottle spun past. Leaves plastered themselves against his trouser legs. Farther down, two people were dragging sandbags from a doorway. Someone yelled that the canal had gone over.

But Number 17 glowed. The room held him.

The woman stood near the fireplace now, one hand on the mantel. Her black sleeves clung to her arms. The damp ends of her hair curved inward, touching her cheeks like scorched wood curling in heat. There it was—the thought came whole, absurd, and undeniable. The chair. She looked like the chair.

Not in shape. Not crudely. It was in the blackened elegance of her, the way some inner fire had passed through and left the outside standing. The hollow where the chair’s seat had been was in her face when she turned from the man, that burned-out absence carefully balanced on four legs of habit. Had the man found her like that, years ago? Had he carried her home from some curb, propped her by the window, mistaken survival for repair?

What a foolish thought. A melodramatic thought. Rain encouraged such things. Loneliness encouraged worse.

Yet once seen, the likeness thickened. The charred chair at the corner had not been discarded; it had been sent ahead as a sign. Or it had escaped from this house, thrown out after some night of flame. Or the woman herself had burned there and walked on, leaving the chair as a husk.

Inside, she lifted the flowers and tore the cellophane away. The white blossoms fell onto the table. She took one stem, snapped it, then another. The man said her name. The watcher could not hear it, but he saw the shape of it: two syllables, perhaps. Anna. Clara. Helen. Names for women in lit rooms, not creatures made of rain and ash.

She turned again toward the window.

This time she came closer.

He could see her clearly now through the gauze curtain. Her eyes were not shining but dark, fixed. She raised one hand and pressed her palm to the glass.

He knew, with a sensation almost of relief, that she saw him.

The man did not notice. He was gathering broken flowers from the table, his shoulders bent. The woman’s palm remained on the pane. Slowly, very slowly, she smiled.

It was not an invitation. It was not a warning. It was recognition, and that was worse.

Water reached his calves.

The current had strengthened. It moved around the tree in small muscular waves. His umbrella had turned useless, tugged by the wind until he folded it and held it at his side like a broken wing. Rain ran down his face into his mouth. It tasted of metal.

From the corner came a knocking sound.

He looked.

The burned chair had broken free.

It came slowly at first, bobbing in the flooded gutter, then faster as the current caught it. On its side, black legs jutting, it drifted down Larch toward him and the house. It struck a parked car, turned, scraped along the curb. The hollow seat filled and emptied, filled and emptied, as if breathing.

He laughed then, or tried to. Of course. Now the chair would arrive at Number 17, and all would be explained. The woman would open the door. She would bring it in. The man would say, Not again, not tonight, and she would set it at the table where it belonged. There had always been three of them.

The chair bumped against the plane tree and lodged there, inches from his knees.

Close up, it looked less like furniture than remains. The curved back was blistered; one spindle had burned to a claw. A smell rose from it despite the rain—wet soot, old smoke, something cooked past usefulness. He stared down at it, and the water pressed it against him as if urging him to take hold.

Inside the house, the woman’s hand slid down the glass, leaving a pale smear.

Then the lights went out.

The whole street seemed to inhale. Windows vanished. The lamps along the pavement flickered, failed, returned dimly, failed again. In the sudden dark, the rain became enormous. Shouts rose from nearby houses. A dog barked from an upper floor.

He could still see Number 17, but only as a deeper darkness with rectangles in it. Then, from within, a small flame appeared.

A candle. The man’s face bent over it, briefly monstrous from below. The woman stood behind him. The flame caught her features one by one: cheekbone, mouth, wet hair, eyes. She seemed not illuminated by the candle but lit from the same source that had burned the chair. Not warm fire. The other kind. The kind that consumes without comforting.

The front door opened.

He did not move.

The man appeared first, holding the candle cupped in one hand. “Who’s there?” he called.

The watcher might have answered. Might have said, No one. Might have stepped forward and apologized, invented something about the flood, the danger, a warning. But his throat closed. He stood in water to his knees, the burned chair pinning him lightly to the tree, and watched the doorway.

The woman came behind the man. She had put her green coat back on though it hung open, dark dress beneath. Her bare feet were visible on the threshold. Bare feet, in this weather. The candle shook in the man’s hand.

“Do you need help?” the man called.

A decent man, then. Decent and tired. Still offering help to a stranger half-hidden under a tree in a flood.

The woman looked past him, not at the watcher’s face, but down at the chair.

Her expression changed to such tenderness that he felt suddenly ashamed. It was the look one gave a child asleep in a place it should not have slept. Poor thing. Poor ruined thing. Found at last.

She stepped out.

The man caught her arm. “Don’t.”

She pulled free and descended one step, then another, until the floodwater covered her ankles. The candlelight behind her framed her body; rain silvered her hair. She moved toward the tree.

Toward him.

No, toward the chair.

The current shoved at her legs, but she did not falter. The man shouted her name. Clara—yes, perhaps Clara. The watcher could not be sure. She came within arm’s length and bent, placing both hands on the chair’s scorched back.

He smelled her then, or imagined he did: rainwater, smoke, a faint floral sweetness crushed underfoot.

She looked up at him.

There was no madness in her face. That disappointed him somehow. No trance, no possession, no ghostly revelation. Only recognition, steady and practical. As though he were the one who was out of place. As though anyone could see the obvious: burned things must be brought in from the flood.

“Help me,” she said.

Her voice was low, roughened.

He obeyed.

Together they lifted the chair from the water. It was heavier than it looked, soaked through, and black water poured from the ruined seat. His fingers sank into softened char. The woman held the other side close to her body, heedless of the soot staining her coat. For a moment they stood joined by the chair, facing each other in the rain.

The man remained on the steps, candle flame flaring and shrinking.

“Clara,” he said, and now the name was clear.

She smiled at the watcher then—not with gratitude, not with secrecy, but with a kind of weary politeness. Thank you for returning what was never yours. Thank you for standing in the rain long enough to understand nothing.

They carried the chair to the steps. The man set down the candle and took it from them, grimacing at the wet ash on his hands. He did not ask questions. Perhaps there had been too many already. Perhaps every marriage was a house into which burned furniture was carried from time to time, while strangers looked on and invented fires.

At the threshold, Clara paused and turned back.

The floodwater ran past the house, past the tree, past his legs, dragging leaves, bottles, a child’s red ball, all the buoyant evidence of other lives. Behind him the city blurred in rain. Before him the doorway glowed.

For one wild second he thought she might invite him in. A towel, a phone call, a place by the lamp until the waters fell. He would sit in their front room, on the recovered chair perhaps, and tell them he had only been walking. They would believe him or not. Bread would be cut. Flowers, though broken, would be placed in a glass.

But Clara only looked at him as if through glass still.

Then she went inside.

The door closed.

He remained beneath the tree until the water reached his thighs and the chair, no longer beside him, existed only as a black shape behind the curtains. After a while the candle moved across the room and stopped near the window. Shadows bent over something. The man’s, the woman’s. The chair’s, perhaps, upright again.

He imagined it at the table, dripping onto the floorboards. He imagined Clara seated opposite it, her hands folded, speaking softly to its burned back. He imagined the man in the kitchen, cutting bread with careful, exhausted strokes.

The rain went on.

At last he turned toward home, though the way was flooded and uncertain. His shoes lifted heavily from the water. At the corner of Fane and Larch, where the chair had first lain, nothing remained but a faint black smear on the curb, already thinning under the rain.

He stopped to look at it.

Not worth thinking about, he told himself.

But he knew that when he returned to his room, if his room was still there, he would find his own chair waiting in the dimness. Plain, unburned, pushed under the little table. He would stand in the doorway and look at it a long time before sitting down. And if in the night he smelled smoke, if he woke to the sound of water rising in the walls, he would not be surprised.

Some things were discarded.

Some things came home.
