At noon, before the rain became serious, I saw the chair.

It sat at the curb outside the old bakery, angled as if someone had dragged it there and lost interest. Once, perhaps, it had belonged to a dining set: high-backed, wooden, with fluted legs and a carved crest like a shell at the top. Now the seat was caved in, the back splintered, one arm blackened to charcoal. It had been burned unevenly, not by accident and not entirely. The right side remained the color of honey beneath its varnish, polished by hands that were long gone, while the left was blistered and scorched to a dull, scaly black.

The strange thing was that I knew, standing over it with rain stippling my shoulders, that it had been loved.

No one else noticed it. People passed, steering umbrellas through the narrow street, their faces folded against the weather. A delivery van hissed by, sending a fan of dirty water over the curb. The chair did not move. Its single intact arm extended toward the road like a gesture arrested halfway between welcome and warning.

I was still looking at it when the couple appeared.

They came out of the florist’s across the street, though they carried no flowers. They were ordinary in the way that certain people are ordinary only at a distance. He was tall and narrow, with a gray raincoat buttoned wrong at the throat. His hair was flattened dark against his head. She was shorter, wearing a yellow coat that would have been cheerful in another light, and a scarf tied beneath her chin. They stood for a moment beneath the striped awning, watching the rain as if it were a performance arranged for them.

Then the woman laughed.

It was a small laugh, almost swallowed by traffic, but it crossed the street and entered me cleanly. Not because it was beautiful. It was not. It was dry and clicking, like a match struck in an empty room.

The man looked at her, and his expression changed. That was what held me: not the laugh, but his face when he heard it. It opened with such naked fear, such pleading devotion, that I felt I had seen him without skin. He reached for her hand. She did not give it to him immediately. She looked past him, across the street, directly toward me.

No—toward the chair.

Her face was calm. Pale, oval, unremarkable. Yet as she looked at the burned chair, something in her seemed to lean forward from very far away. The rain darkened her scarf. A drop slid from her chin.

The man said something. She turned to him, smiled, and placed her hand in his.

They walked away.

I do not know why I followed. It is possible to say I was going in the same direction, that the rain had made taxis scarce, that my apartment lay somewhere north and they were walking north. These are facts with no truth in them. I followed because the moment they moved, I felt the world threaten to close over them, as water closes over a stone, and I could not bear to lose sight of them.

The city was already flooding in its usual ways. Gutters filled first, then crosswalks, then the shallow dips where the road had settled over old pipes. People cursed, stepped around puddles, lifted children over oily streams. The rain thickened until buildings on the far side of the street blurred like scenery painted on wet paper.

The couple walked slowly. He shortened his stride for her, though she did not seem frail. Now and then he bent his head to speak; she answered rarely. When she did, he would look relieved for several steps, then worried again. They did not argue. They did not kiss. They moved with the intimacy of people who know exactly how much space grief requires between their bodies.

At the corner of Mercy Street, she paused.

There was a blocked drain there, and water had gathered around the curb in a brown pool where cigarette ends and leaves revolved. A child in red boots stamped at the edge until his mother tugged him away. The woman in the yellow coat watched the spinning debris. Slowly, without looking down, she lifted one foot and placed it into the water.

The man tightened. His hand gripped hers.

“It’s only water,” she said.

I was close enough then to hear. Her voice was low and pleasant. Nothing like her laugh.

He said, “Not today.”

She looked at him, amused. “When, then?”

He did not answer. After a moment she stepped out of the puddle, leaving a pale crescent of foam swirling where her shoe had been. They continued walking.

I followed them through streets I knew and did not know. Rain changes a city’s map. Alleys open where walls were; familiar houses withdraw behind veils. Twice I thought they had seen me and twice I ducked behind delivery trucks or the dark glass of shuttered shops. I was ashamed, but not enough to stop. Something had begun, though I could not name it. The couple had the terrible magnetism of a door left open at night.

By the time they reached their house, the flood warnings were being carried from phone to phone. Sirens wailed and faded. The river, people were saying, had climbed the embankment. The lower streets were closed. The drains were overwhelmed. Still the rain came, hard now, hammering the pavement until the whole city seemed to smoke.

Their house stood at the end of a narrow lane near the canal, one of four brick terraces pressed shoulder to shoulder. Its windows were tall and old-fashioned, with white-painted frames peeling at the corners. A plane tree leaned over the roof, thrashing its black branches in the downpour. The couple climbed the three steps to the door. The man fumbled with keys. She stood behind him and turned once, looking back down the lane.

I stepped into the shadow of the plane tree.

Her gaze moved over the place where I stood. I thought of the chair again: that intact arm extended, the burned side turned to weather. In the rain, her yellow coat looked darker, almost brown, and the scarf under her chin clung like smoke.

The door opened. Warm light spilled out. They went inside.

For several minutes I remained under the tree, soaked through, listening to the rain explode in the leaves above me. I should have gone home. Instead I crossed the lane and stood beneath their front window.

There are acts that, before one commits them, seem impossible for any decent person. Afterward, the world rearranges itself to include them. I looked in.

The room was modest and bright. A green sofa faced the window, and beside it stood a small table with a lamp shaped like a lily. Books lined one wall. On the mantelpiece were photographs too small for me to make out. The man removed his coat and hung it carefully by the door. The woman did not remove hers. She stood in the center of the room, dripping steadily onto the rug.

He spoke to her. She shook her head.

He approached, hands slightly raised, as one might approach an animal in pain. She allowed him to untie the scarf beneath her chin. Beneath it, her neck was thin and white. There was a mark there—a dark crescent just below the ear. At first I thought it was a bruise. Then she turned and the lamplight caught it.

It was not purple, but black.

Charred.

The man saw it too. Or rather, he knew it was there and could not help looking. His face crumpled. He pressed his hand gently over the mark, as if to cover it from the room, from me, from whatever memory had made it.

She smiled.

Water ran from her coat in tiny streams.

The street behind me had become a shallow river. I heard it before I looked: a continuous rushing at ankle height. Rainwater poured down the lane toward the canal, but the canal must have risen, for the water was coming back up too, nosing over the curb, filling the hollows between stones. It lapped at the couple’s front steps. A plastic bottle sailed past, followed by a branch heavy with leaves.

Inside, the man had fetched towels. He knelt at the woman’s feet and began to dry her shoes. She looked down at him with an expression I could not read. Tenderness, perhaps. Or appetite.

Then she lifted her eyes and saw me.

I froze. The window glass held both our reflections: her face in the warm room and mine floating outside, pale and distorted by rain. She did not cry out. She did not alert the man. She raised one finger to her lips.

Not to silence me. To share the silence.

Behind her, on the wall above the mantelpiece, hung a photograph in a narrow black frame. I had not noticed it before. A group of people stood in summer sunlight around a long table in a garden. There were plates, bottles, someone’s raised hand blurred in motion. At the head of the table sat the woman, younger or unchanged, wearing a white dress. Beneath her was a wooden chair with a carved shell at the crest.

The same chair.

I do not mean one like it. I mean the chair from the curb, whole then, polished and proud beneath her white arms.

The woman watched me understand. Her smile widened a fraction.

The man, still kneeling, took her wet hands in his. He was speaking rapidly now, urgently. I could not hear through the rain and glass, but I saw the words form around despair. Please. Stay. Not again. Something like that. He kissed her fingers. She let him.

The water climbed over my shoes.

It was bitterly cold. I stepped back from the window, but the lane had altered while I watched. The current moved with purpose now, sliding around the trunks of trees, swallowing the bottom step of every house. Across the way, a basement window glowed underwater like an aquarium lamp. Somewhere a car alarm began bleating and was abruptly drowned.

I should have fled to higher ground. Instead I remained, calf-deep, staring into that room.

The woman finally removed her yellow coat. Beneath it she wore a dark dress, plain, close-fitting. Her arms were bare. Along the left arm, from shoulder to wrist, the skin was mottled black and red in patterns too regular to be scars from any ordinary fire. They resembled wood grain after burning: long streaks of soot, pale lines where heat had split the surface, a glossy crackle at the elbow.

The man stood quickly and tried to take the coat from her. She held it away, teasingly. A childish gesture. Then, with great care, she draped it over the back of a chair near the hearth.

Only then did I notice the empty place in the room.

The dining table had three chairs around it, though it had been built for four. On one side, the space gaped. The floorboards there were darker, as if something had stood for a long time and been removed only recently. The woman placed her hand over that absence. Her fingers curled, gripping air.

Outside, something struck my leg beneath the water. I reached down without thinking and caught it before the current carried it on.

A piece of wood.

Blackened. Curved. One end carved into the suggestion of a shell.

It was part of the burned chair.

I dropped it with a cry, but the water did not take it away. It knocked against my shin, returned, knocked again, insistent as an animal. More fragments came after it: a rung, a splintered leg, a strip of varnished wood unburned on one side. They circled me in the brown water, gathering.

From the window, the woman laughed.

That dry match-strike sound.

The man heard it and turned. Perhaps he saw me then, or perhaps he saw only what floated outside: pieces of the chair assembling themselves in the flood, tapping at the submerged step, the door, the brick wall. His mouth opened. He ran toward the window and pressed both palms against the glass.

“Go!” he shouted.

This I heard.

The woman moved behind him. She placed her burned arm around his waist and rested her cheek between his shoulder blades. The gesture was tender enough to break the heart. He shut his eyes. For one moment they looked like any couple in any storm: afraid, clinging, made small by weather.

Then the lamp flickered.

The floodwater reached my knees. It was no longer merely rain. It smelled of river mud and cellars, of rust, rot, and extinguished fires. The current tugged at me. I began to move backward, step by step, but the fragments of chair followed, bumping against me softly.

Inside, the woman lifted her hand from the man’s waist and waved.

Not farewell. Invitation.

The front door shuddered as water struck it. Once. Twice. The man tore himself from her and rushed to push towels along the threshold. He worked frantically, though the water was already seeping beneath, darkening the wood. The woman stood in the middle of the room, arms open slightly, palms forward, as if feeling warmth from an invisible blaze.

The water around my legs warmed.

Impossible, but true. In the midst of cold rain and flood, heat bloomed around the floating shards. Steam rose in faint threads. The blackened wood hissed. I looked down and saw, beneath the muddy surface, a glow threading through the pieces like embers stirred in ash.

I thought then—not in words, but in the old animal part of the mind—that the chair had not been discarded. It had escaped.

Or she had.

The flood surged suddenly to my thighs. I lost my footing and caught the iron railing by the steps. Water roared past, carrying flowerpots, newspapers, a single white shoe. The door of the house gave a cracking groan. The man turned toward the woman, and in his face I saw the same expression he had worn outside the florist’s: fear made indistinguishable from love.

She held out her hand.

He went to her.

The lights failed.

In darkness, the window became a mirror. I saw only myself: drenched, wild-eyed, clinging to the railing while the city filled around me. Behind my reflection, something moved—yellow, black, a pale face lowering toward glass.

A sound came from inside the house, though no window was open. It was the scrape of wooden legs across a floor.

I let go.

The current took me down the lane, spinning me away from the window, the house, the woman, the man. I struck a wall, swallowed water, surfaced beneath the thrashing plane tree. People were shouting somewhere. A rescue light swung across the rain. Hands hauled me onto the back of a truck already crowded with soaked strangers.

As we pulled away, I looked once more toward the terrace.

Water covered the steps. It pressed against the lower half of the door. In the front window, lit by no lamp I could see, sat a chair.

Whole. High-backed. Polished on one side, charred on the other.

A woman sat in it, or seemed to. Perhaps it was only shadow and rain on glass. Her yellow coat hung from her shoulders. One blackened arm rested on the chair’s burned arm, fitting it exactly, as if both had been shaped by the same fire.

Beside her stood the man, his hand on the back of the chair, his face turned down toward her with adoration or terror. The water rose between us. The truck lurched forward. The window slid from view.

By morning the flood had begun to fall. They found no bodies in the house at the end of the lane. The neighbors said no couple had lived there for years. The last tenant, an old widower, had died the previous winter. Before that—well, people remembered a fire, though they disagreed about when it had happened. Some said ten years ago. Some said thirty. One woman claimed there had been a garden party, a quarrel, a chair thrown onto a bonfire, and a wife who laughed until her dress caught.

But no one agreed. Floods stir up stories along with sewage and silt.

The chair at the curb outside the bakery was gone.

For weeks afterward I avoided Mercy Street. I took long routes home, crossed bridges instead of canals, refused to look into ground-floor windows after dark. Yet whenever rain began, even lightly, I smelled scorched varnish. In puddles, I sometimes saw a yellow shape pass behind me where no one stood.

And once, outside a shop after closing, I found a splinter of blackened wood in my coat pocket.

It was warm.
