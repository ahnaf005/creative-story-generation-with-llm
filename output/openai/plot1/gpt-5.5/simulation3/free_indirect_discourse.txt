By four o’clock the rain had simplified the city. It erased the far buildings, softened the traffic lights into colored bruises, pressed pedestrians under awnings until they seemed to have been sorted there by type: office workers, students, delivery boys, the elderly with plastic bags tied over their hats. Water ran along the gutters in nervous, shining ropes.

Mara had nowhere to be, which was not the same as being free. She had left the office at two with a headache she had invented and then acquired. Since then she had been walking, crossing at corners because corners presented themselves, turning down streets because the rain seemed to lean that way. Her umbrella had inverted twice and was now clutched beside her like a crippled black bird. Her shoes made that soft, private sound of water coming through.

On Finch Street, beside the shuttered upholstery shop, someone had put out a chair for the trash.

It was not, at first glance, worth noticing. The city discarded chairs daily, chairs with sprung bellies and torn arms, chairs collapsed in attitudes of apology. But this one had burned. Its back was charred down one side; the left arm had blistered and curled like a blackened hand. The upholstery—once floral, perhaps, though the pattern had been reduced to a stain of browns and yellows—clung in patches to the frame. Rain gathered in its seat, reflecting a piece of gray sky.

Mara stopped before it. She did not know why. Maybe because it had the look of something that had survived badly, not escaped. Maybe because the chair, even ruined, suggested a body: the splayed legs, the stubborn back, the hollow lap filling with water. There it sat on the curb, patient as any commuter, as if waiting for someone late.

Poor thing, she thought, and immediately disliked herself for the tenderness. A burned chair did not suffer. A chair had no use for pity. Still, the rain tapped its glossy puddled seat with a sound almost like breathing.

That was when the couple passed.

They came from behind her and moved around the chair without slowing. Man and woman, neither young nor old, both under a single large umbrella the man held too high. He was tall in a tan raincoat, narrow at the shoulders, his trousers darkened at the cuffs. The woman walked close enough to him that their sleeves touched, though she did not hold his arm. Her hair, black and cut bluntly at her jaw, was tucked behind one ear. She wore a green coat buttoned to the throat and red shoes impractical for the weather.

There was nothing remarkable about them. That was the remarkable thing. They were the kind of couple one passed and forgot before the next storefront. But as they stepped around the burned chair, the woman turned her head.

She looked down at it.

Only for a moment. Her face, pale under the umbrella’s shadow, changed in a way Mara could not name. Not surprise. Not disgust. Recognition, perhaps, though that was absurd. One did not recognize trash unless one had put it there, and even then the recognition was dull and domestic. This was sharper. Her mouth parted slightly, and the rain, blown sideways, caught on her cheek like ash.

The man said something. The woman looked away. They continued down Finch Street.

Mara remained beside the chair, watching the green coat recede between the wet shopfronts.

She should have gone home then. There was soup in the refrigerator, a towel over the radiator, a phone call from her sister she had not returned. Instead she found herself walking after them, slowly at first, then with the careful pace of a person pretending not to follow anyone. The city allowed such pretenses. Everyone was going somewhere; everyone was behind someone else.

It was not following, exactly. It was curiosity with legs. It was the rain, making all directions equally disagreeable. It was the headache, pulsing behind one eye, and the strange kindness she had felt for the ruined chair. Anyway, the couple did not look back.

They crossed Meridian Avenue against the light. Mara waited on the curb while buses heaved past, their windows fogged with anonymous faces. For several seconds she lost them, and panic, ridiculous and immediate, rose in her throat. Then she saw the umbrella, black and glossy, bobbing beyond the bus shelter.

There they were.

Good. As if she had a duty to keep them in sight. As if something might happen if she did not.

The rain thickened. Water in the street no longer flowed only in gutters but spread across the asphalt in trembling sheets. At intersections, drains gulped and failed. Taxi tires hissed through brown waves. Shopkeepers stood in doorways, looking down at the water with resigned accusation, as though it were a customer trying to return something without a receipt.

The couple turned north into a quieter neighborhood of brick row houses, small front gardens, iron railings painted black. Here the street sloped toward the river, though the river itself was six blocks away and hidden by warehouses. Mara knew the area vaguely. An aunt had lived here years ago, before the rents climbed and the aunt fell. On dry evenings, families sat on stoops; children chalked moons and ladders on the pavement. Today the stoops were empty and the chalk had become colored milk.

The man and woman stopped before a narrow house with blue shutters. The woman searched in her bag for keys. The man folded the umbrella and shook it once, violently, so water leapt from it like beads thrown from a torn necklace. He looked up the street, not at Mara but through her, and she turned at once toward a basement window, studying nothing. Her heart beat with such force she could feel it in the soaked seams of her shoes.

What was she doing? She could still leave. She could laugh at herself later: the day she stalked a perfectly ordinary couple because a woman glanced at a piece of garbage. She could make it a story at dinner, if there were dinners, if there were people waiting to hear her speak. But the key had turned, and the door opened, and warm yellow light appeared inside the house.

The woman stepped over the threshold. Before following, she paused and looked back down the street.

This time she saw Mara.

Or nearly saw her. Her eyes moved over the railings, the rain, the facades darkened with water. For an instant they seemed to rest on the place where Mara stood. Then the man touched her shoulder, and she went in. The door closed.

Mara stood under a dripping plane tree opposite the house. Water slid from the leaves onto the back of her neck. A car alarm sounded somewhere distant and gave up. The house showed two lit windows on the ground floor, both curtained with gauzy white fabric. Through them figures moved in soft fragments: an arm, a shoulder, the darker block of the man’s head.

Go home, she told herself.

But where did they know the chair from? No—why had the woman looked at it like that? The question had entered Mara and begun arranging furniture. It would not let her pass. Perhaps the chair had belonged to them. Perhaps there had been a fire. Perhaps someone had died sitting in it, which was melodrama, which was exactly what made it plausible in a city where every closed door contained a melodrama efficiently disguised as rent, laundry, dinner.

She crossed the street.

The small front garden was paved with slate, slick and uneven, bordered by drowned lavender. There was no fence, only a low iron gate that had been left unlatched. Mara opened it with two fingers. It squealed, but the rain covered the sound, or seemed to. She stepped inside and stood near the hedge, where she could see through a gap at the edge of the curtain.

Inside, a lamp with a pleated shade glowed beside a bookcase. The room was orderly, old-fashioned: a red rug, framed prints of birds, a mantel crowded with porcelain dogs. The man had removed his raincoat and now wore a gray sweater. He was setting something on a table—mail, perhaps. The woman stood in the center of the room without taking off her green coat. Her red shoes shone wetly on the rug.

The man spoke. His mouth moved in irritation or fatigue. The woman did not answer. She lifted her hands slowly to her throat, unbuttoned the top button of her coat, then stopped.

Mara leaned closer. Rain ran down her forehead into her eyelashes. She wiped it away and left a smear of grit.

The woman turned toward the window. Mara drew back, breath held. But the curtain hid her. It was absurd to hide from someone whose house one had approached like a thief. Absurd, yes, but not shameful exactly. She felt no desire to steal, no malice. She was only watching. People watched birds, fires, tides. People watched when ambulances stopped and when strangers argued in restaurants. Watching was a human tax, paid in attention.

The man left the room. The woman remained.

Now that Mara had a better view of her, she saw how still she was. Not resting-still, but held. Her face in the lamplight had the waxy calm of a museum figure. Slowly she removed her coat. Under it she wore a cream dress, plain, short-sleeved, too light for the season. Her arms were thin. On the inside of her left forearm, from wrist to elbow, ran a dark mark.

Mara blinked.

At first she thought it was a shadow from the curtain. Then the woman shifted, and the mark remained. It was not one mark but many: glossy, puckered scars, the skin gathered and roped, darker at the edges. Burns. Old burns.

The chair came back to Mara so sharply she almost turned to look for it in the garden. The charred left arm. The blistered wood. The curled black hand. A burned chair at the curb, its seat filled with rain; a woman with fire written along her arm, pausing as if greeting a relative.

There it was, then. Explanation. Not a supernatural thing, not even a mystery. There had been a fire; she had survived; they had thrown out the chair at last. She had looked because it was hers, or had been near hers, or had watched her suffer in that mute furniture way. People attached themselves to objects that had witnessed them. A coat worn to a funeral. A cup from a hospital cafeteria. A chair that burned when a woman burned.

Mara should have felt satisfied. Instead she felt cheated. The explanation was too clean. It left out the woman’s face on Finch Street, the look of recognition not of an owner but of a twin.

The man returned carrying two glasses. He gave one to the woman. She did not take it. He spoke again, more sharply. The woman lifted her burned arm and pointed toward the window.

Mara froze.

Not at the window. Past it. Out into the rain.

The man looked. Mara crouched instinctively behind the hedge. Water soaked through the knees of her trousers. She pressed a hand over her mouth, as though breath might show like smoke.

When she dared to rise, both figures were gone from the room.

The front door opened.

Mara fled the garden, not running exactly—running would confess too much—but moving fast, slipping on the slate, catching the gate with her hip so it clanged behind her. She crossed to the plane tree and continued past it, head down, as if she were simply another drenched pedestrian hurrying through weather. Behind her, the man’s voice called something. Not words she could distinguish. The rain tore them apart.

She turned the corner and stopped beneath the awning of a closed bakery. Her breath came hard. Her knees were filthy. She began to laugh, quietly and without humor. Serves you right. Spy on strangers and be startled to discover they have eyes.

For several minutes she stayed there, watching water pour from the awning in a steady glass rope. The street beyond was changing. The curb had vanished. Water spread from wall to wall, shallow but insistent, carrying leaves, cigarette butts, a child’s pink sandal. Somewhere below the asphalt, drains gurgled with the sound of animals drowning in burrows.

A siren wailed toward the river.

Mara checked her phone. No signal. The screen showed two missed calls from her sister and a weather alert she had ignored: FLASH FLOOD WARNING. SEEK HIGHER GROUND. The words seemed dramatic, official, meant for other people. Yet water had already covered the tops of her shoes.

She should go uphill. She knew this. North led lower, toward the river; south, back past Finch Street, climbed gradually toward the station. But the blue-shuttered house was only one block away, and the woman’s scarred arm had pointed outward, accusing or pleading. Had she pointed at Mara? Or at something behind Mara? Perhaps the chair. Impossible; Finch Street lay far behind. Still, the chair sat vividly in her mind, collecting rain in its lap, rising inch by inch as the city filled.

When Mara stepped back into the street, she went the wrong way.

By the time she reached the corner, the water was mid-calf. It was cold and shockingly strong, tugging at her legs with each passing car’s wake. No cars moved now except one van stalled diagonally, hazards blinking red under the water’s restless skin. People shouted from doorways. A man carried a small white dog above his head like an offering.

At the blue-shuttered house, lights blazed on all floors.

The front door was open. The man stood on the top step, water swirling at the bottom one. He had put on boots and held the black umbrella uselessly at his side. He was looking not at the flood but at the woman, who stood in the garden.

She wore no coat. The cream dress clung to her body, transparent in places, and her hair was plastered to her cheeks. She stood ankle-deep among the drowned lavender, calm as a statue in a fountain. The man shouted. She did not turn.

Mara stopped across the street, water pressing against her shins. She knew she should not be there; knew, too, that she had been expected. The thought arrived whole and cold. The woman had pointed not to accuse but to summon.

The woman lifted her left arm.

The burned one.

In the gray light, under the rain, the scars looked less like scars than char. Not healed skin but wood after flame, blackened ridges, silver ash. Her fingers curled. The gesture was familiar.

Mara remembered the chair’s arm curled inward, burned down to its frame. She remembered the hollow seat brimming with rain. Poor thing. Poor thing. How sentimental she had been, pitying furniture while a living woman walked by. Or had she pitied correctly, only not enough?

The water rose quickly now. It had reached the second step of the house. It pushed at Mara’s thighs, full of hidden debris that knocked against her legs. The man shouted again, and this time she heard him.

“Eleanor! Come inside!”

Eleanor. The name was ordinary, almost disappointing.

The woman—Eleanor—looked at Mara across the water. There was no surprise in her face. No fear. Her eyes were dark and flat, reflecting the drowned street. Then she smiled.

Not kindly. Not cruelly. As if sharing a private joke whose punchline had been waiting in the rain.

Mara wanted to ask about the chair. The urge was so strong she took one step forward before the current shoved her sideways. She caught a lamppost, gasping. What would she say? Was it yours? Did it burn with you? Did you leave it out, or did it leave you?

From around the corner came an object turning slowly in the floodwater.

At first Mara thought it was a crate. It bumped against the stalled van, spun, freed itself, and drifted into the center of the street. Blackened legs broke the surface like the legs of a dead animal. A back. One curled arm.

The burned chair.

No, impossible. It could not have traveled all this way from Finch Street, against slopes, through blocked drains and traffic. Unless the water had made new streets of its own. Unless it knew the route better than she did.

The chair floated with grotesque dignity, seat now overflowing, upholstery streaming like weed. It approached the blue-shuttered house and lodged briefly against the iron gate.

Eleanor moved toward it.

The man lunged from the steps and seized her right wrist. She turned on him with sudden violence. Even through the rain, Mara saw his face change. The umbrella fell from his hand and sailed away open, a black lily on the flood. He held on. Eleanor struggled, silent, her burned arm reaching for the chair.

Mara should help. Of course she should help. But which one? Help the man drag his wife inside? Help the woman reach the burned chair that had come for her? The question was insane, and yet the street itself had become insane, all the firm lines of curb and pavement dissolved. In such water, why should duties remain dry and clear?

The chair broke free from the gate and drifted past.

Eleanor stopped struggling. Her body slackened. The man nearly fell backward with the sudden absence of resistance. For one moment husband and wife stood joined by his grip, looking after the chair as it moved down the street.

Then Eleanor stepped into the flood.

The man still held her wrist, but she pulled him with her. Water surged around their waists. He shouted her name. She did not answer. She followed the chair, or was drawn by it, her scarred arm extended, fingers curved in imitation or longing.

Mara clung to the lamppost. The cold had entered her bones. She understood nothing, but understanding had begun to seem like a dry-land habit, useful only where things stayed where they were put.

The chair turned slowly before her. For an instant it faced her head-on, the burned back rising from the water, the hollow seat dark and full. It looked less like furniture than a throne ruined in some private kingdom. Rain stippled its surface. In its puddled seat, Mara saw a pale oval appear—not a reflection of the sky, not her own face.

Eleanor’s face.

Mara shut her eyes. When she opened them, the chair had passed, and the woman was nearer, water at her ribs, the man behind her still holding on. Eleanor looked at Mara as she moved by. Her smile was gone. What remained was worse: an expression of terrible relief.

Not drowning, Mara thought. Returning.

The current took them all another few feet. The man lost his footing and vanished to his shoulders, came up coughing, his hand torn from Eleanor’s wrist. He grabbed at the gate, at the hedge, at nothing. Mara screamed then, though the sound was swallowed by rain and sirens and the flood’s low animal roar.

Eleanor did not turn back. She reached the chair.

Her burned hand touched its burned arm.

For a second, woman and chair floated side by side. The contact was delicate, almost courteous. Then the water lifted, a brown swell rolling down from the avenue, and both tipped away together. The green coat? No, she had left it inside. The red shoes flashed once beneath the surface. The chair spun. Eleanor’s black hair spread around her head like smoke underwater.

Mara let go of the lamppost.

Later—there would be a later, though she could not imagine it then—she would remember pushing through water toward the man, remember catching his sweater, remember the two of them slammed against the steps of the blue-shuttered house by a force that seemed not liquid but intentional. She would remember strangers hauling them upward. Blankets. Sirens. A rescue boat nosing through the street where taxis used to pass.

They would ask what happened to the woman.

Mara would say she was swept away. It was true. The flood had taken many things: bins, bicycles, flowerpots, a white dog’s leash without the dog, the contents of cellars, the patience of the city. It had taken Eleanor. It had taken the burned chair.

She would not say that the chair had come to collect her.

Weeks later, when the streets dried and left their brown lace of silt on every railing, Mara returned to Finch Street. The upholstery shop remained shuttered. The curb was empty. No mark showed where the burned chair had waited in the rain.

Of course not. What had she expected? A scorch on the pavement? A plaque? Cities did not commemorate the objects they lost, and people only sometimes.

Still, she stood there a long while, feeling foolish and cold in sunlight. Traffic passed. A woman laughed into her phone. Someone had placed a new chair outside the shop, this one striped blue and white, with a broken caster and one cushion missing. It was not burned. It was not waiting. It was only a chair.

Mara told herself this firmly.

Then, in the hollow where the cushion should have been, she saw rainwater glinting—though it had not rained in days—and on its surface floated a single black flake, fragile as ash.
