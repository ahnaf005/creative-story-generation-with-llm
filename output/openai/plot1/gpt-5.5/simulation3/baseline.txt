### The Chair in the Rain

At first I noticed only the chair.

It sat on the curb outside the closed fishmonger’s, where the pavement dipped and collected rainwater in a long black tongue. A dining chair, or what had once been one: wooden, narrow-backed, with a woven cane seat that had burned away almost entirely. The legs were blistered and flaking. One side of the back had curled inward as if the chair had tried to fold itself around the flame. It gave off no smoke, of course. It must have been burned elsewhere and dragged there for collection, set among split bin bags and a broken umbrella.

Still, in the wet afternoon light, it looked recently extinguished.

I was standing under the awning of the pharmacy, waiting for the rain to soften. The forecast had warned of flooding, but forecasts had warned of many things. People stepped around the growing streams at the curb, their faces pinched, their collars raised. Cars hissed past. A bus exhaled and shut its doors on a crowd of damp shoulders.

Then the couple appeared.

They came from the direction of the station, walking close together but not touching. He was tall and stooped, with a bald patch made shiny by rain. She was small, almost slight, wearing a dark green coat belted tightly at the waist. Her hair was pinned up under a scarf, though several strands had come loose and clung to her cheek. They were neither young nor old, neither poor nor rich. They looked like any couple returning home with nothing ahead of them but tea, a towel over the radiator, the evening news.

And yet something in the arrangement of them stopped me.

Perhaps it was the way they did not speak. Not in the ordinary silence of long marriage, but in a silence so complete it seemed rehearsed. Perhaps it was the way the man held an umbrella over himself but not quite over her, and she did not correct him. Or perhaps it was that when they passed the burned chair, the woman turned her head toward it.

Only for a moment.

The man did not look. He kept walking. But the woman slowed, just half a pace, and her face changed. It did not show pity, or recognition, or surprise. It became empty, as if the rain had washed the expression clean away.

Then she continued beside him.

I do not know why I followed them. There are moments in life that later cannot be defended, only described. I could say I had nowhere to be, which was true. I could say the rain had made the city strange, and strangeness breeds permission. I could say I was lonely, and the sight of two people belonging to one another had drawn me after them like warmth seen through a window.

But I think it was the chair.

I think, when the woman looked at it, I felt something look back.

They turned down Mercer Street, where the drains were already choking on leaves. Water ran ankle-deep along the gutters, carrying cigarette ends, petals, a white glove. The man walked ahead now, umbrella angled like a shield. The woman followed precisely in his wake, stepping where he stepped. I kept half a block behind, pausing at windows, pretending to examine menus taped to glass.

At the corner shop, the owner was stacking sandbags across the entrance. “River’s over,” he called to someone inside. “They’ve closed the lower road.”

The man and woman did not react.

They crossed beneath the railway bridge, where the sound of rain became immense. Water poured from the masonry in steady ropes. Under the bridge, the woman stopped again. The man took three steps before he realized. He turned, annoyed, though he said nothing.

She was looking at the ground.

I followed her gaze and saw the rainwater swirling around a scatter of black flecks. Charcoal, I thought. Burnt wood, crushed into the mud by passing tires. Impossible to know if it had fallen from the chair or from some other fire entirely. The woman lifted one foot and pressed the toe of her shoe against the blackened debris, gently, as if testing whether it was alive.

The man came back and took her by the elbow.

Not roughly. Not tenderly.

He guided her onward.

Beyond the bridge the houses grew older, pressed close to the street: brick terraces with basement flats and iron railings. The water was deeper here. It lapped over the curb and spread across the road from one side to the other, so that cars moved slowly, sending brown waves against doorsteps. Sirens wailed somewhere distant and stopped.

The couple reached a narrow house with peeling blue paint on the door. A bay window looked onto the street, its lace curtains yellowed but open. There was a small front garden, if it could be called a garden: three paving stones, a rusting bicycle frame, two pots of dead lavender. Water had already filled the space between the gate and steps.

The man fumbled with keys. The woman stood behind him, rain dripping from her scarf, her face turned slightly toward the street. For one alarming second I thought she saw me. I had stopped beside a plane tree, pretending to tie my shoe, though the water had risen above the laces.

Her eyes moved over me without interest.

The door opened. They went inside.

I should have gone home then. I should have returned by the high roads before they too flooded, made soup, phoned someone, watched the warnings bloom red across the television map.

Instead I crossed to the opposite pavement and stood beneath the plane tree.

The house remained dark for several minutes. Then a lamp came on in the front room.

Through the bay window I could see them.

It is indecent, how much can be seen from the street when people believe themselves alone. The room was narrow and crowded: shelves sagging with books, framed photographs turned silver by age, a television, a mantel crowded with figurines. Near the window stood a dining table with two chairs. Only two.

The man removed his coat and hung it carefully on a hook. He folded the umbrella, shook it once, and set it in a stand. The woman remained in the center of the room, still wearing her wet coat.

He said something to her. I could not hear it through the glass and rain, but I saw his mouth form the words sharply. She did not move. He spoke again. At last she untied her scarf. Her hair, released from its pins, fell in a dull brown rope over one shoulder.

The resemblance began there, though I did not understand it yet.

She sat in the chair nearest the window. The man went through a doorway into the back of the house. The woman placed her hands flat on her knees and faced forward.

Rain beat against the glass.

The water in the street climbed.

It came first as a widening shine across the pavement, then as a trembling sheet over the garden path. The dead lavender pots tipped one by one, releasing small clouds of soil into the flood. A plastic bottle drifted past me, nudged my shin, and continued. I stepped back onto the low wall around the tree. From there I could still see into the room.

The man returned carrying a tray. Two cups, a plate of biscuits. He set one cup before the woman. Steam rose between them. She did not pick it up.

He sat in the other chair and drank from his own cup. Now and then he spoke. She did not answer. He appeared unsurprised by this. After a while he reached across the table and, with his thumb, wiped rainwater or a tear from her cheek. She permitted it. That was the only word: permitted.

The room light flickered once.

Somewhere down the street, a car alarm began to bleat as water reached its engine.

I thought of the burned chair outside the fishmonger’s. I thought of its collapsed seat, the scorched wood turned inward. The woman in the window sat with the same narrow uprightness, the same damaged grace. Her dark coat, drying in patches, clung to her shoulders like char. Even her hands, pale and still on her knees, seemed not hands but the last unburned pieces of something pulled from a fire.

I told myself this was nonsense. The mind makes patterns when it is frightened. It sees faces in wallpaper, omens in birds, guilt in strangers.

Then the woman turned her head toward the window.

Toward me.

This time there was no mistake. Her eyes found mine with a directness that emptied the distance between us. I froze on the wall, water swirling below, rain running into my collar.

She raised one hand.

Not in greeting. Not in alarm.

She pressed two fingers to the inside of her wrist, as if taking her own pulse. Then she lowered her hand and looked at the empty space beside her chair.

But there was no empty space. There was only the table leg, the edge of a rug, the pale cup steaming untouched.

The man followed her gaze and stiffened.

He turned toward the window, and I ducked behind the tree.

For a moment I was a child again, hiding after doing something forbidden, heart hammering with the certainty that punishment had already begun. When I dared look back, the man was at the curtains. He drew them halfway, but not fully. Through the remaining gap I could see the woman still seated, still facing forward.

The flood rose to my calves.

People had begun shouting at the far end of the street. A police van crawled past, blue light making the water flash. A voice through a loudspeaker advised residents to move upstairs, to avoid basements, to prepare for evacuation. Doors opened. Neighbors emerged carrying bags, pets, children wrapped in blankets. The couple’s blue door stayed shut.

A current took hold of the street. It was slow but strong, moving with the heavy patience of something that knew it would win. I stepped down from the wall and nearly lost my balance. The water was bitterly cold. It climbed past my knees.

I should go, I thought.

But behind the gap in the curtain, the woman had begun to change.

No—nothing so dramatic. Her skin did not blacken before my eyes. Her hair did not smoke. Yet each time the lamp flickered, her outline seemed less human and more angular. Her back straightened until it was almost impossible. Her elbows pressed close to her sides. Her knees met beneath the table. She was becoming not a chair, exactly, but the memory of one: an object made to bear weight, to hold the shape of another body, to remain after conversation ended.

The man stood over her now. He was speaking quickly, bending low, his face near hers. Pleading, perhaps. Or scolding. He took her shoulders and shook them once.

The lamp went out.

For three heartbeats the room vanished.

When the light returned, the second chair at the table was empty.

The man stood alone.

The woman was still there, seated nearest the window. But the chair beneath her had changed.

Its wooden back was blackened. One side curled inward. The cane seat was gone, and yet she did not fall. She sat suspended within it, or it within her; I could not tell. Her coat was no longer green but the color of wet ash. Smoke, or steam, rose faintly from her hair.

The man put both hands over his mouth.

The flood struck the front door hard enough to make it shudder.

Then, from down the street, something came turning slowly on the brown water.

At first I thought it was a branch. Then it revolved, and I saw the narrow back, the blistered legs, the missing seat. The burned chair from the fishmonger’s drifted past the gate, bumped once against the submerged railing, and lodged there as if it had found its place.

Inside the room, the woman lifted her face.

The burned chair outside and the burned chair beneath her were identical. Not similar. Identical. The same curled back, the same ruined seat, the same scars of fire. One floated in the flood. One held her in the lit room. Or perhaps there had only ever been one, and I was seeing it twice because the water had risen high enough to reflect what should have stayed hidden.

The man turned toward the door. He moved at last with urgency, stumbling into the hallway. I saw his shape pass behind the frosted glass. He tried the door, but water pinned it shut. He shouted. This time I heard him, though not the words.

The woman remained at the window.

She looked past the man, past the floating chair, past the rain-thick glass, directly at me. Her expression was no longer empty. It was patient.

The kind of patience wood has.

A wave rolled down the street, larger than the others, perhaps from a lorry moving somewhere beyond sight. It lifted the burned chair from the railing and carried it through the little garden. The blue door gave with a cracking sound. Water burst into the house.

The lamp exploded in a white flash.

Darkness swallowed the room.

I ran then. I ran through water to my thighs, stumbling against railings, grabbing lampposts, hearing behind me the vast rearrangement of furniture, doors, lives. At the corner I looked back once.

The bay window of the blue house reflected only rain.

The next morning the flood had begun to withdraw, leaving mud in the streets and drowned leaves pasted to every surface. Rescue crews moved from door to door. Neighbors stood in clusters, stunned and shivering, telling one another what had been lost.

I returned to the fishmonger’s.

The rubbish bags were gone. The broken umbrella was gone. The curb was streaked with silt. There was no sign of the burned chair.

Later I walked past the house with the blue door. Its windows were open. Men in rubber boots carried out ruined rugs, swollen books, a table. One of the two dining chairs emerged intact, dripping.

The other did not come out.

I asked a woman in a red coat whether the couple had been rescued.

She looked at me strangely.

“What couple?”

“The people who live there. A man and woman.”

“No woman,” she said. “Mr. Vale lives alone. Has done for years.”

I looked at the bay window. Behind the muddy glass, the front room was stripped nearly bare. In the place where the woman had sat, there was only a dark mark on the floorboards, round and deep, as if something hot had once stood there and burned patiently downward.

The woman in the red coat followed my gaze.

“Terrible thing,” she said. “His wife died in a fire. Long before he moved here, I think. Or maybe after. I can never remember.”

But I remembered the woman’s eyes. I remembered her hand at her wrist, searching for a pulse that might have belonged to her or to the thing that held her. I remembered the chair turning in the flood, coming home.

That evening, when the rain began again, I moved my own chairs away from the window.
