**The Chair in the Rain**

By five o’clock the rain had polished the city to a dark shine. Water ran along the gutters in quick, nervous streams, nudging cigarette ends and bottle caps toward the drains. The drains themselves were overwhelmed, coughing up bubbles and scraps of leaves. Everyone moved with shoulders hunched, umbrellas tilted like shields.

I had taken shelter beneath the awning of a closed bakery. The display window behind me showed three plaster cakes from some more generous era, their icing roses faded to the color of old bruises. Across the street, at the curb, stood a chair.

It was the chair that first held me.

It had once belonged in someone’s dining room, I guessed. A straight-backed wooden thing, plain but decent, with curved legs and a seat woven from cane. Now one side of it had been burned black. The left arm was gone entirely, gnawed away by fire, and the back was charred in a crescent shape as if someone had pressed a great hot mouth to it. Rainwater gathered in the seat where the cane had sagged and split. Every few seconds, a drop fell from the remaining arm.

People passed it without looking. They stepped around it the way people step around sleeping dogs, or beggars, or anything that asks to be noticed.

Then the couple came.

They emerged from the mouth of the subway stairs, rising together from underground as if lifted by the same mechanism. They had no umbrella. The man held his coat over both their heads, though it was too small to cover them properly, and rain streaked his face and hair. The woman laughed when a passing bus sent water up over the curb and onto their shoes. Not loudly—there was nothing theatrical in it—but with the kind of surprise that makes the world seem briefly forgiven.

They were ordinary. That was the remarkable thing.

He wore a gray coat with a missing button and carried a paper bag darkening in the rain. She wore a green dress under a black cardigan, the hem sticking wetly to her calves. Her hair, loosened by the weather, clung to her cheeks in thin brown strands. They might have been clerks, teachers, people returning from the dentist, people who kept receipts in a kitchen drawer. There was nothing about them that justified the sudden arrest of my attention.

Yet I watched them as though they were actors entering at last in the third act of a play I had unknowingly been waiting to see.

They slowed when they reached the burned chair.

The man looked at it first, only briefly. His expression did not change. The woman, however, stopped.

The coat slipped from over her head, and the rain touched her without resistance. She stood facing the chair, her lips slightly parted. In that moment, her laughter was gone so completely that it seemed it had never belonged to her. She did not appear frightened. She looked, rather, like someone recognizing a face across a crowded room—a face altered by age or violence, but still known.

The man said something. I could not hear it over the rain and traffic.

The woman reached out one hand.

She did not touch the chair. Her fingers hovered inches above the blackened back, trembling slightly. Then the man took her wrist, gently but with purpose, and lowered her hand. He glanced around, not suspiciously, not like a man with a secret, but like anyone might glance around when someone they love behaves strangely in public.

His eyes passed over me beneath the bakery awning and did not stop.

The woman gave a small nod. They walked on.

I do not know why I followed them.

There are reasons that can be supplied afterward: curiosity, loneliness, the peculiar magnetism of bad weather. I had nowhere pressing to be. My apartment waited three blocks away with its unwashed cup in the sink, its one lamp with the crooked shade, its silence. But those are explanations, not the truth. The truth is that when they moved away from the chair, something in me moved after them.

I crossed the street.

The water at the curb came over my shoes at once, cold enough to make me gasp. Ahead, the couple turned down Market, then left onto Alder, keeping close together. The man still held the paper bag, now clutched against his chest beneath his coat. The woman walked with her head bowed, but once, twice, she looked back—not at me, I thought, but toward the street we had left, toward the burned chair waiting in the rain.

The storm was getting worse. Sirens sounded somewhere distant and then were swallowed. At intersections, cars moved slowly through water that fanned out from their tires in brown wings. People shouted from doorways. A man in shirtsleeves tried to drag a sandbag across the entrance of a pharmacy, cursing when it burst and spilled a dark tongue of sand into the flood.

The couple did not hurry.

Their calm began to frighten me. Not because it was dramatic, but because it was not. It was the calm of people walking a familiar path to an inevitable appointment.

They lived in a narrow brick house near the river, on a street I had never entered though I had passed its corner many times. The houses leaned close together, their basement windows already half-submerged. Rain hammered the stoops. The river, two blocks away and usually invisible behind warehouses, announced itself now in the smell of mud and metal.

The man unlocked the blue front door. Before going in, the woman paused on the threshold.

She turned.

This time I knew she saw me.

There was no accusation in her face. No alarm. Her eyes met mine across the rain-filled street with a strange patience, as though she had expected me eventually and was not pleased or displeased, only resigned.

Then she stepped inside, and the door closed.

I should have gone home then. Even now, recounting it, I feel the proper shape of that moment: the door shut, the watcher dismissed, the rain a sufficient warning. But I remained beneath a sycamore tree whose leaves shook and flashed in the downpour, and I watched the house.

A light came on in the front room.

Through the thin curtains I could see movement: the man crossing with the paper bag, the woman standing still near the window. Their forms were blurred by rain and fabric, made soft and underwater. I told myself I would wait only until I saw that everything was ordinary. That they would eat soup, perhaps. That the man would remove his wet shoes. That the woman would laugh again, and the burned chair would be only some private association—a dead grandmother’s furniture, a memory of a fire, some human thing.

The water rose around my ankles.

In the houses along the street, people began carrying things upstairs. Boxes, lamps, framed pictures, a child with a stuffed rabbit. Doors opened and shut. A woman called for someone named Leo. Two teenagers pushed a floating trash bin away from a cellar door. The storm drain at the corner had become a little whirlpool, sucking and failing, sucking and failing.

Inside the blue-doored house, no one carried anything upstairs.

The man stood at the front window now, parting the curtain with two fingers. He looked out at the street, at the water, at me. His face, distorted by the wet glass, seemed older than before. He was a thin man, I saw, with tired cheeks and a careful mouth.

He let the curtain fall.

A moment later the front room went dark.

Then, at the side of the house, another light appeared.

There was a narrow passage between their house and the next, barely wide enough for the garbage bins lined there. I should not have entered it. I entered it.

The water was deeper in the passage, trapped between the walls. It climbed my shins with a slow, intimate pressure. My socks filled; my trousers clung. The bins bumped against one another like dull bells.

At the back was a small yard enclosed by a leaning fence. Beyond it lay an alley, already a channel of moving water. The kitchen window glowed yellow. There were no curtains there.

I stood outside and looked in.

The kitchen was old-fashioned and cramped, with a black-and-white tiled floor and a table set for two. A kettle sat on the stove. The paper bag lay open on the counter; from it protruded a loaf of bread and the green tops of carrots. Perfectly ordinary things. More ordinary, in fact, for being seen like this, through rain and glass, by someone who had no right to see them.

The woman sat at the table.

The man stood behind her, his hands resting on the back of her chair.

For several minutes neither moved. Rain crawled down the window in wandering paths, joining, splitting, joining again. I became aware of my own reflection faintly imposed over the scene—my pale face, my dark coat, my hair plastered flat—so that it seemed I too was inside with them, hovering near the ceiling.

Then the man bent and said something into the woman’s ear.

She shook her head.

He straightened. His hands tightened on the chair back. It was an ordinary chair, unburned, one of a mismatched set around the table. Pine, perhaps, with a woven cane seat.

The woman lifted her hand and placed it over his. Her fingers were slender, the nails unpainted. For an instant they were merely a married couple in a kitchen during a storm, performing some private ritual of comfort.

Then she turned her face toward the window.

I stepped back, but too late. Her eyes found me again. She did not startle. She did not call out. She looked through the glass and the rain and the dark as if I were the thing trapped behind the pane.

The man followed her gaze.

His expression changed then. Not anger. Not exactly. It was worse than anger: pity.

He came to the back door and opened it.

Rain blew into the kitchen. Warm light spilled over the floodwater at my feet, breaking there into trembling pieces.

“You shouldn’t be here,” he said.

“I’m sorry,” I told him. My voice sounded small under the storm. “I saw you on the street. I don’t know why I—”

“Go home.”

Behind him, the woman still sat at the table. She had not turned away from me. The light made her skin appear almost translucent.

“The chair,” I said.

The man flinched.

I had not meant to say it. The word came out by itself, dropped between us like something heavy.

He stepped onto the threshold, blocking my view of her. “Go home,” he repeated, but now there was pleading in it.

“What happened to it?”

He looked past me toward the alley, where water rushed against the fence. When he spoke, he did so softly. “Nothing that belongs to you.”

That should have been enough. It was more than enough. Yet something had opened, and I, being foolish or lonely or cruel, pressed my hand against it.

“She knew it,” I said. “On the street. She knew that chair.”

His face twisted then with such sudden grief that I regretted every word. He gripped the doorframe as though the house had tilted.

“No,” he said. “It knew her.”

The kitchen light flickered.

From the street came a shout, then another. Somewhere, glass broke. The water around my legs surged, tugging at me, and I realized with a dull surprise that it had risen to my knees. Bits of debris knocked against me: a plastic cup, a branch, the lid of something.

“Please,” the man said. “Leave before she remembers more.”

Behind him, the woman stood.

The chair she had been sitting in scraped back across the tile with a long, dry sound impossible in that wet world. The man turned quickly.

“Anna,” he said.

She did not answer.

She looked taller now, though perhaps it was only the way she held herself. Her wet dress clung darkly to her body. From the hem, water dripped onto the kitchen floor, though she had been inside for nearly an hour. Drop after drop fell, forming a black puddle beneath her.

The smell reached me then.

Not merely rain, not river mud, not the sour odor of flooded drains. Smoke. Old smoke, soaked into wood. The smell of a fire long extinguished but not finished.

The man shut the door in my face.

I heard the bolt slide.

For a time, nothing happened.

I stood in the yard with water pressing at my thighs and the rain striking my scalp, and I told myself to move. I told myself I was in danger. I told myself there were warnings being shouted in the street, that the river had come loose, that no curiosity was worth drowning for.

But through the kitchen window, I saw her raise both hands to her throat.

Not choking. Remembering.

The man stood before her, speaking rapidly. His shoulders shook. She listened, or seemed to listen, with her head tilted. Then she smiled.

It was not the smile from the street, the small laugh after the bus splashed her shoes. It was a burned smile, if such a thing can be said: black at the edges, hollowed by heat.

The kitchen light flickered again, brighter this time, too bright, white and pulsing.

In that sudden glare I saw the room change.

The table was gone. Or rather, it remained and did not remain, its outline wavering like a reflection disturbed by a thrown stone. The tiled floor blackened. The walls sweated smoke. And the chair—the chair on which Anna had sat—stood in the center of the kitchen with one arm burned away and its back charred in a crescent, rainwater collected in its broken cane seat.

The same chair.

No. Not the same. The one on the street had been its shadow, its shed skin, its warning.

Anna reached for it.

The man caught her around the waist. She did not resist, but he cried out as if burned. Steam rose where his arms touched her dress.

“Anna!” he shouted. I heard him even through the glass and rain. “You’re here. You’re here with me.”

She looked down at his arms around her, puzzled and tender.

Then the water rose over the kitchen sill.

It did not break the window. It seeped through it, not as water should, but as smoke seeps under a door. A dark line appeared along the frame, then widened. Water slid down the inside wall. It spread across the floor toward the burned chair.

Outside, the flood reached my waist.

A garbage bin floated past and struck the fence, bursting it open. The alley beyond roared. I grabbed the window ledge to keep from being pulled away.

Inside, Anna sank into the charred chair.

The man fell to his knees before her, his hands clasped in her lap. He was weeping. I could see his mouth forming words again and again. Stay. Stay. Stay.

Anna rested one hand on his head. For a moment, she looked entirely human and entirely sorrowful.

Then the chair began to smoke.

Not flames. Not yet. Just thin gray threads rising from the blackened wood, curling around her arms, her hair, her face. She closed her eyes. The floodwater in the kitchen climbed the chair legs, hissed against the char, and turned to vapor.

I understood then, or thought I did.

Somewhere, some time, there had been a fire. A room. A chair. Perhaps she had died in it; perhaps she had survived when she was not meant to, or returned when she should not have. Perhaps the man had found her afterward, or made a bargain beside the ashes, or simply loved what came back enough to call it by her name. The burned chair discarded on the street had not been trash. It had been a piece of the event working its way outward, appearing in the city like a symptom.

And I had followed the fever home.

The window cracked.

A single line ran from top to bottom. Through it came a breath of heat so intense that I recoiled, losing my grip. The current took me at once, spinning me toward the broken fence and the alley’s black rush.

As I was swept away, I saw the man turn toward the window.

For one instant his eyes met mine. There was no pity in them now. Only a terrible, exhausted envy, as if I were fortunate to be carried off by mere water.

Behind him, Anna sat in the burning chair while the flood rose around her knees, her waist, her folded hands. Smoke braided with rain. The kitchen filled with steam until the glass whitened and the house disappeared.

I struck something hard in the alley. A step, perhaps, or a submerged curb. Pain flashed up my side. I clung to an iron railing and hauled myself, inch by inch, onto higher ground, where strangers dragged me through an open doorway and wrapped me in a blanket that smelled of dog.

By morning, the rain had stopped.

The river withdrew slowly, leaving mud on the streets and drowned leaves plastered to windows. Sirens returned, now close and weary. People came out to measure what they had lost.

I went back to the street with the blue-doored house.

Its windows were dark. The front door hung open. Inside, the rooms were ruined in the usual way: overturned chairs, swollen floorboards, a watermark three feet up the walls. Neighbors said the couple had lived there six years. Quiet people. No children. The man’s name was David, or Daniel; no one agreed. The woman was Anna, yes. Lovely woman. Sickly, maybe. Kept to herself.

No bodies were found.

In the kitchen, there was no burned chair. Only four mismatched chairs around the table, all waterlogged, all intact.

I told no one what I had seen. Even if I had found the words, they would have made the thing smaller, and I feared that more than disbelief.

For weeks afterward, I avoided the bakery street. Then one afternoon, under a dry pale sky, I turned the corner without thinking.

The chair was still there.

It stood at the curb where the couple had first stopped, though the flood had carried cars from their parking spaces and torn grates from the drains. Its left arm was gone. Its back was burned in a crescent. In the split cane seat lay a shallow pool of rainwater from some shower I had not noticed.

As I approached, the water trembled.

Not from wind. There was no wind.

I looked down into the little pool and saw, reflected there, not my face but a woman’s hand hovering just above the charred wood, fingers trembling, almost touching.

Then, from somewhere very near, I heard a man whisper:

“Leave before she remembers more.”

I stepped back.

The chair remained.

People passed it without looking. They stepped around it, careful not to brush against the burned arm, and hurried on with their ordinary lives.
