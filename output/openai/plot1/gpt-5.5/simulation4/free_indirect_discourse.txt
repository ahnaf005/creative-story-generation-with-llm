At first it was the chair that stopped him.

It lay at the curb among the black plastic sacks and splintered crates, not so much discarded as expelled. Someone had set fire to it, or else it had burned elsewhere and been carried here, though why would anyone carry a burned chair? Its legs were charred to brittle points; one arm had cracked open to show pale wood inside, like bone under a scab. The seat sagged, collapsed in the middle, stuffed fabric bloated by rain and soot. A kitchen chair, perhaps. Or no, a sitting-room chair. Something that had once received a body each evening and kept its shape afterward, faithful as a dog.

He had been on his way nowhere in particular. That was how the afternoon had arranged itself: grey light, swollen gutters, shops lit too early, people hurrying with their collars up. The rain had paused but not ended. It hung in the air, waiting.

A burned chair on the pavement. People stepped around it without looking. Of course they did. City people saw ruin every day and called it furniture.

Then the couple appeared.

They came from the mouth of the arcade, walking side by side beneath a single umbrella that was too small for them. The man held it; the woman inclined herself toward him but did not touch him. It was the sort of arrangement anyone might see a hundred times in wet weather and forget at once. The man wore a dark overcoat shiny at the seams. He was tall but folded, as if apologizing to doorframes. The woman wore a pale coat belted at the waist and a scarf of a color that might have been blue before the day diluted it. Her hair was pinned up carelessly, and one loose strand clung to her cheek.

Nothing remarkable. That was the odd thing. Nothing at all. Yet he found himself staring as though a signal had been given.

They paused beside the chair.

Not because of it, surely. The man had stopped to shift the umbrella from one hand to the other; the woman turned her face slightly, following the movement. But for an instant she seemed to look directly at the burned chair, and in that instant the street tightened around them. The buses, the wet windows, the smell of diesel and rain—everything drew in its breath.

She did not smile. She did not start. She looked, if she looked at all, with the exhausted recognition of someone passing an old acquaintance in a crowd and choosing not to speak.

Then they continued.

He remained by the curb, his eyes moving from the chair to the woman’s retreating back, then to the chair again. Absurd. There was no relation. A woman in a pale coat; a chair burned black. Only the day had made them kin. The rain made all things resemble one another eventually. Still, when the couple reached the corner and turned left, he followed.

He did not decide to follow. That would have been improper, and he was not improper. He merely walked in the same direction, as his feet had already been pointed that way. If they happened to be ahead of him, if the umbrella bobbed in and out of sight beyond shoulders and shop awnings, that was the city’s doing. Anyone might go left. Anyone might keep the same pace.

They passed the bakery with its steamed windows. They passed the betting shop, where two men smoked under the NO SMOKING sign and argued about a horse with the solemnity of priests. The couple spoke little. Once the man bent his head and said something, and the woman’s mouth moved in answer. Her voice did not carry. He imagined it low, dry, perhaps amused. Or perhaps she had no voice at all. That thought pleased him in a small, private way, not because silence was desirable but because it made her more complete. A silent woman could contain anything.

At the next crossing the drains were already overwhelmed. Water gathered along the curb in a slick brown ribbon, carrying cigarette ends, leaves, a red ticket stub, a white feather flattened like a torn petal. The couple stepped over it. The woman lifted her hem just enough to show narrow ankles above dark shoes. A person noticed such things against his will. He looked away at once, or meant to.

The rain began again, quietly.

It made a fine clicking on the umbrella. It darkened the man’s shoulders where the umbrella failed. The woman remained nearly dry, though one sleeve was wet from elbow to cuff, and this seemed unfair. Or deliberate. As if she had chosen that one portion of herself to expose to weather.

Where were they going? Home, obviously. Couples in weather went home. They had the purposeful fatigue of those who had bought what they needed, said what must be said, and now returned to the private room where their real faces waited. He pictured a flat above a shop, narrow stairs smelling of old cabbage and paraffin. Or a terrace house with lace curtains and a brass number. He would stop long before then, naturally. At the next corner, perhaps. The one after that.

The streets sloped downward as they moved east. He had not noticed it before, though he knew this district well enough. Water ran along the gutters now with intention. The drains gulped and failed. In doorways, shopkeepers stood with folded arms, watching the road as though expecting someone late.

The couple turned into Fenton Street.

Now he should certainly stop. Fenton Street was residential, narrow and quiet, with plane trees that shed strips of bark like old bandages. No one went there by accident. To enter it behind them would be to admit something.

He entered it behind them.

It was not his fault that the rain thickened just then, obliging everyone to hurry. Not his fault that the street lamps flickered on, one after another, making the wet pavement glow. He could hardly stand exposed at the corner like a fool. Better to continue with the confidence of a man who lived there, or visited someone there, or had business beyond. Yes, business. The city was full of errands no one could explain.

Halfway down the street the couple stopped before a narrow house with a green door. Number 27. The man folded the umbrella before he reached the step, letting rain strike both of them. The woman did not flinch. She stood while he searched his pockets for keys. Her face turned outward, toward the street.

He had time to move past them, to keep walking, to vanish respectably. Instead he paused beneath a plane tree and pretended to examine his shoe.

She was looking at him.

No—past him, through him, at the rain, the road, the whole dimming world. Yet his body knew it had been seen. Heat rose in his neck despite the cold. The man found the key and opened the door. Light spilled yellow onto the wet step. The woman entered first. The man followed, closing the door gently behind them.

There. Finished. A small trespass, already completed. He could go home.

But where was home at that moment? His own rooms, with their unwashed cup and the radiator that knocked at night, seemed not to exist. The house at number 27 had taken the place of home. It held within it the continuation of a sentence he had begun reading in the street. To leave now would be to close the book before the verb.

He crossed to the opposite side and stood beneath the shallow awning of a boarded-up florist’s. Through the front window of number 27, curtains had been drawn but not entirely. A narrow gap remained. Not an invitation. Of course not. Only negligence. Curtains were always doing this; houses could not be expected to defend themselves perfectly.

The room beyond was visible in fragments: a lamp with a pleated shade, the edge of a mantel, books in no order, the glossy leaves of a plant. The couple moved in and out of sight. The man took off his coat and hung it somewhere near the door. The woman stood motionless in the middle of the room, still wearing her pale coat.

Why did she not remove it? Wet wool chilled the bones. Anyone knew that.

The man spoke. She shook her head. He gestured toward the back of the house. She remained. Then, slowly, she unfastened the belt of her coat. The movement had the gravity of ceremony. He ought not to watch. He watched.

Under the coat she wore a dark dress. So ordinary again. Everything about them returned, cruelly, to the ordinary. A woman taking off a coat. A man crossing a room. A lamp. A plant. Rain ticking on the awning above him. What had he expected? That she would reveal ash beneath the wool? That her arms would be blackened sticks? Foolishness. He was tired, and the day was peculiar, and the burned chair had lodged in his mind like a piece of grit under an eyelid.

Still, when she folded the coat over her arms, it seemed to him she held it the way one might hold a damaged thing rescued from a fire.

Water lapped over the curb.

He looked down. The street was flooding in earnest now, the gutter indistinguishable from the road. A shallow sheet moved around his shoes and climbed the soles. Somewhere uphill a drain cover rattled under pressure. The rain fell harder, no longer clicking but hissing, as if the sky had leaned close to whisper through clenched teeth.

He should leave before the buses stopped running. Before the water rose. He imagined the underground stations closing, tunnels filling, commuters standing in tidy lines while black water climbed their shins. It happened every few years in some district or other; everyone was surprised each time. Rain came down, rivers rose, basements surrendered, and people said extraordinary, extraordinary, as if they had not built their lives in bowls and gullies and old marshland.

Inside the house, the man had disappeared. The woman sat now in an armchair near the lamp.

Not the burned chair. Naturally not. This chair was upholstered in green, high-backed, with wings at the sides. Yet the sight of her sitting there struck him with such force that he pressed his palm against the florist’s window to steady himself.

She sat upright, hands on the arms, feet together. Her face was turned slightly toward the curtained gap. Not toward him. No. Toward the street. Toward the water. Toward the place where a watcher might be. The green chair enclosed her body, or her body completed it. Chair and woman formed a single shape: pale face, dark dress, wooden arms, stillness. The burned chair returned to him entire—the charred legs, the sagging seat, the split arm. He saw it not lying at the curb but waiting there, with invisible weight pressing into it, a missing occupant burned away or never risen.

How could a chair be abandoned before it was empty? The thought came without meaning and yet with the cold authority of truth.

The woman lifted one hand and touched her cheek, precisely where the strand of hair had clung earlier. Then she lowered it to the arm of the chair. Her fingers curled. The gesture was intimate, possessive. The chair might have been an animal she was calming.

Where had he seen that before? Not in life. In a photograph? In a dream? A room after a fire, walls smoked, furniture hulks under white powder, and a woman untouched at the center. Or a woman burned everywhere except the face. No, no. Melodrama. He was inventing horrors because the alternative was worse: that nothing was happening, and he had stood in the rain for half an hour to watch a stranger sit.

The water reached his ankles.

Cold pushed through his socks. He stepped backward, but the awning ended and rain struck his hair, his collar, ran down his neck. Across the street, number 27 glowed warmly. The house seemed to float already, though its bricks remained fixed. The narrow front garden had become a dark pool in which the low hedge trembled.

The man reappeared carrying two glasses. He handed one to the woman. She did not take it. He stood with the glass extended, waiting. Then he set it on a small table beside her. He spoke again, leaning down. His face, in profile, looked strained now. Not apologetic but pleading. The woman’s expression did not alter.

So there was unhappiness in the house. Good. Or not good—understandable. Unhappiness made sense of watching. If they had been cheerful, laughing over supper, he would have felt obscene. But sorrow opened a window. Sorrow had public rights.

The man straightened sharply, as if she had answered him at last. He turned away, rubbed both hands over his face, then crossed to the mantel and braced himself there. The woman remained in the green chair.

The burned chair at the curb had been hers. Impossible. Yet the conviction rose like the water, muddy and unstoppable. Not literally hers, perhaps; literal ownership was a vulgarity. It belonged to her in the way certain objects belonged to certain dreams. It had been thrown out because she had finished with it, or because it had failed to contain her. Burned because what else could one do with a chair that had known too much of a body?

He laughed once, softly, and the sound frightened him. The water took it away.

A car nosed into Fenton Street, slowed, then reversed out. Sensible. Somewhere a siren began and faded. A plastic bin floated past, rotating with stately importance. The water was above his ankles now, tugging at him each time a hidden current found the slope. He should move to higher ground. He would, in a moment. After one more moment.

Inside, the woman turned her head.

This time there could be no mistake. She looked directly through the gap in the curtains, across the drowned street, at him.

He did not move. The rain ran into his eyes. The cold climbed his shins. He had the ridiculous impulse to raise a hand in greeting, as if they were acquaintances separated by traffic.

Her face was pale but not frightened. Not accusing. She looked at him with that same exhausted recognition she had given the burned chair. Yes, it was the same. He understood then with a clarity that filled him like light: she had known he was there from the first. She had seen him beside the chair, seen him follow, seen him take his place beneath the florist’s awning. She had permitted it. No, more than permitted. She had required a witness.

For what?

The man at the mantel seemed unaware. He lifted his glass and drank. The woman kept looking out. Then, very slowly, she smiled.

It was not a smile of pleasure. It was the small, patient smile of someone hearing footsteps on the stair at last.

The lights went out.

All along the street, all at once: lamps, windows, signs. Darkness dropped so completely that he gasped. Only the rain remained visible, silver where it crossed the distant headlights at the corner. Number 27 vanished. The woman vanished. The green chair vanished.

After a few seconds, an upstairs window in the house flickered with candlelight. Then another. People calling to one another, doors opening, a dog barking madly. The ordinary world fought to return.

But the front room of number 27 stayed dark.

He waded forward without deciding to. The water was mid-calf now and full of unseen things brushing his legs. He crossed the street, arms out for balance. A current shoved him; he caught the iron fence of number 25 and swore aloud. His voice sounded foreign.

What was he doing? Nothing. Checking. That was all. If the power had failed and the room was dark, if water was rising, if someone inside needed—needed what? A stranger dripping at the window? Still he moved to number 27’s gate, which swung loose in the water. The little front path had disappeared. He made for the window by memory, by the dim rectangular paleness of curtains.

Up close, the gap revealed nothing. His own reflection hovered there, stretched and broken by rain: long face, wet hair plastered to the skull, eyes too wide. Behind it, darkness.

He tapped on the glass.

No answer.

He tapped harder. “Hello?”

The word was swallowed by rain.

A shape shifted beyond the curtain. His heart lurched. But it was only the fabric moving in a draft, perhaps. He pressed his face nearer. The glass smelled of cold and dirty water.

Inside, something pale appeared at floor level.

A hand?

No. The woman’s coat, fallen perhaps from a chair. Or a cushion. The mind made bodies wherever it could.

He stepped back and looked toward the door. Knock, then. Ring, though the bell would not work. Speak to the man. Explain—what? That he had followed them home and now worried about their sitting arrangements? Ridiculous. Criminal. He would be arrested or struck or, worse, invited in and made real.

The water surged suddenly to his knees. A wave, caused by something large turning into the street—an emergency vehicle, perhaps, though he saw no lights. It slapped the house fronts and rushed through the low garden walls. In that motion the curtain lifted away from the window.

The room appeared.

Even in darkness he could see it by the faint grey light from outside. The lamp, dead. The plant. The mantel. The small table with two glasses. The green wing chair near the window.

Empty.

Of course empty. She had gone to another room when the lights failed. She and the man were upstairs with candles, like the neighbors. Ordinary. Again and again the world corrected him, and again and again he resisted correction. Fool.

Then he saw the floor around the chair.

Blackened.

Not wet shadow. Not the pattern of a rug. A burned circle spread from beneath the green chair, dark fingers reaching over the boards. The chair itself was untouched, its high back and wings intact, but the floor beneath it had charred. At the edges, the burn faded into brown wood. At the center it was pure black.

He knew that black. He had stood over it at the curb.

A sound came from behind him: the scrape of wood in water.

He turned.

Something floated down Fenton Street, slow and deliberate, turning once in the current. At first he thought it was a branch, then a crate. It passed beneath the glow of an upstairs candle and showed itself.

The burned chair.

No. Not possible. The chair had been left streets away, uphill or down, he no longer knew. Perhaps the flood had carried it. Floodwater collected everything, redistributed the city according to secret laws. A burned chair could float as well as any other debris.

It came toward him, bobbing. One charred arm rose above the water like a beckoning hand. The collapsed seat gurgled as it filled and emptied. It bumped the gate of number 27, turned, and lodged against his thigh.

He could not move.

The wood, saturated, should have been soft. Yet against his leg it felt warm. Warm as flesh under a sleeve.

From inside the dark room came a creak.

He turned back to the window. The green chair was no longer empty.

The woman sat there again, though he had not seen her enter. Pale face, dark dress, hands on the arms. The burn mark spread beneath her like a shadow cast by fire rather than light. Behind her, the man stood in the doorway, but his posture had changed. He leaned oddly to one side, his arms hanging. He looked less like a husband than like a coat hung badly on a peg.

The woman’s eyes met his through the glass.

The burned chair pressed harder against his leg, held there by the current. He understood—not in words, but in the body, where older knowledge lived—that there had once been another chair in that room. Or this same chair before burning. That someone had sat, and someone had watched, and someone had failed to rise when the fire took hold. That certain houses kept their furniture even after it was thrown away. That certain women did not leave rooms by doors.

The woman lifted her right hand from the arm of the green chair and extended it toward the window.

Not pleading. Inviting.

Behind him, the flood climbed. Water filled his shoes, gripped his knees, tugged at his coat. The burned chair knocked once against him, gently, insistently, like a dog asking to be let in.

He thought of stepping away. He thought of turning, wading back toward the corner, finding higher ground, hot tea, dry socks, all the bright stupid comforts of survival. He would tell no one. He would forget. People forgot anything if they worked at it. The city itself was built on forgetting—marshes paved, rivers buried, fires painted over, chairs set out with the rubbish.

But the woman was still holding out her hand, and the gesture had become unbearable. One did not refuse recognition twice in the same day.

He placed his palm against the glass opposite hers.

The window was icy. For a moment nothing happened. Rain coursed between them on the outside; darkness held her on the inside. Then warmth bloomed under his hand, slow and terrible, as if the glass had become skin.

The burned chair at his legs shifted. Water lifted it. It turned in the current and settled behind him.

Like something waiting for him to sit.

The woman smiled again, patient, almost kind.

He closed his eyes. The flood rose another inch, and the city, with all its drowned streets and darkened windows, seemed to draw in its breath once more.
