At the corner of Voss and Meridian, where the gutters had begun to speak in their small silver tongues, there was a chair.

It sat beside a heap of swollen trash bags and the ribbed mouth of a storm drain, pushed half into the street as though someone had begun to carry it away and then changed their mind. A dining chair, once. Its back was narrow and tall, with two spindles missing. The seat had been upholstered in something pale, perhaps blue, perhaps gray, but fire had eaten the fabric down to a crust of black foam. One leg was burned clean to a taper, elegant in its injury. It leaned toward the roadway with the posture of an old woman about to confide a secret.

Martin noticed it because of the rain.

On dry days the city was full of abandoned things—mattresses on corners, televisions with blind faces, shoes separated from their partners—and none of them meant anything. But rain restored intention. Rain made every object look recently placed, deliberately displayed. It lacquered the chair’s charred wood until it shone. It filled the cavities in the scorched cushion. It made the missing spindles look less like damage than design.

He had left the office early because the river had risen past the lower promenade and there were rumors about the subway closing. Everyone had been standing at the windows, murmuring with the low pleasure of catastrophe not yet personal. Martin had packed his briefcase with exaggerated calm. Yes, he knew the route. Yes, he had candles. No, he did not live in the evacuation zone, not officially.

Outside, the city had already changed registers. Buses shouldered through brown water at intersections. Cyclists moved like penitents, heads bowed. A siren began somewhere, was answered, and then dissolved into the rain.

At Voss and Meridian, Martin stopped beneath the awning of a shuttered florist and looked at the chair.

It was ridiculous to linger. His shoes were taking on water; his socks had collapsed around his toes. Still, there was the chair with its burned seat and one graceful crippled leg, as if it had escaped a dining room fire and dragged itself here to witness the flood. Who burned a chair and then left it out in the rain? Or had it been struck by lightning? No, chairs were not struck by lightning. Chairs burned indoors, in rooms where people made mistakes.

He might have gone on thinking about it—about kitchens, arguments, a fallen cigarette, a blue flame catching the fringe of a cushion—if the couple had not appeared on the opposite curb.

They were waiting to cross Meridian. Ordinary was the word that offered itself and then failed. They were ordinary in the way certain paintings of fruit were ordinary until one noticed the knife.

The man was tall and soft-looking, with a beige raincoat darkened at the shoulders. He held a black umbrella at an angle that protected the woman more than himself. His hair was thinning in a polite crescent. The woman wore no hood. Rain braided her dark hair to her cheeks and neck, and yet she did not appear wet in the embarrassed, practical way other people did. She stood inside the rain as though inside a room.

They did not speak. That was what caught Martin first. Couples in bad weather usually quarreled with the elements and, by extension, with each other: hurry up, watch the puddle, I told you we should have taken a cab. But these two waited in a quiet so complete it seemed to create a pocket around them. The traffic light changed. The man touched the woman’s elbow. They stepped from the curb.

When they passed the burned chair, the woman turned her head.

Not much. Not enough that anyone else under the awning would have noticed; no one was under the awning but Martin anyway. Her glance was small, almost filial. A person recognizing a relative across a station platform and deciding not to wave.

Then she smiled.

It was not amusement. It was not pity. It was closer to relief.

Martin felt, with an abruptness that embarrassed him, that he had been waiting all afternoon for someone else to see the chair.

The couple continued up Voss Street. Their umbrella moved among other umbrellas, a black disk in a school of black disks. Martin looked in the direction of the subway, then toward home, then back at the couple. The flood warnings would be repeated on the radio; the river would rise whether he watched it or not. But the woman had smiled at the chair. That was a fact, and facts required neighbors. One could not leave them standing alone in the rain.

He stepped from beneath the awning and followed.

At first he kept half a block between them. Nothing suspicious in that; many people were walking north. His briefcase knocked against his knee. Water ran down the back of his collar. He should call his sister, who worried as a hobby and a profession, but the phone in his pocket felt heavy and accusing. Later. Once he knew where they were going. Knew—what an absurd word. He was not investigating anything. He was merely walking in the same direction as a couple who had happened to pass a burned chair.

Still, he matched their pace.

They turned left on Halpern, where the shops were older and narrower, their apartments above them squeezed behind rain-streaked windows. The storm had dimmed the afternoon into something almost evening. In the bakery, a young woman was taping cardboard over the bottom of the door. At the laundromat, machines churned bright circles as if unaware the whole city had become a wash.

The couple walked on. The man occasionally leaned close to say something, but if he spoke, Martin heard none of it. The woman’s hands hung at her sides. She carried no bag, no phone. Once she lifted her palm to the rain, inspected it, and let it fall again.

They crossed Abram Square, where the plane trees were shedding leaves into water that already covered the pavement. Children had chalked hopscotch squares there that morning, maybe, or yesterday, and the numbers wavered beneath the brown sheet of water. The woman paused at the edge of the square.

The man paused with her.

For several seconds they stood facing the empty benches. Martin ducked behind a newspaper kiosk whose headlines announced the storm in letters now bleeding ink. He watched the woman tilt her face upward. Rain struck her eyelids, her mouth. The man looked at her not with impatience, exactly, but with practice. He had waited through this before.

Then she lowered her face and walked on.

What was she? Not who. What. The question entered Martin’s mind with the stealth of mildew. A woman, of course. A wife, perhaps. Someone’s daughter. Someone who liked burned furniture, who greeted rain like a visitation. There was no mystery except the one he was manufacturing to avoid going home to his own dark apartment, his own windows rattling in their frames.

He almost laughed. There he was, Martin Bell, accounts manager, forty-two, divorced without melodrama, a man who sorted receipts by month and owned two identical umbrellas so one could always be lost. Following strangers through a flood because a woman smiled at a chair. Put that on the emergency bulletin.

And yet.

The couple stopped at a narrow house on Carrow Street, three stories of soot-dark brick wedged between a shuttered tailor and an empty lot. It had a green door and a brass knocker shaped like a hand. The windows on the first floor were tall and uncurtained. Warm light glowed beyond them.

The man fumbled for keys. The woman stood beside him, looking not at the door but back down the street.

Martin stepped into the recessed doorway of the tailor. The shadow took him in. He lowered his head and pretended to study his phone, though the screen showed only his own dim face, blurred with rain.

Had she seen him? Her gaze passed over the cars, the gutters, the leaning street signs. It paused—no, surely not—on the doorway where he stood. For an instant he had the sensation of being inspected by something patient and without malice. A chair in a corner. A photograph on a mantel. An object whose usefulness had not yet been determined.

The door opened. The man ushered her inside. Before entering, she touched the green paint with her fingertips. Tenderly. As one might touch the forehead of a sleeping child, or test whether wood was dry enough to burn.

Then the door closed.

Martin remained in the tailor’s doorway.

The rain intensified. It came down now not as weather but as occupation. The street drains were overwhelmed; water gathered along the curb, thick with leaves and cigarette ends. A delivery van rolled past too fast, sending a wave over the sidewalk. Martin jumped back, but his trousers were soaked to the thigh.

Go home. The instruction arrived in his mother’s voice, which was unfair; she had been dead seven years and had no business joining the weather. Go home, Martin. You’ll catch your death.

But the first-floor windows of the house on Carrow Street were uncurtained, and through them he could see the couple moving in a front room.

It was not a glamorous room. A sofa in faded yellow. Bookshelves. A low table with newspapers stacked in squared piles. On the wall, a large framed print of a winter field. No television. No visible mess. Nothing burned.

The man took off his raincoat and hung it on a stand. He said something. The woman did not answer. She stood in the middle of the room with rainwater dripping from her hair onto the floorboards. The man disappeared through an interior door and returned with a towel. He approached her carefully, almost ceremonially, and placed it around her shoulders.

She allowed this.

A tenderness passed through Martin so unexpectedly that he hated it. Of course. It was only marriage. One person bringing another a towel. Hadn’t he once done the same? Hadn’t Clara come in from a storm laughing, her mascara smudged, while he scolded her for forgetting a hat and secretly felt chosen by the sight of her? Perhaps the woman’s smile at the chair meant nothing more than a private joke. Perhaps they had thrown it out themselves. Perhaps it had belonged to an aunt they both despised.

But the chair had been blocks away.

He imagined the burned chair placed at their dining table. The man at one end, the woman at the other, the chair between them like an invited guest. No, like an ancestor.

Inside, the man knelt to untie his shoes. The woman turned toward the window.

Martin froze.

There was no reason she should see him. The tailor’s doorway lay across the street and slightly down from the house, deep in shadow; the rain made a glassy veil between them. Still, she faced the window as if the entire room had been arranged for this purpose. The towel slipped from one shoulder. Her dress—dark wool, he now saw—clung to her narrow frame.

She raised her hand.

Not a wave. Her palm outward, fingers spread.

The burned chair at Voss and Meridian had one arm? No, it had been armless. Dining chairs were armless. But the charred spindles of its back had fanned upward like fingers. Hadn’t they? Two missing, three remaining, blackened and slick. A hand held up from the curb. Wait. Witness. Remember.

Martin stepped back and struck the tailor’s locked door with his shoulder. The sound was small, covered by rain. Inside the house, the man looked up from his shoes. The woman lowered her hand.

Enough. This was enough. Whatever foolish chain his mind was making, it could break it by walking away. He pushed off from the doorway and started down Carrow Street in the direction from which he’d come.

The water was higher already.

It covered the street entirely now, from curb to curb, a moving brown skin wrinkled by rain. On the sidewalk it lapped over the soles of his shoes. Halfway down the block, a storm drain belched, and something pale bobbed up and vanished. The empty lot beside the couple’s house had become a shallow pond where plastic bottles nudged one another like snouts.

He could not remember whether Halpern sloped down or up from here. The city, under water, had lost its grammar. Curbs disappeared; steps became traps; reflections made false doors in the pavement. He took another few steps, then stopped.

From behind him came a sound like wood scraping.

He turned.

Nothing in the street. The green door remained closed. The first-floor windows glowed.

But at the edge of the empty lot, half submerged, something dark rocked in the water.

A branch, surely. A crate. The flood was lifting all manner of debris.

It turned slowly, nudged by the current, and a black spindle rose from the water.

No.

Martin backed away. The thing bumped against the low iron fence of the lot and lodged there. Its shape assembled itself with obscene leisure: the tall narrow back, the broken spindles, the seat caved into a bowl of char. One leg, burned to a taper, trailed beneath the surface like a root.

The chair from Voss and Meridian.

Impossible, unless the water had carried it through the streets. But the current would have had to turn corners, climb grades, choose this block, this lot. It would have had to want something.

He laughed once, too loudly. It was the same kind of chair, that was all. The city was full of discarded furniture. Burned chairs rained from the sky, apparently; there was a municipal surplus. He had not looked closely enough at the first one. He was cold, wet, unnerved. Anyone could be unnerved in a flood.

The chair jerked against the fence.

A wavelet passed through the flooded lot, though no car had gone by. The chair’s front legs dipped and rose. It seemed to be trying to stand.

Martin’s mouth filled with a metallic taste.

In the house, the woman appeared again at the window. The man stood behind her, holding a cup. Steam rose from it. Domestic, harmless steam. He said something over her shoulder. She did not turn. Her eyes were on the chair.

No, not on the chair.

On Martin seeing the chair.

That was the difference, and it opened beneath him. She did not care that the chair had arrived. She cared that he understood it had.

The water around his ankles pulled, insistent. A taxi nosed into the far end of the street, reconsidered, reversed with a chirp of brakes. In upper windows, neighbors had begun to watch the flood, their faces pale rectangles above the street. They did not look at Martin. They did not look at the chair. Catastrophe, when shared, still left certain sights private.

The woman leaned closer to the glass. Her breath made a faint cloud.

And in that cloud he saw it—not with his eyes exactly, or not only with them. He saw the chair as it must have been before burning: set at a table beneath a bright lamp, its cushion pale blue, its spindles polished by hands over years. He saw the woman seated there, younger or unchanged, her dark hair pinned up, her fingers resting on the seat edge. He saw the man across from her, or another man, or no one. He saw flame bloom beneath the chair without consuming her dress. He saw her continue to sit while the cushion blackened, while the wood blistered, while smoke climbed around her like hair. She had not screamed. Why scream, when what was burning was only the part of her that could remain behind?

Nonsense. Vision. Weather in the brain.

But the burned chair outside the lot rocked once more, and the woman’s shoulders moved in the same rhythm.

Martin whispered, “What are you?”

The rain answered by thickening.

The front door opened.

The man stepped out first, pulling his raincoat around him. “Hello?” he called. His voice was ordinary, almost irritated. “Are you all right out there?”

Martin could have replied. Yes. Sorry. Got turned around. Terrible weather. Instead he looked past him.

The woman stood in the doorway barefoot. Water swept over the threshold but did not enter, or seemed not to. The light behind her made a dark shape of her body, narrow-backed, upright, two arms hanging like broken spindles. Her hair clung in ropes. Her face was unreadable.

“You should go home,” the man said. He had seen Martin, then. Of course he had. “The water’s rising fast.”

Home. The word floated uselessly. Home was an apartment on the fourth floor with a half-dead fern and a refrigerator humming to itself. Home was safe, probably. Home had chairs that had never burned, though now he mistrusted them.

The woman lifted one foot and placed it on the top step. The man turned toward her sharply.

“Evelyn,” he said.

There it was: a name. Human, domestic, slightly old-fashioned. Evelyn. Not apparition, not omen. Evelyn who needed towels and hot tea. Evelyn whose husband was tired of strangers staring from doorways.

But when her bare foot touched the floodwater, the chair in the lot shuddered.

The man said her name again, softer this time. Warning or plea.

Evelyn looked at Martin, and for the first time her expression changed. The smile returned, the one she had given the chair. Relief. Recognition. Not of him as a person—no, that would have been comforting—but of his function. He had followed; he had watched; he had carried the sight from street to house. An observer, and therefore a kind of bridge.

He understood then, with a calm that did not belong to him, that the chair had not been discarded. It had been waiting. Or she had. Perhaps there was no difference. Some things burned but did not end. Some things needed water to travel.

The flood rose over his shins.

“Come inside,” the man called, though his face said he regretted it as soon as he spoke. “You can’t stand there.”

Martin almost went. The warm room, the towel, the cup of tea—these exerted a terrible pull. He imagined sitting on the yellow sofa while the man phoned emergency services and Evelyn stood nearby, dripping. He imagined explaining about the chair. He imagined the man’s look: weary, defensive, afraid. How many times had he hidden the truth of her from strangers? How many floods had he dreaded?

But in the empty lot the chair broke free from the fence.

It drifted backward into the street, turning until its ruined seat faced Martin. Rain filled the hollow where the cushion had been. The blackened spindles trembled. It looked less like furniture now than an invitation to sit.

Evelyn stepped down another stair.

The man caught her wrist. “No.”

The word entered the rain and vanished.

Martin took one step toward the chair. Water pushed against his knees. It was cold enough to ache. Behind him someone shouted from an upper window; ahead, the chair bobbed gently, keeping its distance. Evelyn stood motionless on the step, her wrist in the man’s hand, but her eyes were on Martin with a patience older than pity.

He could still turn away. A life could be saved by embarrassment, by the sudden memory of unpaid bills, by the ridiculous thought of wet socks. He could laugh, apologize, wade to higher ground. Tomorrow the city would count damages; crews would clear debris; someone would find a burned chair lodged under a car and curse the sanitation department. Martin would tell no one. In time he would doubt he had seen anything at all.

The chair dipped, and the water inside its ruined seat spilled out like black milk.

Evelyn opened her mouth.

Whether she spoke his name or the chair’s or no word at all, he could not tell. But he felt the sound move through the floodwater into his bones.

The rain, the street, the watching windows, the man at the door—everything became very clear, then very far away. Martin moved forward. Not because he wanted to. Not because he was compelled. Those explanations were for people safely indoors. He moved because the chair had been seen, and what had been seen must be followed to the end.

Behind him the man cried out.

Evelyn laughed then, quietly, or the water did.

The flood climbed to Martin’s thighs. The chair waited just beyond his reach, burned black and shining, ordinary as any discarded thing. He stretched out his hand.

At the window of the house on Carrow Street, in the warm room with the yellow sofa, the glass reflected a woman standing very still. For a moment her dark outline and the chair’s charred back aligned perfectly: spine for spine, spindle for rib, the hollow of the ruined seat where a heart might have been.

Then the streetlight failed, and the water took the shape of everything.
