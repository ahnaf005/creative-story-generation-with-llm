At first, there was nothing remarkable about the couple.

That was what held me.

They stood at the corner of Larch and Tenth beneath the dead traffic light, waiting for the rain to loosen its grip on the afternoon. The man had one hand in the pocket of his gray coat and the other cupped above a cigarette he never managed to light. The woman beside him wore a blue scarf knotted too high around her throat, as if she were cold only at the neck. Her hair was black and straight and wet at the ends. She held a paper bag against her hip, and from the bag’s mouth protruded a loaf of bread, the green frill of celery, and the slick silver corner of something wrapped from the butcher.

Ordinary people, going home.

The street was full of them. Office clerks with their collars turned up. A boy dragging a violin case through gutter water. A deliveryman standing under the awning of the pharmacy and cursing the sky. Since morning, rain had been coming down in hard, stubborn cords. The sewers coughed at every corner. Water moved along the curbs with purpose.

Still, my attention fixed on the couple.

The man said something. The woman did not answer. She was staring across the street, not at the shops, not at the jammed cars or the brown water climbing over the curb, but at the alley beside Wexler’s Hardware.

I looked, too.

There, half in and half out of the alley, sat a burned chair.

It had once been a dining chair, perhaps, with a high back and carved legs. Now the back was a blackened rack, its spindles charred thin as bones. The seat had fallen in. One arm—if it had been an armchair—was gone entirely. Rainwater struck the scorched wood and ran from it in oily threads. Someone had dragged it there and left it, or else it had crawled out of a fire on its own and collapsed in the street to cool.

The woman looked at it with an expression I could not name. Recognition was too simple a word. Grief was too human. She looked like someone standing in a museum before a portrait of an ancestor whose eyes exactly matched her own.

Then the man touched her elbow, and she turned away.

The light changed, though there had been no light to change; perhaps the traffic simply surged. The couple crossed the street. I did, too.

I had not meant to follow them. That is what I told myself at the first corner, and the second. I had been walking that direction anyway. My room was on Marlowe Street, my dinner was a tin of beans and two heels of bread, my evening a blank wall. Anyone might drift behind strangers for a block or two when the rain erased the city’s edges and made every doorway look like the entrance to a life more vivid than one’s own.

They did not hurry. The water was already over the woman’s shoes. She stepped carefully, placing each foot as if the pavement might give way. The man walked slightly ahead, glancing back not at her but at the bag she carried, as though worried the bread would get wet.

At Powell Street the gutters overflowed. At Saint Agnes, the church steps had become a waterfall. Sirens moved somewhere beyond the low roofs, muffled by rain. A taxi stalled in the intersection, its driver standing knee-deep in water and waving his arms at no one.

The couple turned down a narrow lane I knew only by sight: Alder Court, a crooked passage of old brick houses pressed shoulder to shoulder, their lower windows barred, their upper windows curtained. A shallow canal of muddy water already ran down its middle. I stopped at the mouth of the lane.

This was when I should have gone home.

Instead, I followed.

They entered the third house on the left, a narrow red-brick place with a green door and two stone urns on the steps. The urns held dead stems, black from winter. The man fumbled with his keys. The woman stood still behind him, the grocery bag dampening against her coat. Once, she turned her head, and I stepped into the shadow of a plane tree.

Did she see me?

Her eyes moved across the lane, over the tree, over the railings, over the rising water, and for one second it seemed they rested on mine. But there was no alarm in her face. No irritation. Only that same listening look she had worn before the burned chair.

The door opened. Warm yellow light showed briefly in the hall. They went inside.

I remained under the tree.

Rain ticked through the remaining leaves. Water climbed the curb and spread over the pavement with a faint, greedy sound. From inside the house came muffled movements: a door closing, the knock of a cabinet, pipes shuddering in the walls. A square of light appeared in the front room window. The curtains were thin and poorly drawn.

I told myself I was only waiting for the rain to lessen.

Through the gap in the curtains I saw the man remove his coat. He was taller indoors, or perhaps he only seemed so in the low room. He hung the coat on a hook and bent to unlace his shoes. The woman remained standing in the center of the room with the paper bag in her arms.

There was something wrong with the room.

It took me a moment to understand what it was. The room was furnished for absence. A rug lay on the floor, patterned with red vines. A sideboard stood against one wall, bearing a clock, two candlesticks, and a bowl of oranges. There were framed photographs, a standing lamp, shelves of books. But at the center of the room, before the empty hearth, there was a wide space where a chair should have been.

Not simply where a chair might have been. Where a chair had been. The rug showed four pale squares, clean and sharp, where legs had rested for a long time.

The woman looked at the empty place.

The man spoke. He gestured toward the kitchen. She did not move. He came close to her and took the grocery bag. She let him. A celery stalk fell out and rolled under the sideboard. Neither of them bent to retrieve it.

The water in Alder Court rose over the first step of the houses opposite. I felt it seep into my boots.

Inside, the man carried the bag away. The woman walked to the empty square in the room. She stood within it, facing the hearth, as if measuring herself against something invisible.

Then she sat down.

There was no chair beneath her.

For a moment I thought she would fall. Her knees bent, her hands lowered, her body arranged itself with the calm certainty of habit. But she did not drop. She came to rest in the air, seated in a chair that was not there, her back straight, her hands folded in her lap.

I leaned forward, both palms against the wet iron fence.

The woman sat motionless above the rug.

The man returned from the kitchen. He saw her and stopped. His face changed—not to surprise, but to exhaustion. He rubbed both hands over his mouth. Then he crossed the room and knelt before the empty place.

He spoke for a long time.

I could not hear the words. Rain filled the lane. The drains gargled. Somewhere glass broke, followed by shouting. The city was coming apart in water, and still that silent room held me more fiercely than any disaster.

The woman did not answer him. She stared over his shoulder toward the window.

Toward me.

I stepped back. My heel sank into cold water nearly to the ankle. The flood had risen faster than I realized. It covered the lane from wall to wall now, carrying leaves, a child’s red glove, a white plastic cup turning slowly as if considering escape.

In the front room, the man took the woman’s hands. She allowed this. He kissed her knuckles. He pressed his forehead to them. His shoulders shook once, twice. She remained perfectly composed in her invisible chair.

Then the lamp flickered.

For one instant, perhaps less, the room went dark. In that instant the reflection in the window sharpened. I saw not the room but the street behind me, warped by rainwater on the glass.

And in the reflection, beside me, stood the burned chair.

I turned so quickly I slipped and struck my shoulder against the fence.

There was nothing there. Only the lane, the flood, the blurred shine of water under the streetlamps.

When I looked back through the window, the lamp had steadied. The woman was standing now. The man was at the hearth, one hand braced on the mantel. She walked past him, past the empty square on the rug, into the hall. He did not follow at once.

The water reached my calves.

I should explain that I have never been a brave person. I am not one of those men who runs toward fires or pulls strangers from rivers. I have avoided illness, arguments, crowded rooms, dogs, debts, and every form of entanglement. My life has been a careful stepping-around.

Yet when the woman appeared in the upper window, I did not leave.

She stood behind the glass of what must have been a bedroom, framed by darkness. No lamp burned behind her. Her blue scarf was gone, and her wet hair lay against her cheeks. She looked down into Alder Court.

The floodwater carried something large around the corner.

At first I mistook it for a trunk. It came slowly, bumping against submerged steps, turning once, snagging on the railing two houses down before wrenching itself free.

It was the chair.

Not the idea of it, not the reflection. The burned chair from the alley drifted through Alder Court as if delivered. Its blackened back rose above the water, spindles like fingers. It turned in the current and struck the gate before the couple’s house.

The sound was soft: wood against iron.

At the upper window, the woman lifted one hand and touched the glass.

The front door opened.

The man stood in the doorway with water swirling over his feet. He had put his coat back on but not his shoes. His socks darkened instantly. He looked at the chair lodged against the gate. Then he looked up at the woman.

“Please,” he said.

I heard him clearly despite the rain. Perhaps he shouted. Perhaps the word was meant for me, or the water, or whatever had followed them home.

The woman opened the upstairs window. Rain blew inward, stirring the curtains around her like pale arms.

The man waded down one step. “Mara,” he said. “Don’t.”

So she had a name.

Mara looked at him without expression. Then she looked at the chair.

The current pressed it harder against the gate, grinding it there. A strip of burned wood broke away and spun past me. I reached down without thinking and caught it.

It was cold, though it had been born of fire. Black soot slicked my fingers. Beneath the char, the wood smelled faintly sweet, like old smoke in hair.

The man opened the gate. The chair lurched forward into the small garden. Water followed, pouring around the dead urns, rising over the path. He seized the back of the chair with both hands.

The moment he touched it, the woman made a sound.

It was not loud. It was hardly a cry at all. But the hair along my arms lifted. The sound contained no fear, no pain. It was the noise a sleeper might make when someone tries to wake them from a dream they prefer to inhabit.

The man let go.

The chair floated past him and nudged the front step. There it stopped, as if it had arrived not by chance but by appointment.

“Mara,” he said again, and now his voice broke around the name.

She withdrew from the window.

For several seconds nothing moved but the rain and the flood.

Then the door behind the man opened wider.

Mara stood there.

She had come downstairs impossibly fast, or time had folded while I stared at the empty window. She stood on the threshold in her stocking feet, her dress pale beneath the unbuttoned coat. Without the scarf her throat looked exposed and white. There was a dark mark at one side of it, perhaps a bruise, perhaps a shadow.

The man turned. “Go back inside.”

She looked past him. Past the garden. Past the gate.

At me.

I could no longer pretend concealment. I stood in the street with water to my knees, holding a blackened piece of her chair.

Her eyes lowered to my hand.

Then she smiled.

It was a small smile, private and almost courteous, and it filled me with such dread that my fingers opened. The charred fragment fell into the water and vanished.

The man followed her gaze and saw me for the first time. His face tightened. In any ordinary circumstance he might have shouted, demanded, threatened. But no ordinary circumstance remained. The city had become a river. His wife—or whatever she was—stood before a burned chair that had drifted from an alley to their door. And I, a stranger, had followed them home to witness it.

“Help me,” he said.

The words struck harder than the rain.

I moved toward the gate. The water resisted, thick with silt and floating rubbish. By the time I reached the garden, the chair had wedged itself against the bottom step. Mara descended toward it.

The man grabbed her arm.

She turned her head slowly and looked at his hand.

He released her.

“Mara,” he whispered, no longer a warning but an apology.

She stepped into the water. It rose around her ankles, then her calves. Her dress darkened, clinging. She stood before the chair and laid one hand upon its burned back.

There was a hiss.

Not steam. The sound was too soft, too intimate. Like a sigh through teeth.

The rain around us seemed to pause. Even the floodwater lost its voice.

I saw then that the chair was not merely burned. It was burned in the shape of a person. The broken hollow of the seat curved like hips. The remaining arm jutted at the height of an elbow. Across the back, among the charred spindles, the blackening gathered in long strands, hairlike, and one pale patch of unburned wood gleamed where a face might turn toward a window.

Mara bent her head.

For an instant, her body and the chair’s ruined outline aligned: her spine with the back, her arms with its arms, her dark hair with the soot. She did not appear to touch the chair so much as return to it, like a flame finding the wick it had left smoking.

The man made a strangled sound and stepped forward. I caught his sleeve. I do not know why. Perhaps because he had asked for help, and this was the only help I could imagine: preventing him from interrupting what could not be stopped.

He fought me once, weakly. Then he sagged.

The water rose to the second step.

Mara sat.

She lowered herself onto the burned chair as naturally as she had sat upon the empty air inside. The ruined seat should not have held her. The legs should have snapped. Instead the chair settled beneath her with solemn obedience.

The flood lifted it.

At first only slightly. Then the current turned it toward the gate. Mara sat upright, hands folded in her lap, face calm. The chair floated backward through the drowned garden.

The man broke from me and lunged after her, but the water had grown deep and strong. He stumbled. I seized him under the arms before he went under. Together we watched her pass through the open gate into Alder Court.

She drifted there in the rain, seated on her blackened throne.

As she passed me, her eyes met mine one last time. I expected accusation, or gratitude, or the empty stare of the dead.

Instead I saw recognition.

Not of me, perhaps, but of my watching. As if all evening she had known I was necessary. A witness. A stranger to carry the sight away and be unable to set it down.

The current took her toward Larch Street, toward the alley, toward whatever fire had first made room for her in the world. The man called her name until the flood swallowed the sound.

By morning, the waters had begun to recede.

They found branches in the tram wires, fish in basement kitchens, a piano lodged beneath the bridge. They found three cars overturned near Saint Agnes and an old woman alive on the roof of a bakery. They did not find Mara. They did not find the chair.

I went back to Larch and Tenth when the streets were passable. The alley beside Wexler’s Hardware was empty except for a crescent of soot on the bricks, untouched by rain.

For weeks afterward, I avoided Alder Court. Then, because avoidance is only another form of following, I went.

The green door was painted black. The urns were gone. Through the front window I saw the room had been rearranged. A table occupied the center now, with two straight-backed chairs tucked beneath it.

Only two.

No empty square showed on the rug.

And yet, as I stood there, a woman passed behind me on the street, laughing softly to herself. I turned. She was only a stranger beneath an umbrella.

Still, for one moment, I smelled smoke.

Not the harsh stink of a house fire, but the faint sweet smell of old wood burning slowly in the rain.
