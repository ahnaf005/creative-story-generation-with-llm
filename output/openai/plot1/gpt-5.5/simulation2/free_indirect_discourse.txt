By five o’clock the rain had been falling for two days, and the city had begun to show its seams. Water gathered in the bus lanes and trembled there, brown and shining. It came up through grates in the pavement with a patient glugging sound, as though something underneath had been holding its breath and could hold it no longer. At the corners, pedestrians stood in little herds, pretending to wait for the light when what they were really waiting for was courage.

Martin had nowhere to go that required courage. He had left the museum early because the galleries were empty and the roof had started dripping into the room of Dutch interiors, a faint, regular plink between a woman peeling apples and a child holding a hoop. He had rolled up his umbrella badly. One spoke stuck out like a broken finger. It didn’t matter. His coat was wet at the shoulders, his socks wet inside his shoes. It was all wet now, so one might as well become part of it.

He saw the chair on Alder Street.

It had been left beside a heap of split rubbish bags, just beyond the awning of a closed bakery. A dining chair, or what had once been a dining chair, with a narrow back and carved rails. Someone had painted it pale blue years ago, but fire had taken most of that away. One side was blackened to a crisp sheen, the legs on that side shortened into blunt, charred stumps. The seat was split open, stuffing dark with rain. It looked less discarded than escaped, as though it had crawled there from some private disaster and collapsed in public.

Martin stopped. People stepped around him. A delivery cyclist rang his bell though there was ample room to pass.

What was it about the chair? Not pity, surely. A chair had no claim on pity. Still, it sat with such exhausted dignity, holding its ruined posture in the rain. Burned, soaked, put out with the trash. How familiar. How theatrical. He almost laughed, and then he did not.

That was when the couple passed.

They were ordinary in the way certain people become extraordinary by refusing to offer any reason for attention. The man was tall but not remarkably tall, wearing a dark green raincoat with a belt tied carelessly behind him. He carried two paper grocery bags against his chest, each bag darkening at the bottom where the rain had touched it. The woman walked half a step ahead, bareheaded, her hair pinned up in a twist that the weather had loosened strand by strand. She wore a grey wool coat too fine for the day and held a bunch of yellow tulips upside down, as if she had forgotten they were flowers and not keys.

They paused near the burned chair. The woman looked at it. Not for long—hardly longer than anyone might look at a strange thing in the street—but she looked, and in that instant something passed between her and the wrecked object. Recognition? No, that was absurd. But her face changed. It did not soften. It emptied.

The man said something. Martin could not hear it over the rain. The woman smiled then, quickly, and the emptiness vanished, shut behind the smile as behind a door. They moved on.

Martin stood beside the chair and watched them go.

Well, why not walk that way? His own flat was not that way, true, but his own flat contained an unmade bed, a saucepan in the sink, and a ceiling stain in the shape of Greenland. No one waited there. No one would remark on whether he arrived at six or nine or not at all. And the couple had taken Alder Street east, which eventually led toward the river. It might be useful to see how high the river had risen. Practical, almost civic-minded.

He followed.

Not closely. He was not a fool. He kept half a block between them when he could, slowing at windows, pausing under awnings, pretending to examine menus of restaurants closed by the weather. They were easy to track: the green of the man’s coat, the bright drooping tulips in the woman’s hand. Twice the man looked back, but not as if suspicious. He looked back the way people do in heavy rain, to confirm the world remained behind them.

The streets grew narrower. The gutters overflowed and erased the distinction between pavement and road. Cars pushed through with a slow, vulgar confidence, throwing waves against the shopfronts. In the low places, water lapped over the curb and spread across the sidewalk in reflective sheets. Martin’s shoes filled and emptied with each step. The paper bags the man carried began to sag.

They did not hurry. That was part of the fascination. Everyone else was rushing, hunched and resentful, but they moved as if weather were a room they had entered together and did not mind. At one corner, the woman stopped beneath the broken sign of a locksmith and retied the belt of the man’s raincoat. He bent his head to make the task easier. She used both hands, still holding the tulips, so the flowers bobbed against his hip, ridiculous and tender.

Married, Martin decided. Or nearly married. Or long past married, which could look much the same from behind. There was something practiced in the way he shifted the groceries from one arm to the other before she asked. Something careless in the way she touched him, as if his body were partly hers. Martin had once been touched that way in a train station in Lyon, or had he invented the station later because memory required architecture? It didn’t matter. Some woman, some platform, a hand at his sleeve. Gone now into the great administrative silence of former lives.

The couple turned onto Rill Lane, where the houses stood narrow and soot-stained, their ground-floor windows barred against a crime no longer fashionable. Basements here were always flooding. Even in dry months the lane smelled of damp brick and old leaves. Today water coursed along both sides of the street, carrying cigarette ends, petals, a child’s red glove. The drains made hopeless sucking noises.

Number 18 had a blue door. The woman climbed the steps first and fumbled in her pocket. The man stood below her, shoulders dark with rain, grocery bags collapsing inward like lungs. For a moment the woman turned and looked back down the lane.

Martin stepped behind a plane tree.

Childish. If she had seen him, what then? A man on a public street in a public rain. He could be anyone. He was anyone.

When he looked again, they had gone inside.

He should have left. The story, if it had ever been a story, was over. Ordinary couple returns home in storm. Woman with tulips. Man with groceries. Chair burns elsewhere, unrelated. Curtain.

But the house held him.

There was a basement areaway with black iron railings. A narrow front window at street level glowed yellow behind wet glass. Above it, the parlor window remained dark for several minutes, then bloomed with light. Shadows moved across the curtains.

Martin crossed the street and stood under the inadequate shelter of a sycamore. Rain ticked through the leaves onto his scalp. From here he could see into the basement window if he bent a little. Not clearly, not rudely—nothing so sordid. Shapes only: shelves, perhaps, a hanging coat, the white square of a washing machine. The water in the gutter had risen over the curb and was spreading around his ankles.

He would wait until the rain eased. That was all. Sensible. The buses would be delayed; the underground might be closed near the river. And there was a small awe in watching a lit house during a flood, a domestic aquarium, lives continuing behind glass while the city dissolved outside.

In the parlor window the curtains had not been drawn all the way. Through the narrow gap he saw the man’s arm reach upward and place something on a shelf. Then the woman crossed into view. She had taken off the grey coat. Beneath it she wore a dark dress, severe at the collar. Her loosened hair fell along one side of her face, blackened by water, or by the shadow of the room. She carried the tulips now upright in a jar.

She stood very still.

The man entered behind her. He said something—silent through glass, rain, distance. She did not turn. He set a hand lightly on her shoulder. She flinched.

No, not flinched. Perhaps merely shivered. A wet person in a cold room shivers. Martin, come now.

But the man withdrew his hand. The woman moved to the mantel and placed the flowers there. Yellow against the dim room. Flames, almost. Little flames with green stems.

Water swirled around Martin’s shoes, then halfway up his laces. Somewhere a siren wailed and faded. A neighbor emerged two houses down, dragging a rolled carpet from a basement stairwell, cursing at the rain as if the rain were capable of shame. Martin did not move to help. The neighbor did not look at him.

In the parlor, the couple had disappeared.

The basement light flickered.

Martin bent again, peering through the lower window. The glass was streaked with water, but he could see them more clearly now. They were in the basement. The man had rolled up his sleeves and was lifting boxes from the floor to a high shelf. The woman stood near the stairs with her arms folded. Around them were the accumulated embarrassments of a household: paint tins, a folded ironing board, a lampshade without a lamp, bundles of newspapers tied with string.

And a chair.

Martin’s breath caught so sharply that he coughed.

It stood in the center of the basement, facing the window. Not the same chair, of course. Impossible. The burned chair was on Alder Street, half a mile away, rain filling its split seat. This chair was whole. Unburned. Pale blue. Narrow back, carved rails. A dining chair, old-fashioned, almost delicate.

The woman looked at it too.

The man lifted another box. He spoke; she shook her head. He set the box down and approached the chair. The woman put out a hand—not touching him, only the air between them—and he stopped.

How expressive people were when one could not hear them. Without words, everything became truer or more false. The man’s mouth opened in a question. The woman’s face hardened. She took one step toward the chair. Martin could not see her eyes, but he felt the direction of them. She was not looking at furniture. No one had ever looked at furniture like that unless furniture had first looked back.

Water pressed cold through Martin’s trousers. It was up to his ankles now, moving with intent. The drains had surrendered. From the end of the lane came the slow push of a brown current, widening, confident, carrying leaves, a plastic bottle, a lemon. The flood was entering the street not dramatically but administratively, taking possession.

Inside, the woman reached for the chair.

Her fingers touched the top rail. She closed her hand around it.

At once the basement light went out.

Martin straightened. Darkness filled the lower window, and in the darkness his own reflection appeared: pale face, wet hair, the ridiculous broken umbrella hooked over one wrist. Behind his reflection, a faint movement, perhaps. He leaned closer.

The light returned.

The chair was blackened.

Not entirely. Not as the one on Alder Street had been, not yet. But one side of the seat was scorched, the pale blue paint bubbled and peeled. Smoke, or what looked like smoke, threaded upward in the bright basement air. The woman stood beside it with her hand still on the back rail. The man had retreated against the shelves. His face was visible now, white and open with fear.

Martin smiled.

He had not meant to. The smile simply arrived, small and private. Because of course. Of course there had to be an explanation, though not the kind anyone would accept in daylight. The chair on Alder Street had not been discarded after a fire; it had been sent ahead. A warning? A husk? The shed skin of this chair, or its future, thrown out into the rain for anyone to see. For him to see. And the woman—what was she? Not witch; that word belonged to plays and village gossip. Not ghost either, since ghosts did not buy tulips or retie a man’s belt in the rain. She was something more domestic and more terrible. A hearth gone wrong. A room remembering its own burning.

He laughed then, but the rain took the sound.

The man moved suddenly. He seized the woman’s wrist and pulled her hand from the chair. She turned on him. For a second her face filled the basement window, enlarged by wet glass and yellow light, and Martin saw—or believed he saw—black streaks down her cheeks, as if soot were running from her eyes.

The flood climbed his calves.

He should go. The thought came gently, almost fondly, like advice from a nurse. He should go before the water reached the electrics in these houses, before cars stalled and floated sideways, before some policeman in a reflective coat ordered everyone uphill. He should go home and place his wet shoes by the radiator, though the radiator rarely worked, and make tea. He should never have followed strangers through rain. He should never have stopped for a burned chair like an omen in a cheap novel.

Instead he gripped the railing of Number 18 and lowered one foot from the curb into the street. The current tugged at him, not strong, not yet. The basement window was at chest height now because he stood in the road, and he could see the water beginning to pour down the areaway steps, a dark tongue licking at the door below.

Inside, the man had lifted the chair. He held it awkwardly before him, as if it were both shield and offering. The burned side smoked harder. The woman stood near the washing machine, arms loose at her sides. She seemed calm now. Almost bored. The tulips upstairs glowed in Martin’s mind, yellow flames on the mantel.

The man carried the chair toward the basement door that led to the areaway. He meant to throw it out. Sensible man. Remove the object, remove the danger. Husbands had built whole religions on such thinking.

He opened the door.

Water rushed in.

It struck his shins and spread across the basement floor, lifting old envelopes, nudging paint tins. The man staggered. The chair fell from his hands and landed seat-down in the rising water. A hiss came through the glass—or perhaps Martin imagined it—the sound of coals quenched. Steam clouded the window.

When it cleared, the woman was looking out.

At him.

There was no uncertainty this time. She saw him. Across the flooded areaway, through the rain-lashed pane, between the yellow basement and the brown street, she fixed him with a look of mild recognition, as if he had arrived late to an appointment she had made without telling him.

Martin raised one hand.

Absurd gesture. Apology? Greeting? Surrender? His fingers trembled in the cold.

The woman smiled. It was the smile from Alder Street, the quick door-closing smile. Behind her, the man shouted silently, wrestling with the chair as it bumped against his knees. The water inside was rising fast, already above his calves. Outside, the street current thickened around Martin’s thighs.

The chair turned slowly in the basement flood.

For a moment its back faced the window. Pale blue rails, carved, elegant. Then the current rolled it, and the burned side came into view. Black, shining, familiar. It was the Alder Street chair exactly now, ruined in the same pattern, the same crippled legs, the same split seat drinking water. Not similar. The same. Martin knew it with the useless certainty one brings to dreams.

Had he seen the future abandoned on the curb? Or had the past washed loose and circled back? Perhaps the city was full of such things in flood weather: tomorrow’s wreckage floating into yesterday, private disasters surfacing at random corners. Perhaps all anyone needed was to pay attention. Most people did not. They stepped around omens, rang bicycle bells at them, hurried home with groceries.

But he had stopped. He had followed. He had been chosen, or had chosen himself, which amounted to the same loneliness.

The water rose to his waist.

The cold entered him with a clean, bright pain. He clung to the railing. Upstairs in Number 18, the parlor light flickered, and for an instant the curtains glowed orange, not with electricity but with fire. He smelled smoke through the rain: wet wood, scorched paint, hair. Impossible, with all this water. Impossible was becoming a small word, a dry word, no use here.

The woman began to climb the basement stairs. Slowly. The flood swirled around her dress, but the fabric did not float. It clung to her as if soaked not with water but ash. The man remained below, bent over the chair. He seemed unable to let it go. Or it would not let him. The blackened rails knocked softly against his chest.

Martin imagined calling out. Madam, your husband. Madam, the water. Madam, I saw the chair before it burned.

But she already knew.

At the top of the stairs she paused and looked once more toward the window. Not at him now, perhaps, but through him—to Alder Street, to the bakery awning, to the heap of rubbish bags where the burned chair sat or would sit, patient in the rain. The circle closing. The furniture of a life arranged and rearranged until all rooms became one room, all floods one flood, all fires one fire.

Then the basement light went out again.

In darkness, Martin heard the chair strike the window from inside.

Once.

Twice.

On the third strike, the glass cracked in a white star.

He let go of the railing. The current took him a step, only a step, and turned him sideways. Rain filled his ears. The broken umbrella drifted from his wrist and sailed off like a small black boat. He could still see the house, its blue door above the flood, its windows lit and unlit by turns. He could still see, or thought he could, the woman standing in the parlor with the tulips burning beside her.

The cracked basement window gave way.

Water rushed in; something rushed out. A chair, blackened and pale blue, bobbing low, turning once as it entered the street. It floated past Martin within reach.

He did not touch it.

He watched it go, solemn and upright for one uncanny second, as though someone invisible sat upon it, being carried through the flooded city with perfect composure. Then the current drew it away toward Alder Street, toward the place where it had always already been waiting, burned and drenched and dignified in the rain.
