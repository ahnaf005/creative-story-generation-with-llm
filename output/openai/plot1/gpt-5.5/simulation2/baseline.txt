The rain had been falling since morning with the dull patience of someone waiting for a confession.

By four o’clock the gutters were already full, and the streets shone black and yellow under the traffic lights. People moved through the weather with their shoulders raised, umbrellas tilted forward like shields. Buses sighed at the curbs. Water climbed over the toes of shoes and licked at the steps of closed shops.

I had nowhere in particular to go.

That was not unusual for me then. I had been let go from the archives three weeks earlier, after thirteen years of organizing the lives of others into labeled boxes. Births, marriages, bankruptcies, deeds, wills. I had spent my days among proof that people had passed through the world, and now, outside it, I found myself unrecorded and strangely transparent. I walked for hours. I watched faces. I invented destinations for strangers.

I first noticed the chair on Lark Street, tipped on its side beside a storm drain.

It was a dining chair, or had been. One of the back legs was missing, and the woven seat had burned away to a blackened ring. Its arms—odd for a dining chair, but there they were, narrow and formal—were scorched halfway down, the varnish bubbled into dark blisters. Rainwater pooled in the hollow where the seat had been and overflowed in small, nervous tremors whenever a car passed.

A scrap of paper was taped to its back.

FREE, someone had written in thick marker, though the ink had run in the rain until the word looked less like an offer than a warning.

I stopped in front of it. I do not know why. There was something indecent about the chair, the way it had been abandoned after damage had revealed its inner wood. It seemed not merely discarded but accused.

As I stood there, a couple came around the corner and slowed beside it.

They were ordinary in the deliberate way some people are ordinary: neatly dressed, neither young nor old, careful in their movements. The man wore a gray raincoat, belted tight, and carried a brown paper parcel under one arm. The woman wore a green coat with a hood, though she had not raised it. Her hair was black and cut to her jaw, the ends wet and sharp against her cheeks.

They stopped at the chair.

The man said something I could not hear over the rain.

The woman did not answer at once. She looked down at the burned thing, and I saw her face change. Not much. A tightening at the mouth, perhaps; a widening of the eyes. But it was as if the rain around her paused.

Then she smiled.

It was not amusement. It was recognition.

The man touched her elbow. She turned away, and they continued down Lark Street, stepping around the spreading water. I remained by the chair until they had nearly reached the next corner.

Then I followed them.

I have often tried to explain this to myself. There was no desire in it, not of the usual kind. I did not want the woman, nor the man, nor anything they carried. I wanted only to know what had passed between her and the ruined chair. In the archives, one document always led to another: a deed to a dispute, a death certificate to a birth, a name crossed out and written again elsewhere. I had lived too long believing everything had a trail. That afternoon, in the rain, I simply took one.

They walked unhurriedly, though the weather worsened. Twice the man looked up at the sky with irritation, as if the storm were a late train. The woman kept her eyes forward. Once she slipped her hand through his arm, but the gesture seemed practiced rather than affectionate, like a line spoken at the proper cue.

I kept half a block behind them.

At the corner of Lark and Bessemer, water burst from a manhole in a dirty fountain. A taxi stalled in the intersection, horn blaring in short panicked bursts. People shouted from under awnings. The couple did not stop. They crossed through water already over their ankles. The woman lifted her skirt with one hand, exposing pale calves, and the man held the parcel high.

They turned onto Calder Avenue, a street of narrow brick houses pressed shoulder to shoulder. Basement windows glowed at pavement level, each one a small submerged eye. The drains had given up. Water ran along both curbs in fast brown streams, carrying leaves, cigarette ends, a child’s red glove.

Halfway down the block, the couple climbed the steps of number 38.

I passed on the opposite side and kept walking until I reached the corner. There I stopped beneath a dripping sycamore and looked back.

Number 38 was like the others: three stories, black railings, a brass knocker polished to a small dull moon. In the second-floor window hung lace curtains. On the ground floor, no curtains at all. The couple had entered; light bloomed behind the glass, warm and immediate.

I should have gone then.

Instead I crossed back and stood in the narrow areaway between number 38 and the empty house next door. The rain fell harder there, funneled by the brick walls. Water drummed on trash can lids and splashed cold against my legs. From that angle I could see through the ground-floor window into their front room.

They had not drawn the shades.

The room surprised me. I had expected the usual modest comforts: sofa, bookshelves, framed photographs, lamps. There were lamps, yes, and a sofa upholstered in cream fabric, and a cabinet with glass doors. But the center of the room was bare except for a square of darker floorboards, as though something large had stood there for years and had recently been removed.

The man placed the parcel on a side table. He took off his raincoat and shook it sharply before hanging it on a hook. The woman stood in the center of the room without removing her coat. Water darkened the shoulders and ran down in threads.

The man spoke to her. Again, I could not hear. The rain filled my ears.

She looked at him, then toward the bare square on the floor.

He turned away first. He unwrapped the parcel. Inside was a bottle of wine and two white paper packages tied with string. Dinner, perhaps. Bread and cheese. Something ordinary.

The woman raised her hands slowly to her throat and unbuttoned her green coat. Beneath it she wore a dark dress with long sleeves. When she slipped the coat from her shoulders, I saw that the dress was marked across the back with pale streaks, as if rubbed by ash.

I leaned closer to the window.

At once the water in the areaway surged around my shoes. It had risen to my ankles without my noticing. The storm drain at the street gave a long choking gurgle.

Inside, the man had gone into another room. The woman remained alone. She stood with her back to me, facing the empty square of floor. Then, with extraordinary care, she bent her knees and lowered herself into a sitting position in the air.

I thought at first she meant to sit on the floor, but she did not. She stopped halfway, thighs level, hands resting lightly on nothing. Her back straightened. Her chin lifted. She assumed the posture of a person seated in a high-backed chair.

In the empty room, she sat upon absence.

The hair at the nape of my neck prickled.

She did not waver. She seemed supported. Her hands curved over invisible arms. Her shoulders relaxed, and her face—reflected faintly in the dark window—settled into an expression of grave contentment.

Then the man returned.

He froze in the doorway.

For several seconds neither moved. The room was perfectly still except for the trembling of lamplight in the window glass.

He said something. His mouth formed her name perhaps, or a warning. She turned her head toward him with the slow, courteous attention of a queen receiving a servant. The smile she had given the burned chair returned to her lips.

The man crossed the room quickly. He reached for her arm.

She vanished.

No—not vanished. She collapsed. There is no other word. The shape of her seated body loosened all at once, as if the strings holding her upright had been cut. She fell sideways to the floor, striking the boards with a sound I imagined more than heard. The man dropped to his knees beside her.

I pressed both palms to the wet brick wall. The rain coursed down my sleeves.

After a moment she sat up. The man helped her to her feet. He was speaking rapidly now. She listened with apparent calm, then turned and looked directly toward the window.

Toward me.

I stepped back, but the areaway was narrow, and the water resisted my movement. It had risen above my ankles, cold as cellar stone. For one second her eyes met mine through the rain-streaked glass.

They were not startled. They were expectant.

I stumbled out to the sidewalk.

The street had changed. In the time I had watched, Calder Avenue had become a canal. Water covered the pavement from house to house, swirling around lampposts and stoops. It carried trash bags, a broom, a loosened flowerpot spilling soil like blood. Far down the block someone shouted for help from an upstairs window. The taxi horn from earlier sounded again, distant and absurd.

I should have left then, truly. The flood was rising fast; anyone sensible would have moved to higher ground. But I could not tear myself away from number 38.

I climbed the steps of the empty house next door and crouched beneath its shallow portico. From there I could still see into the couple’s room.

The man had drawn one curtain halfway, but not enough. Through the gap I saw him pacing. The woman stood near the mantel. She had removed her shoes. Her bare feet were very white against the dark floor.

He went to the window and looked out at the street. I flattened myself against the door of the empty house. When he turned away, I looked again.

The woman was laughing.

Not loudly—I could tell by the movement of her shoulders. A silent, helpless laughter. The man seized her by both arms. She stopped laughing and brought one hand gently to his cheek. He flinched as though burned.

There it was again: burned.

The chair on Lark Street returned to me with such force that for a moment I smelled smoke beneath the rain. The blistered arms. The hollow where the seat had been. The word FREE running down the back like a sentence dissolving.

Had it come from their house? Had they carried it out that morning, in shame or fear, and left it by the drain for the city to take? Had the fire been accidental? Had she sat in it when it burned?

I imagined the chair in the room’s bare square, its legs planted precisely where she had lowered herself into the air. I imagined her seated there night after night while the man moved about quietly, serving dinner, opening wine, avoiding her eyes. I imagined flame starting small beneath the seat, a coal dropped from the hearth, a match, a deliberate hand. I imagined her refusing to rise.

The water reached the second step of the house where I crouched.

Across the street, basement windows disappeared one by one under the brown surface. The rain made the flood seem to boil.

Inside number 38, the lights flickered. The man went from lamp to lamp as if he could persuade electricity to behave by vigilance. The woman had returned to the center of the room.

She stood over the bare square.

The man saw and shook his head violently. He took a step toward her. She raised one finger, and he stopped.

It was a small gesture. Tender, almost. Do not interrupt.

Then she sat again.

This time I watched without blinking. She lowered herself with the same careful grace, but as she did, something darkened around her. At first I thought it was a trick of the flickering lamps, shadows thrown by the rain. But the darkness took shape: a vertical line behind her spine, two curved arms beneath her hands, a black ring below her.

A chair appeared around her by degrees.

Not wholly. Not solidly. It emerged the way breath fogs glass, delicate and incomplete. A high back, charred along one side. Arms scarred by fire. No woven seat, only a blackened rim. It was impossible, and yet I recognized it. The chair from Lark Street was in the room, or its memory was, or something that had worn its form.

The woman sat upon it as if she had never left.

The man covered his face.

Water slapped against my knees.

A siren wailed somewhere beyond the block, then cut off. In the house beside me, the locked door trembled with the pressure of water below. I thought of archives again: paper swelling, ink loosening, names dissolving into gray pulp. Records drowned. Proof erased. Perhaps that was all a flood was—a great unfiling.

The woman turned her head toward the window. I do not know whether she could see me in the portico’s shadow. I believe she could.

Her smile widened.

Then the glass of the front window cracked.

Not from within. From below. The flood had reached the sill and pressed against the pane with the weight of the street behind it. A thin fracture leapt upward like lightning. The man lunged toward the woman, but she remained seated, hands resting on the burned arms of her invisible-visible throne.

The crack spread.

I shouted then. I do not remember what. A warning, perhaps. Her name, though I did not know it. The man turned toward the window just as the glass gave way.

Water burst into the room.

It came not as a spill but as an animal. The curtain snapped back. The lamp nearest the window toppled and went out. Brown water surged across the floor, lifting the white paper packages from the table, carrying them in circles. The man was knocked backward against the sofa.

The chair did not move.

The water rushed around its legs, though they were only half there, only shadow and scorch. The woman sat above the flood, dry from the knees upward, serene. The man struggled to stand. He called to her—I saw it in the shape of his mouth, the anguish of it.

She looked down at him with pity.

Then she rose.

As soon as she stood, the chair was gone. The flood swallowed the bare square, hiding all evidence of absence. The woman stepped forward into the water, which reached her thighs now, then her waist. Her dress spread around her like ink.

The man grabbed her hand. For a moment they clung to each other. Ordinary again: a husband and wife in danger, two frightened people in a room filling with water.

Then she looked past him, through the broken window, out into the street.

At me.

She lifted her free hand and pointed.

Not at my face. Not at the house. Down the street, toward Lark Street.

Toward the place where the burned chair had been abandoned.

I turned.

Through the veil of rain and the long brown channel of Calder Avenue, I saw something moving on the water. It came slowly, bumping against parked cars, spinning when the current caught it. At first I thought it was a crate or a piece of fencing.

Then it struck a lamppost and turned.

The burned chair drifted toward us.

It should have sunk. It should not have navigated corners or found its way through the flood like a returning dog. Yet there it was, black arms raised, hollow seat brimming, the scrap of paper still clinging to its back.

FREE, the rain had written again and again, until the word had become only three black wounds.

The chair floated past me so close I could have touched it. I did not. The water was at my waist now, and the cold had entered my bones. It moved to the broken window of number 38 and lodged there, half in, half out, as if waiting to be invited.

Inside, the woman gave a cry—not fear, not joy, but something older than either. She pulled her hand from the man’s grasp.

He tried to hold her. He failed.

She waded to the window.

The chair tipped forward. For one strange second, I thought she meant to climb out and sit in it there, in the flood, before all of us. But when she reached it, she placed both hands on its burned arms and bent her head as if listening.

The man came up behind her. His face was twisted, pleading. He touched her shoulder.

She turned, kissed him once on the mouth, and stepped through the window.

The chair sank.

So did she.

There was no struggle. The water closed over her black hair, her green coat floating up behind her like weed. The man screamed soundlessly behind the broken glass. I plunged forward without thinking, but the current caught the chair—or what was left of it—and spun it away. Something struck my hip. I went under.

The flood was full of objects and darkness. For a moment I saw the world beneath the street: shoes, leaves, silver bubbles, a woman’s pale hand resting on a charred wooden arm. Then the current rolled me hard against a railing, and I came up choking.

Neighbors pulled me onto the steps of a house near the corner. Later there were boats, blankets, questions. I told them about the woman in number 38. Rescue workers entered through an upstairs window when the water dropped. They found the man alive on the second-floor landing, half mad with cold. They did not find her.

They did find, in the front room, four deep marks in the floorboards, arranged in a square.

Where a chair had stood.

No one cared for that detail. There were gas leaks to manage, missing people to count, insurance forms to file. The official report said the flood had taken her. It did not mention the burned chair.

But three days later, when the waters receded and the city began dragging its ruined furniture to the curbs, I walked back to Lark Street.

The storm drain was clogged with mud and leaves. Beside it lay the scrap of paper, washed clean of tape.

FREE, it said, the letters blurred but legible.

There was no chair.

Still, whenever it rains with that same steady patience, I find myself stopping at windows, searching lit rooms for a bare square of floor. And sometimes, in the reflections on the glass, I see a woman seated behind me where there is no seat at all—hands resting lightly on burned arms, face calm, waiting for the water to rise.
