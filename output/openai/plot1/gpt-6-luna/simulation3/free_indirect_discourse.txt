The chair was already burning when Daniel first saw it.

Not in flames. That would have been a different matter, one involving a call to the fire brigade and people leaning out of windows. It was simply blackened, abandoned beside a heap of wet leaves on Bell Street, its backrest charred to a ragged oval. One wooden arm remained pale and smooth, as if the fire had stopped there out of politeness. Rain shone in the seat’s cracked leather.

Someone had dragged it to the curb for collection. Someone had decided it was no longer worth keeping. Daniel knew the look of things set out to be taken away. He had been set out that way himself, once, though he preferred not to think of it.

He stopped beside the chair. The street smelled of rain and old brick. Beyond the row of shops, the river lay hidden behind warehouses and its own low gray mist. A warning had been posted on the bridge: HIGH WATER EXPECTED. People were used to warnings here. They went on buying bread and complaining about buses.

Daniel touched the pale arm with one finger. The wood was dry.

Then a couple came along the pavement.

They were ordinary, he thought at once, which was perhaps why he watched them. The man wore a navy coat and carried a paper bag of groceries. The woman had a rust-colored scarf and short, dark hair, damp at the ends. Neither was young, neither old. They walked close enough that their sleeves brushed now and then, but they did not hold hands.

There they were, two people walking home. Nothing to see.

The man stepped around a puddle. The woman stepped around it a beat later, placing her foot in exactly the same narrow strip of dry pavement. He glanced at a shop window; she glanced at it too. Not together, not quite. Just the little repetitions of long familiarity. A private rhythm worn smooth by years.

Daniel felt the rhythm pass through him. He had once walked that way with someone. Or imagined he had. The memory was less a scene than a set of sensations: a sleeve against his, a shared umbrella, the comfort of not needing to decide where to go next.

The couple reached the chair. The woman slowed.

Only slightly. A hesitation. Her gaze went to the blackened backrest, then to the pale arm. She looked at it with such quiet attention that Daniel felt he had interrupted something. The man took two more steps before noticing she had stopped.

“You all right?” he asked.

She nodded. “Yes.”

They moved on.

Daniel should have kept walking. He had errands, technically. There was a pharmacy, a small grocery, a letter he had meant to post for three days. But the couple had turned toward the lower streets, and he found himself following them at a distance.

It wasn’t following, exactly. They were going the same way. The city belonged to everyone. He paused at a bus shelter when they paused at a crossing; looked into a closed café window when they passed beneath its striped awning. The man’s paper bag bumped his leg. The woman’s scarf lifted in the wind, then settled against her throat like a hand.

At the next intersection, they turned down a narrow street sloping toward the river. Daniel waited until they were half a block ahead.

The rain thickened. It made the pavements shine and the windows blacken, turning each house into a dark reflection of itself. At the bottom of the street, the gutters were already full. Water slid along the curb in a steady brown stream, carrying leaves and cigarette packets toward the river.

The couple stopped at a green door.

There. Home.

The man searched his pockets for keys. The woman stood behind him, looking back up the hill. For one strange second Daniel thought she was looking directly at him. But her eyes passed over him, or through him, and settled on the street beyond.

Then the door opened, and the couple disappeared inside.

Daniel kept walking. Their house had a bay window on the ground floor, its curtains drawn halfway. A lamp glowed behind them, yellow and intimate. He went past, crossed the street, then doubled back to a narrow passage between buildings, where he could see the window without standing directly beneath it.

Ridiculous. He knew that. A man did not hide in a passage to watch strangers eat supper. He was not that sort of man.

Still, he waited.

The first warning siren sounded at six. Far off, a long rising note that seemed to come from the river itself. In the house, a shadow moved across the window. A hand lifted the curtain, then let it fall.

Daniel could go now. The rain had soaked through his coat. His shoes were wet. The water in the gutter had risen over the curb and was lapping at the bricks of the passage.

The couple emerged into the lighted room. The man set groceries on the table. The woman took out a loaf, a jar, a bundle of green onions. They spoke, their mouths moving soundlessly behind glass. Nothing remarkable. A life, neatly assembled from ordinary acts. A kettle filled. A plate set down. The man reached behind the woman to open a cupboard; she shifted without looking, making room for him.

Daniel watched them and felt the old ache of being outside a warm place. Not envy, exactly. He had no right to envy people for a kitchen and a shared loaf of bread. But the ache came anyway.

The woman glanced toward the window.

He moved back into the passage. His heart gave a silly, panicked thump. Surely she could not have seen him through the rain, the reflections, the curtain. Surely she had been looking at the street.

The siren sounded again.

Now there was water at the end of the passage, crawling over the pavement. It carried a twig, then a plastic cup. The flood had reached the lower streets. Daniel told himself to leave. His legs stayed where they were.

The woman stood alone in the bay window. The man was somewhere behind her, out of sight. She had taken off her scarf. Her dark hair lay close to her head, and in the lamplight her face seemed paler than it had on the street.

She was looking at the chair.

Daniel knew it before he followed her gaze. The discarded chair had been on Bell Street, a good distance uphill. It could not be here. It could not have followed them.

But something bumped against the outside wall below the window.

A dark shape rolled in the floodwater. It turned once, caught against the iron railings, and tipped sideways. One pale arm showed above the water.

The chair.

Daniel stared at it. The current had carried it downhill, along the same streets he’d walked, through the same gutters. It was absurdly natural. Of course the flood could carry a chair. Of course a chair could travel.

The woman lowered herself into the air.

No. Not into the air. There was a chair inside, just beyond the window, a plain wooden chair with a woven seat. She sat down on it, hands resting on its arms. The movement was so exact that Daniel felt he had seen it before: the slight tilt of her head, the shoulders easing, the fingers settling.

She sat like the burned chair.

Or the chair had been made to sit like her.

The pale arm of the outside chair bobbed up against the glass. Water pressed it there, gently, almost tenderly. Inside, the woman’s hand lay on the arm of her own chair. The two pale lines met, one in the room, one in the flood.

Daniel leaned closer.

The woman looked down at her hands. Her mouth moved. Perhaps she was speaking to the man in the kitchen. Perhaps she was counting. She had the stillness of someone listening to a fire in another room.

And then Daniel saw the burns.

Not on her skin. Her hands were unmarked. But the window’s reflection lay over her, and the dark water outside made a second figure around her shoulders. A blackened shape, ragged at the edges, with one bright arm. The chair and the woman occupied the same outline. For a moment the glass did not know which of them to show.

Daniel tried to step back. His heel struck the wall.

Inside, the man returned with two cups of tea. He set one on the table beside her. The woman did not move. He touched her shoulder.

At that, she turned and looked toward the passage.

This time she saw him. He knew it.

Her expression was not frightened or angry. It was not even surprised. It was the expression she had worn beside the discarded chair: the look of recognizing something that should not have been there, but was.

The flood rose over the bottom railings. The chair shifted, turned slowly, and pressed its charred back against the window. The glass shivered in its frame.

The man put down his cup. He said something to her. She did not look away from Daniel.

He could leave now. He could climb the hill, get out of the water, go home. He was not trapped. The passage had an open end. His legs would carry him if he asked them.

The woman lifted one hand and placed it flat against the inside of the glass.

Daniel’s own hand rose before he could decide. He held it there, a few inches from the wet brick, matching hers.

A second siren began, closer now. The water surged through the passage and struck his shins. Behind the window, the man seized the woman’s shoulder and pulled her upright. She let him. The black shape outside slipped away from the glass, turning in the current.

The chair drifted toward the street.

The woman followed it with her eyes. Then, at last, she looked back into the room.

Daniel lowered his hand.

The flood pulled at his coat. He took one step toward the open end of the passage, then another. The water tugged at his shoes, eager to take them. He climbed the hill without looking back.

At Bell Street, the chair was gone. Only a dark smear remained beside the wet leaves, and the rain was washing that away too.

Daniel stood for a moment, listening to the water move beneath the city. Then he went home by the long route, past the lit windows of other people’s kitchens, where no one was waiting for him and nothing had been burned.
