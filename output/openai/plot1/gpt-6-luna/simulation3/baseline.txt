### The Empty Place

By the time the rain had lasted three days, the river had begun to show itself in the streets.

It came first through the drains, lifting their iron covers with a hollow clank. Then it crept over the curb on Mercer Street, carrying leaves, cigarette packets, and the small, bright things people dropped without noticing. The radio in my rented room advised us to move to higher ground. I kept it on for the weather and went walking anyway.

I saw the couple just after four.

They were ordinary in the way people are ordinary when you don’t know them: a man in a brown coat, a woman in a dark green one, each carrying a paper bag of groceries. He was taller by half a head. She walked close beside him, but they didn’t touch. At each corner, they stopped together. When the light changed, they crossed together. They had the same pace, the same slight forward lean against the rain.

I would have forgotten them if they hadn’t stopped beside the burned chair.

It had been put out for collection near the alley behind the old bakery. An armchair, though most of its arms were gone. The upholstery was a faded green, patterned with small red flowers. One side had been blackened to the springs; the other looked merely old and damp. It sat crookedly on the pavement, as if it had tried to get up and failed.

The woman looked at it.

Not for long. The man said something, and she turned away. But for that brief moment her face had changed. I thought she seemed startled, or ashamed. Then they went on, turning down a narrow street that sloped toward the river.

I followed them.

I told myself I wanted to know where they lived. This was not a town I knew well, and the couple seemed to belong to it in a way the rest of the wet, hurried street did not. They moved as if they had all the time they needed. When the woman’s paper bag split at the bottom, the man caught a jar before it fell. She thanked him with a small smile, and he tucked the jar under his coat.

I felt, foolishly, that I had witnessed something private.

They stopped at a blue door in a brick house with narrow windows. The man unlocked it. The woman waited under the shallow porch roof, looking back up the street. I stepped behind a delivery van. She could not have seen me, but she gazed in my direction long enough that I felt the small, hot embarrassment of being caught.

Then she went inside.

I walked on to the river. The water was high and yellow-brown, sliding along the embankment with the heavy, purposeful look of something that had decided where it was going. A police car passed slowly, its lights turning on the rain. I went back to my room, put on a dry sweater, and listened to the radio say the lower streets should be evacuated.

At six, I returned to Mercer Street.

The rain had thinned to a mist. Water now covered the whole road, shallow at the top of the hill, deeper toward the river. The burned chair was gone from the bakery alley. I wondered if the collection truck had taken it, though no truck had been through. I found the blue-doored house and stood across from it beneath the bare branches of a street tree.

There was light in the front room.

Through the window I could see the man moving about. He carried bowls from the kitchen, set them on a table, took them away again. He seemed to be preparing for something that required the house to be orderly. The woman stood near the window, still wearing her green coat. She watched the rain.

The room was oddly bare. A table, two lamps, a narrow bookcase. On the rug, where an armchair might have been, four faint square marks showed in the pile, as if something heavy had stood there for years. The woman stood inside that pale square. Her shoulders sloped slightly to one side. Her left arm hung close to her body; her right elbow bent outward, not quite naturally.

The shape of the burned chair came into my mind.

I shifted beneath the tree. Water lapped at my shoes. The woman lowered her head, and the man came to stand behind her. He put his hands on her shoulders. She did not lean into him. They remained that way for a few seconds, a picture of concern, or comfort, or something I could not read through the glass.

Then the man left the room.

The woman stayed by the window.

More water slid down the street. It carried a child’s red ball and turned it gently against a parked car. Somewhere up the hill, a siren started and stopped. I knew I ought to leave. I could feel the cold climbing through my shoes. But I kept watching the woman, who seemed, in the wavering reflection of the flooded street, to be watching me too.

She took off her coat.

Underneath she wore a dark dress printed with small red flowers.

The same pattern as the chair’s upholstery.

I leaned closer to the glass. The woman’s hair was pinned at the back, and one loose strand lay along her cheek. She rubbed at the sleeve of her dress, as if trying to remove a spot. Then she looked down at the bare patch on the rug. For the first time, she smiled.

It was not a happy smile. It was the expression of someone recognizing an old mistake.

The man returned carrying a wooden stool. He set it in the middle of the pale square. The woman stared at it. He spoke to her, too low for me to hear. She answered with a shake of her head. He tried to take her hand, but she slipped away from him and went to the front door.

I moved behind the tree again.

The door opened. The woman stood on the porch, looking up the street toward the water. She was not wearing her coat. Her dress clung close to her narrow shoulders. From where I stood, the black window behind her made her look like a figure cut out of a photograph.

For an instant she lifted her right arm, elbow jutting outward, and held it there.

Then the door shut.

The flood reached the first step.

I could no longer pretend I was only passing by. I had followed the couple home and returned to watch them. I was cold, wet, and becoming afraid, but the fear seemed to belong to someone else. The woman’s strange, familiar posture held me in place. I remembered the chair’s broken arm, the way its seat had sagged toward the burned side.

A dark shape moved in the water at the end of the street.

It came slowly around the corner, turning with the current. The chair floated past the bakery, one leg caught in a strip of blue plastic. Its ruined upholstery had darkened in the rain. It drifted sideways, bumping gently against the curb, then swinging free.

It was heading down toward the blue-doored house.

The woman appeared at the window again. The man stood behind her. He was gripping her shoulders now, not gently. She pressed both palms to the glass.

The chair knocked against the front steps. It spun once, then lodged against the porch rail. Water pushed it against the house, lifting its broken legs. The woman’s face was pale behind the window. She looked down at the chair. Then she raised her right arm, elbow bent.

The chair’s remaining arm lifted on the current, a blackened stump pointing toward the window.

The man pulled her back. She resisted. He pulled harder. From the street I could see her mouth move, but I heard nothing over the rain. She didn’t look angry. She looked as if she had been asked to leave a place she had never truly vacated.

Water surged over the first step and pressed the chair flat against the window. It turned on its side. The pattern of red flowers floated just beneath the surface, wavering like something alive.

For a moment, the glass held two images: the woman inside, standing with one arm crooked; the burned chair outside, its broken shape tilted toward hers. The reflections met. Her dress became the chair’s upholstery. Her shoulder became its charred back. There seemed to be no clear line between them.

The man let go.

The woman sat down on the bare floor in the pale square. She bent one knee beneath her and leaned to the left. The movement was so exact that I felt a sharp, absurd certainty: she had been waiting for the chair to come home.

The lights went out.

The room vanished into darkness. I saw only the window reflecting the flooded street, and my own face in it, stretched and broken by the water.

Something touched my knee. I looked down and saw the water rising around it. Behind me, people were shouting for anyone still on Mercer Street to get out. I turned uphill.

At the corner, I looked back once.

A pale shape sat in the dark front room. It might have been the woman. It might have been the chair. I could not tell which side of the glass it was on.

Then the current pulled the burned armchair away from the porch, and the window went black.
