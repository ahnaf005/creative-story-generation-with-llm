### The Chair at High Water

By four o’clock, the rain had turned the gutters into narrow, brown rivers. Mara stood beneath the awning of the closed pharmacy, watching the street shine.

She had been watching the couple for nearly ten minutes.

There was nothing remarkable about them. The man wore a navy coat and carried a paper bag folded neatly at the top. The woman had a red scarf, a dark skirt, and the composed expression of someone waiting for a kettle to boil. They stood outside the bakery, speaking quietly. Every so often one of them glanced at the other, and the other seemed to know precisely when to look back.

That was all. Nothing at all.

Still, Mara could not turn away.

The woman tilted her head as the man said something. A small, private smile appeared, then vanished. He lifted the paper bag and she reached to steady its bottom. Their hands met for a moment. They did not look at one another. They didn’t need to.

Mara felt an odd, unpleasant pinch in her chest. How did people manage that? The unthinking tenderness. The ease of being expected.

She had been on her way home. It was only six blocks. There was no reason to stay beneath the pharmacy awning, growing damp at the edges, watching two strangers buy something for dinner.

The couple set off east.

So did Mara.

She kept half a block behind them, telling herself the walk was in the same direction. It wasn’t a lie, exactly. She did live east of here, though she might have taken the covered arcade instead. The couple walked slowly, stopping at a crossing to let a bus grind through the rain. The woman tucked her arm into the man’s. He lowered his head to hear her over the weather.

They had a way of moving together that made the pavement seem to tilt beneath them. Not hurried, not cautious. Certain. When they reached the corner of Bell Street, they turned down the narrow road that ran alongside the old canal.

Mara paused.

A gust shoved rain under her coat. Well. She knew Bell Street. It would bring her home too, eventually. She crossed after them.

Halfway down the block, she passed the chair.

It had been left beside a heap of soaked cardboard, one of those old dining chairs with a wooden back and curved arms. Or it had once been a dining chair. One front leg was missing. The seat had been burned black in the middle, its varnish blistered into pale, peeling scales. The backrest still had its shape, though the upper rail was charred through at one end, leaving a dark crescent against the wet gray wood.

Someone had dragged it to the curb and given up.

Mara glanced at it, then at the couple ahead. The woman had turned to say something. In the rain, her red scarf showed like a small flame.

The chair was behind her now. There was no reason to look back.

The couple stopped at a narrow red-brick house with a green front door. The man fumbled with his keys; the woman leaned against his shoulder while he found the right one. The door opened. Warm light spilled onto the wet step, and they disappeared inside.

Mara stood in the rain.

So that was that. They lived here. She had followed them all the way to their house. A fine thing to discover about herself.

The sky muttered above the rooftops. Water gurgled in the gutters, rose over the curb, and swirled around the wheels of parked cars. Mara should go. Her own flat was still dry. She was cold, and the people inside that house had no idea she existed.

She went closer anyway.

At the end of the street, a yellow flood sign had been set out, though the water was already past its base. The canal had overtopped its banks. Mara knew the district flooded when the rain came hard enough and long enough; she had lived here three years. She had watched water creep over the lower road before. It was usually an inconvenience, not a disaster. The council would pump it out by morning.

Still, the current had grown quicker than it should. It dragged leaves, plastic cups, and a small branch through the gutter. The branch struck the curb, spun, and shot away.

Mara moved beneath the shallow shelter of the house’s front bay. The curtains were open in the front room. There they were, the couple, close together in amber light. The man set the paper bag on a table. The woman stood with her back to the window, removing her scarf.

Mara should have felt ashamed. She did feel ashamed. And yet the shame sat alongside a sharp, bright curiosity, as if the two feelings could share one small room.

The woman’s hair was pinned up. Her neck looked pale and narrow. She turned toward the man, and he touched her cheek. Not a kiss. Just a brief touch, as if checking that she was there.

Then she went to the table and began unpacking the bag. He took out a loaf, a jar, a bundle of herbs. Their movements were unremarkable, even dull. They were making dinner. The man rinsed something at the sink. The woman opened a cupboard, reached for a plate, and came back to the table.

Mara watched the rain crawl down the glass.

At some point, the woman stopped moving.

She was looking at the window. Not out at Mara—surely not. Her eyes seemed fixed on something beyond it, something in the street. She held the plate against her hip.

Mara drew back.

Nothing. The woman turned away. A moment later she began setting the table, as ordinary as before.

The water reached the doorsteps just after six. It lapped over the paving stones and covered the bottoms of the green door. Mara had to shift her feet onto the low brick ledge beneath the bay window. The cold found its way through her shoes.

She could leave now. It was absurd to remain. The rain was worsening, the street was flooding, and if anyone looked out and saw her standing there, she would have to explain. What would she say? She had found them beautiful. She had followed them home.

The couple sat down to eat. The man took the chair nearest the window. The woman sat opposite him, her back to the street. They talked, though Mara could not hear through the rain and glass. He laughed at something. She smiled, and the smile was not the one from the bakery. This one was tired. Or sad. No—just the expression people wore when they were at home and no one was watching.

Except someone was.

The woman rose, carried her plate to the sink, and stood there with both hands braced on the counter. The man remained at the table. He watched her.

Mara noticed, then, how the woman held herself. Her shoulders were squared, but one sat a little higher than the other. Her head tipped faintly to the left. The posture seemed familiar. There was something in the angle of her arm, the careful curve of her back.

The burned chair, she thought.

The thought was ridiculous. The chair had been broken and abandoned in the rain, while the woman was warm inside her house. They had nothing to do with one another.

And yet the woman’s silhouette against the lit kitchen seemed to complete the chair’s shape: the raised shoulder like one curved arm, the tilted head like the broken rail of its back. Mara remembered the blackened seat, the empty place where its front leg should have been. She remembered how the chair seemed not so much thrown away as left waiting.

The woman turned.

For the first time, she looked straight at the bay window.

Mara’s breath caught. The glass reflected the room behind her, the gray street, the rain. The woman’s face was pale in the yellow light. Her eyes seemed to rest on the exact patch of shadow where Mara stood.

Then the man came up behind her and put a hand on her shoulder. The woman leaned into him. Her gaze softened. She turned away.

The floodwater reached the bay window’s lower panes.

Mara stepped onto the brick ledge, then onto the metal drainpipe bracket. The water was rising too fast now. It tugged at her shins and slapped the house with a wet, heavy sound. She could hear people calling somewhere up the street, a door banging, the thin shriek of a car alarm. It was time to go.

Something bumped against her leg.

Mara looked down.

The chair had come loose from wherever it had been caught. It drifted along the curb, turning slowly in the flood. Its missing leg dipped beneath the surface; its burned seat rose and fell like a dark raft. The current pushed it toward the house.

Mara climbed another inch up the bracket.

The chair caught against the iron gate, then swung free. It turned once, showing the blackened seat, and drifted to the front step. There it lodged, upright, wedged between the stone post and the green door. Its charred back leaned against the house. Its one remaining front leg stood on the threshold.

Inside, the woman stopped.

She had been walking toward the hall. Now she stood at the edge of the room, her body angled toward the front door. The man appeared behind her and said something. She did not move.

The chair knocked against the door.

The woman lifted one hand to her shoulder. She pressed her fingers into the place where the man had touched her.

Mara saw, then, a dark mark on the back of the woman’s neck: a crescent-shaped burn, no larger than a coin. The woman’s fingers covered it. Her hand stayed there.

The chair knocked again.

The man reached for her arm. She let him take it, but her eyes remained on the door. Mara could not see her face clearly now. The window was streaked with rain and the water was higher, climbing over the bottom of the glass.

There was no chair inside the room. Mara had noticed that already. No armchair by the fireplace, no dining chair beyond the one at the table. There was only the couple, standing close together in the yellow light, and the dark outline on the step.

The woman lowered her hand.

She leaned toward the glass.

Mara felt the cold water press against her waist. The current tugged at her coat, at the loose hem of her skirt. She looked over her shoulder. The street had become a moving brown surface with islands of rooftops and hoods. There was no clear way back.

The woman moved her mouth.

Mara could not hear her, but she thought she knew the words.

Come in.

No. Surely not. She was imagining it. People imagined all sorts of things when they were cold and afraid, watching strangers through a flooded window.

The green door opened a fraction.

The man held it from behind, keeping the current out. The chair shifted on the step. One of its arms pressed against the gap.

The woman stood in the doorway. She looked smaller there, and older, and somehow more familiar than she had before. The crescent mark on her neck was no longer hidden. It curved like the blackened rail of the chair.

Mara gripped the drainpipe. The floodwater rose to her ribs.

The woman raised her hand and touched the charred wood.

For an instant, the old chair seemed to settle beneath that touch. The broken back straightened. The missing leg no longer mattered. The woman bent her knees slightly, as if preparing to sit.

Mara shut her eyes.

When she opened them, the door was closed. The couple stood together beyond the glass. The chair had vanished beneath the water.

A current struck the house and spun Mara away from the bay window. She reached for the iron post, missed, then caught a drainpipe with both hands. The street moved around her, dark and cold. She had followed them to their home, and now there was nowhere else to go.

Through the glass, the woman watched her.

For a moment, Mara thought she saw a chair beside the table: one blackened arm, one curved back, a pale hand resting on the seat.

Then the lights went out.
