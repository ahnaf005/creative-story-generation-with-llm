### The Chair in the Water

The chair was already burning when I first saw it, though there was no flame.

It sat at the curb on Calder Street, among the wet newspapers and split bags of rubbish, waiting for the city collection. A wooden armchair, once upholstered in some greenish fabric. The seat had collapsed in the middle. Its back was blackened from the left side to the center, the wood blistered and dark as old bread. One arm remained pale. The other was charred almost to a claw.

I stopped to look at it because I had nowhere to be. That was how most things began for me then.

Rain had been falling since morning, soft and persistent, making the streetlights glow though it was only four in the afternoon. Calder Street ran downhill toward the river. The city had posted sandbags at the corner, and a few people had been talking about the flood. Nothing serious, they said. Nothing that hadn’t happened before.

I stood under the awning of a closed bakery and watched the rain gather in the chair’s torn seat.

Then the couple came along.

They were not remarkable. That was what drew me to them. The man wore a gray sweater with the sleeves pushed up. The woman had a yellow coat and a canvas shopping bag. They were maybe forty, maybe a little younger. He carried an umbrella, but held it mostly over her, so that one shoulder of his sweater had gone dark with rain. She was telling him something. He listened with his head inclined, smiling now and then as if the story had a part he already knew.

At the corner, the woman stopped. The man took two more steps before he noticed, then turned back. She had seen a small dog sheltering beneath a parked car. She crouched in her yellow coat, talking softly to it. The man stood beside her, holding the umbrella over all three of them.

There was nothing in the scene. A kindness, perhaps, but the ordinary kind, the kind people perform without expecting to be seen. I watched until the dog finally darted away. The woman rose, and the man brushed rain from her hood with the back of his hand.

Then they continued downhill.

I went after them.

I can’t give you a reason. I had been lonely for years, but loneliness is too simple an explanation for the strange focus that came over me. I wanted to know where they were going. I wanted to see what they did once they were inside their own home, with no one to witness them.

They walked slowly. At a pharmacy window, the woman leaned close to examine a display of cheap reading glasses. The man made a face at one pair. She laughed, and he touched her elbow. At a bakery, they went in and came out with a paper bag that steamed in the rain.

I stayed half a block behind.

They turned onto Bell Street, where the houses stood close together, their narrow fronts pressed between a laundromat and a boarded-up dentist’s office. The couple stopped at a blue door. The woman searched her bag. The man leaned on the umbrella and waited, looking down the street as if he had all the time in the world.

When she found her keys, she held them up like a prize.

He said something I couldn’t hear. She shook her head. He kissed her, briefly, on the forehead.

Then they went in.

I passed the house without stopping and continued to the end of the block. There was a bus shelter there, glass-sided and fogged with rain. I sat on the bench and watched the blue door from across the street.

I told myself I was waiting for the rain to ease. It didn’t. I told myself the couple might come back out. They didn’t.

By six, the water had reached the gutters. It ran fast along the curb, carrying cigarette ends and maple seeds. I watched people moving their cars to higher streets. A man in a red cap wrestled with a sandbag in the doorway of the laundromat. Somewhere, a siren sounded and stopped.

I should have gone home.

Instead, I crossed Bell Street and stood beneath the narrow awning of the house next door to the blue door. From there I could see through the couple’s front window. A curtain hung across most of it, but a gap near the edge showed the yellow-lit room beyond.

The woman came into view carrying two cups. She set one down, then folded the shopping bag and placed it in a drawer. The man appeared behind her and took her coat. She stood with her back to him while he eased it from her shoulders, an old, unthinking movement. Then he hung it on a hook.

They were home. That was all.

Still, I watched.

They ate at a small table by the window. The man spoke more than the woman. She listened with her chin in one hand. Sometimes she reached across the table to touch his fingers, as if checking he was still there. Afterward, they cleared the plates together. He washed. She dried.

The water rose to the bottom of the car tires.

A little before eight, the lights in their front room went out. The couple moved farther back into the house, beyond the gap in the curtain. I could have left then. But the water was now over the curb, and the streets had become black mirrors. The city’s emergency siren began again, louder this time. A neighbor shouted from an upstairs window that people should get out while they could.

I heard a door open next door. Someone hurried away through the water.

I stayed beneath the awning, my shoes filling.

It was then that I remembered the chair.

The discarded, burned chair was still at the curb on Calder Street, downhill from here. I pictured its broken seat collecting rain, its blackened back leaning toward the river. Something about it had bothered me from the first. Not the sight of damage, exactly. I had seen fire damage before. It was the way the chair seemed to be waiting for someone to come back and sit in it.

A light appeared in the couple’s front room.

The woman crossed the gap in the curtains. She was no longer wearing the yellow coat. She wore a pale dress, or perhaps a long shirt, and her hair hung loose around her face. She paused beside the window, looking out at the water.

Then she did something I couldn’t understand. She folded her arms close to her body and leaned slightly to the left. Her head dipped. Her knees bent a little, as though she were lowering herself into a seat that wasn’t there.

The posture was familiar.

I saw the chair again: the unburned arm, the collapsed seat, the blackened back. I saw the shape its charred wood had made against the wall of rain. The chair had leaned to the left, its pale arm raised as if to receive someone. Its scorched back had curved around an empty space.

The woman stood upright and disappeared from view.

I stepped off the sidewalk. The water reached my ankles, cold enough to sting. I went toward the corner, keeping one hand on the brick walls. I wanted to see the chair. I needed to know if it was still there.

At the end of Bell Street, the current pushed hard against my legs. The water had covered the curb and reached the steps of the houses. A plastic crate bumped against my shin and floated on. The rain blurred the streetlights into pale halos.

The chair was gone.

I looked up and down Calder Street. Garbage swirled in the flood. A traffic cone turned slowly in the middle of the road. There was no chair.

For a moment, I thought the water had carried it away.

Then something struck my calf.

I looked down. The chair drifted alongside me, its legs dragging beneath the surface. It turned as the current shifted, the burned back rotating toward me. One pale wooden arm lifted from the water.

I backed away. The chair bumped the curb and lodged there, below the couple’s house, turning gently in place.

I looked toward their window.

The woman was standing behind the curtain. The room was lit again. She had one hand pressed to the glass, and the other hung straight down at her side. She was looking not at me, but at the chair.

Behind her, the man crossed the room carrying a lamp. He stopped when he saw her at the window. He put the lamp down and came close. His arm went around her waist.

She didn’t move.

The chair rocked against the curb. Its burned side faced the house now. For one impossible second, I saw in its blackened back the outline of a woman’s shoulder, the slope of a neck, the place where a head might rest. The shape matched the woman behind the glass. Not perfectly. Not enough to trust. But enough that I could not look away.

She lifted her free hand and placed it beside the first.

The man said something to her. He reached for the curtain, but she caught his wrist. She turned her head toward him, and even through the glass I could see the expression on her face. It was not fear. It was recognition.

The flood pressed the chair against the house. Its pale arm tapped the brick, once, twice.

The woman lowered herself again, slowly, into the empty air.

Her husband’s arm tightened around her. The light behind them flickered. For an instant, the room went dark, and the window reflected only the street: the rain, the flood, and me standing in the water.

When the light came back, the woman was gone.

The man remained at the window, looking out. He searched the street, then the water, then the place where I stood. I couldn’t tell whether he saw me. His face held no surprise. He looked tired, the way someone looks after carrying a heavy thing upstairs.

Then he drew the curtain.

The chair was still outside, lodged against the wall. The flood climbed its legs and darkened its pale arm. I watched the water rise over the seat. It covered the blackened back last.

I stayed until I could no longer see the chair at all. Then I turned toward the high street and began to walk.

Behind me, in the house, a lamp was still burning.
