### The Chair at the Curb

By the time Mara noticed the couple, the rain had been falling for three hours and the gutters were carrying leaves like little boats.

They were walking beneath a single umbrella, though there was room under it for two. The man held it tilted toward the woman. His right shoulder was dark with rain. She walked close to him, not touching, her hands folded in the pockets of her coat. They were ordinary people. That was what caught Mara’s attention: how thoroughly ordinary they seemed, and how, for a moment, the street appeared to have been arranged around them.

They stopped at the window of a bakery already closed for the night. The man said something. The woman looked at the pastries in the dark, then at him. She smiled, briefly, as if she had remembered the proper expression and put it on.

Mara slowed.

There was nothing to see. A couple out in the rain. A man with a damp shoulder. A woman who smiled a fraction too late. Still, Mara watched them move on, and when they turned down Bell Street she turned too.

She could go home. Her building was only four blocks away. She had bought milk and bread. The rain was getting into the seams of her shoes. Quite enough observing for one evening.

But the couple kept appearing just ahead of her, as if the street were drawing them forward and her after them. They crossed beneath the railway bridge. They paused at a narrow shop window. They passed the empty lot where someone had left a chair at the curb.

The chair was old, wooden, and upholstered in a faded blue fabric. It had been burned on one side. The flame had eaten the cloth from the arm and blackened the frame beneath, leaving the rest of it oddly intact. It sat angled toward the street, as if it had been placed there to watch the traffic.

Mara stopped beside it.

The rain tapped the scorched seat. The chair’s left arm was burned nearly through, but the right one still wore a strip of blue velvet. Such a thing could have been lovely once. Or not. Chairs weren’t lovely, really. They were useful, until they weren’t.

When she looked up, the couple were already at the next corner.

She hurried after them.

At the end of Bell Street they turned into a row of narrow houses with shared brick steps and ground-floor windows. The man opened the door of a blue-painted house. The woman went in ahead of him. He glanced back toward the street, not quite looking at Mara, and shut the door.

There. They lived here. A normal house with a brass knocker, a blue door, a little iron railing. Nothing to it. Mara should have been satisfied.

Instead she stood beneath a dripping plane tree and watched the front windows.

For a while nothing moved behind the curtains. The rain ran down the glass in bright, trembling threads. Then a light came on in the front room. A shadow crossed it; another followed. The woman’s silhouette was narrow and upright. The man’s was broader, bent slightly at the neck. They moved in and out of view, performing the small private tasks of evening.

Mara began to feel foolish. It wasn’t a crime to walk down the same street. She could keep walking. She could go home and make tea and forget them. People were allowed to be strangers. Their lives did not have to contain a secret just because she wanted one.

The front-room curtain shifted.

The woman was standing behind it. Even through the rain and the glass, Mara could see the pale oval of her face. She seemed to be looking out. At the street, at the wet pavement, at nothing in particular. Her features were indistinct, but her posture was familiar: the shoulders slightly forward, the head tipped toward one side.

Like the chair, Mara thought.

No. Not like the chair. A person didn’t look like a chair. She was tired, that was all.

The woman stepped away from the window.

Mara went home.

She woke to the phone vibrating on the bedside table. For a moment she thought it was still evening. The room had the same gray light, the same rain at the window, only deeper and colder. The phone buzzed again. A flood warning, the message said. Avoid low-lying roads. Do not enter floodwater. The river had breached its banks in the night.

Mara sat up. Her shoes, left in the hall, were wet through. From the kitchen window she could see the street shining under a sheet of brown water. A garbage bin drifted past, turning slowly. Somewhere, a car alarm called out in a thin, helpless voice.

She thought of the couple’s house.

The thought came too quickly, too easily. They were probably safe. Their street sat lower than hers; she’d noticed that when she followed them. A pointless fact, unless it wasn’t. She dressed in the clothes from yesterday and took her coat.

Outside, the water had climbed over the curb. It was cold around her ankles, clouded with grit and bits of leaf. The neighborhood had become quiet in the way a room became quiet when someone had just left it. No traffic. No buses. Only the rain and the soft push of water along the streets.

Mara went down Bell Street.

The row houses appeared through the rain, their lower windows dark. Water lapped against the blue door and swirled around the iron railing. A plastic bottle turned in circles at the foot of the steps. She had to brace a hand against a wall as the current pressed at her knees.

The front room was lit.

She moved nearer, gripping a lamppost. The water was cold enough to make her calves ache. The window was uncurtained now, or the curtain had been drawn back. Inside, the man stood with his trousers rolled up, lifting books from a low shelf. He placed them on a table. A lamp glowed beside him. The room looked warm, dry, and very far away.

The woman sat in a wooden chair near the window.

Mara could see the chair clearly. It had a high back and rounded arms, with blue upholstery. The woman’s hands rested on the arms. She wasn’t looking at the man or at the water. Her face was turned slightly toward the glass.

The burned chair. For one wild second Mara thought the discarded one had followed them home. But no. This chair was whole. Its velvet was dark in the warm light. The woman sat as if she were holding it down.

The man came to her and put a hand on her shoulder. He said something. She did not move. He leaned close, his mouth near her ear. Her head tilted, slowly, toward him.

There, again. That posture. The tipped head, the stillness, the hands set neatly on the arms.

Like the chair at the curb. Like the woman in the chair. The difference between them thinning until Mara could no longer find it.

She shifted her weight. Water slid past her, tugging at her coat. The man had gone back to the shelves. The woman continued to sit beside the window, looking toward the street. Perhaps she saw Mara now. Perhaps she had seen her all along.

Mara remembered the burned strip of velvet on the discarded chair, the charred arm and the untouched one. She imagined the fire stopping where it had, not by accident but because it had been told to stop. She imagined a woman once sitting in that chair, her hands on the arms, her head tilted as she listened. Then smoke in the room, the man at the door, the chair left outside in the rain.

The woman in the window raised one hand and touched the place where the chair’s arm would have been.

Mara’s fingers tightened around the lamppost. The floodwater pressed against her hips now. She needed to get out of the street. Everyone needed to get out of the street. But she could not leave until the woman looked away, until the little movement was undone.

Inside, the man lifted a box onto the table. The woman stood.

She was taller than Mara had expected. Or perhaps she had been sitting so still that standing seemed to make more of her. She crossed the room and vanished from the window. A moment later, the front door opened.

The man stood on the step, water swirling around his ankles. He looked directly at Mara.

For a second neither of them moved. Then he said, “Are you all right?”

A sensible question. A kind one. Mara nodded, though the water was rising and she could feel its current pushing at her knees.

“We’re going upstairs,” he said. “You should, too.”

His voice was ordinary. His face, in the doorway, was ordinary. He looked tired and worried, as anyone would look with floodwater entering his home. This was all there was. A man trying to save his books. A woman trying to save her chair. A stranger in the rain who had followed them for no reason she could explain.

The woman appeared behind him. She stood in the doorway, one hand on the frame. Her gaze passed over Mara’s face and settled somewhere beyond her, out on the street.

For an instant Mara saw the chair at the curb: its blue cloth darkened by rain, its burned arm angled toward the road. Waiting.

The woman’s mouth moved. Perhaps she said something. Perhaps it was only the rain against the glass.

Then the man reached for her hand, and she let him lead her back inside. The door closed. The light in the front room went out.

Mara turned toward higher ground. The water tugged at her coat, patient as a hand. On the corner, something blue floated past, turning end over end. It might have been a cushion. It might have been a piece of the chair.

She watched it until the current carried it out of sight.

Then, at last, she went home.
