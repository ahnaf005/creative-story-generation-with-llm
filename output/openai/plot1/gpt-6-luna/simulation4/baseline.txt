### The Chair in the Water

At first, there was nothing remarkable about the couple.

I saw them on Harrow Street, where the pavement sloped toward the river and rainwater ran in silver threads along the gutters. They were walking slowly, not because they were old or tired, but because they had fallen into the particular pace of people who had nowhere urgent to be. The man carried a paper bag beneath his coat. The woman held a folded umbrella by its crooked handle. They wore no wedding rings that I could see.

They were ordinary enough that I ought to have forgotten them at once.

Instead, I stopped beneath the awning of a closed bakery and watched them pass.

The woman said something. The man laughed, though I couldn’t hear the words, and tilted the bag toward her as if offering a secret. She leaned over it. For a moment their heads touched, and the rain shone on their hair. Then they separated and went on, shoulder to shoulder, in a silence that seemed to belong only to them.

I had been alone for three days.

That is not a defense. It is merely a fact.

The river had risen all week. It pressed against its walls, brown and swollen, carrying branches, bottles, and once a child’s red mitten. The radio said the flood barriers would hold. The radio also said the storm would pass by morning. I had lived beside the river long enough to know that such assurances were made by people who slept uphill.

I watched the couple cross the street. The woman stepped around a puddle without looking down. The man stepped into it. He did not seem to notice. She reached back and touched his sleeve, and he followed her without turning his head.

Something in that small gesture caught me. The ease of it. The trust.

I left the bakery awning and followed.

They turned down Larch Lane, past a shuttered pharmacy and a row of brick houses with their basement windows boarded over. At the corner, the woman looked briefly over her shoulder. Her face came clear beneath a streetlamp: pale, narrow, with a small crease between her brows, as if she were listening to a sound too faint for anyone else.

She looked straight at me.

Then she turned away.

I could have gone home. Instead, I kept walking.

The couple stopped at a house near the end of the lane, a narrow white place with a green door. The man fumbled with his keys while the woman stood under the shallow porch roof. When the door opened, warm light spilled across the wet steps. They went inside, and the door shut behind them.

I stood across the street, listening.

For a while there was nothing. Then, through the front window, I saw the man cross the room carrying two cups. The woman appeared behind him and took one. They sat on a sofa, close but not touching, facing the dark window as if they were watching the street.

I moved along the pavement until I stood beneath a plane tree. I told myself I was sheltering from the rain.

That was when I remembered the chair.

It had been lying behind the bus shelter on Fennel Street, where the city collected things no one wanted. I’d noticed it on my way out that afternoon: a wooden dining chair with its back twisted sideways and its seat blackened by fire. The legs were pale where the varnish had peeled, but the curved crest was charred nearly through. It looked less thrown away than left behind after some private disaster.

A strip of red fabric clung to one arm.

I had looked at it longer than I should have. Then the rain began, and I walked on.

Now, under the tree, I pictured the chair’s curved back. Its dark, uneven shape. The way one leg had bent inward, as if someone had sat in it for years and slowly leaned to one side.

The woman in the house sat with one shoulder lower than the other.

I noticed this only because the rain blurred the window and made every movement seem deliberate. She turned her cup in her hands. The man spoke. She didn’t answer. He reached for her wrist, and she let him hold it.

The resemblance was absurd. A chair was a chair. A woman was a woman. Yet the two shapes seemed to belong to the same sentence, the same dark thought.

A gust of wind shoved rain down the street. The river, hidden beyond the buildings, gave a low, distant roar.

I went home.

My room was on the ground floor of a boardinghouse three blocks away. I packed a small bag and put it on the table. I checked the radio, then the door, then the radio again. The announcer’s voice crackled through reports of water spilling over the eastern embankment. Residents in low-lying streets were advised to move to higher ground.

I sat on the bed and waited for morning.

At midnight, I got up and left.

The water had reached Harrow Street. It covered the gutters and spread across the road in a thin, wavering sheet. At the next corner it was ankle-deep, flowing toward the river with the patience of something that knew it would get there eventually. A shopping cart drifted past me, its wheels turning slowly beneath the surface.

The couple’s house still glowed.

I waded toward it.

The water was cold enough to hurt. It pressed at my trousers and tugged when I lifted my legs. Halfway down Larch Lane, a branch struck my shin and spun away. I caught myself against a lamppost. The flood had risen past the bottom step of the house. The green door was shut. Light trembled in the windows.

I stood across the street, submerged to my knees.

Through the front room, I saw the man carrying books upstairs. He made several trips, his arms full, his movements quick now. The woman stood beside the sofa, watching him. She seemed not to hear the water at the threshold.

When he came back down, he took her by the shoulders. They spoke sharply. She shook her head. He pointed toward the stairs. She pulled away from him, moved to the window, and looked out.

At me.

I held my breath.

Her expression did not change. She did not raise a hand or call for help. She simply looked, and I felt, with the foolish certainty of dreams, that she recognized me. Not my face. Something behind it. Something I had brought with me from Fennel Street.

Then she turned away from the glass.

The man was gone upstairs again.

The water reached the doorstep. It lapped at the green door, withdrew, and came back higher. I heard the soft knock of something floating against the house. The woman lowered herself onto the sofa.

She sat with her back straight and her hands folded in her lap.

The old chair appeared in my mind again, not as it had lain by the bus shelter but upright, restored, its blackened frame standing in some room I could not see. In that image the woman sat in it. Her lowered shoulder matched the inward bend of the leg. The red cloth on the arm became the red fabric I had seen at her wrist—or perhaps it was only the reflected lamp, staining the glass.

I blinked.

The sofa was empty.

The woman had gone out of sight, perhaps into the hall. The man descended the stairs with a blanket. He hurried to the front window and pulled the curtains. The room vanished.

I should have left then. The water was rising fast, and the current had begun to move small things down the lane: leaves, a plastic bottle, a single child’s shoe. Somewhere behind the houses, an alarm started and stopped.

But I stayed.

A shape drifted from the far end of the street. It turned once in the current, struck a parked car, and rolled free.

A chair.

For a moment I thought it was the discarded one from Fennel Street. The same curved back, the same pale legs. But this one was intact. It came toward me, bumping gently along the flooded road. A strip of red cloth streamed from its arm.

I stepped sideways. The chair passed, turning as it went, its seat dipping beneath the water and rising again. Behind the curtained window, a shadow moved.

The chair floated past the house and vanished into the dark.

I looked at the curtains. The water had reached the bottom of the window, covering the sill. A pale shape appeared behind the cloth, close to the glass. It might have been the woman. It might have been the reflection of a streetlamp trembling in the flood.

I leaned forward.

The shadow lifted one arm.

I lifted mine without deciding to.

Then the current caught my legs. I stumbled, struck the curb, and went down hard on one knee. Water closed over my hands. When I looked up, the house was dark. The curtains hung still. The street behind me had become a single black surface, reflecting no lights at all.

I found my way to higher ground by following the line of telephone poles. At dawn, the rain stopped. The river began, slowly, to draw back.

They never found a chair in the house on Larch Lane. They found books swollen with water, a sofa tipped against the wall, and two cups floating in the kitchen sink. They found the couple on the second floor, safe and shivering beneath a blanket.

By then I had gone home.

For weeks afterward, I tried to remember what the woman had looked like. I could recall her face, but not clearly. I remembered the crease between her brows, the uneven set of her shoulders, the way she had watched me through the rain.

Mostly, I remembered the chair.

It had turned up in the river mud, some distance downstream, with its legs intact and its back burned black. A strip of red fabric clung to one arm. The newspaper photograph showed it upright in the reeds, as if waiting for someone to sit.

I cut out the photograph and put it beside my bed.

Sometimes, when the house is quiet, I wake with the feeling that someone is standing outside my window, watching me. I don’t open the curtains. I know better than to look.

I know that if I do, I will see the woman.

And behind her, in the rain, the chair.
