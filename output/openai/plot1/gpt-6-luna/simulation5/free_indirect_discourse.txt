### The Chair at the Curb

The chair had been on the pavement since Tuesday, though by Friday the rain had softened its cardboard sign to a grey rag.

FREE, it had said. The chair itself was a low, old-fashioned thing with a wooden frame and green upholstery. Someone had burned a hole through the seat. The left arm was blackened, the varnish blistered into little scales. Still, it looked comfortable, if you didn’t mind the smell.

Elias minded the smell. He had passed it on his way to the grocer’s and smelled it again on his way back: wet ash, faintly sweet. By the third day, he had begun to look for it.

On Friday, he was standing beneath the awning of the closed bakery, waiting for the rain to slacken, when the couple came down the street.

There was nothing remarkable about them. The man wore a navy coat and carried a paper bag. The woman had a red scarf tucked into her collar and a damp strand of hair stuck to her cheek. They walked close enough to share the awning of a shopfront, but not so close that anyone would think they were making a point of it. A couple, then. Long married, perhaps. Or newly enough together to have learned each other’s pace.

The man said something. The woman smiled, and he looked pleased with himself.

Elias watched them pass. He had no reason to keep watching. They were only people out in the rain, going somewhere together. The man’s shoes were scuffed at the toes. The woman’s scarf had a little pull in the fringe. She reached over and brushed a raindrop from his sleeve.

Such a small gesture. It settled in Elias like a stone.

They turned at the corner, and he followed.

Not followed. He was going that way too. The street ran toward the river, and he lived near the river; anyone could have been going that way. The rain had eased to a mist, anyway. No reason to hurry home. The couple were easy to keep in sight: the woman’s red scarf, the man’s broad shoulders, the paper bag swinging from his hand.

They stopped at a crossing. The woman looked back, not at Elias, but at a bus pulling up behind them. Still, he felt caught. How foolish. People looked back all the time.

When the light changed, she touched the man’s elbow to guide him across. They continued down the narrow streets past rows of brick houses, past a boarded-up pub, past the spot where the burned chair sat on the pavement.

The woman slowed.

She looked at the chair. The man did too, glancing at the blackened seat, then at her. He said something Elias couldn’t hear. She shook her head and moved on.

That was all. A look, a shake of the head. Still, Elias stood by the curb a moment after they’d gone. The chair’s ruined arm pointed toward the direction they had taken.

A neighbour’s dog barked behind a gate. The chair’s wet fabric sagged in the seat, as though someone had just got up.

He went on.

Their house stood near the river, in a street of close-set terraces with shallow front steps. It was one of those houses that appeared to have been painted a different colour every few years: the door was blue, the frame around it yellow, the upstairs windows white. The man took a key from his pocket. The woman leaned against the door while he fumbled with it, patient and amused.

Ordinary people, Elias told himself. There was nothing in them worth following.

Then the door opened, and the woman glanced once over her shoulder. For an instant her eyes seemed to rest on him. Not a stare, exactly. A recognition without surprise.

He stopped across the street beside a lamppost. The door shut behind her.

There. He should go.

Instead, he walked to the end of the block and turned into the alley behind the house. He told himself he wanted to see whether the street flooded as badly as people said. The river had been rising for days. Sandbags lay in doorways along the lower streets, and the local radio had begun repeating the word evacuation in a careful, unalarming voice.

From the alley he could see the couple’s kitchen window. Light showed through its yellow curtains. A shadow crossed it, then another. The man came into view carrying the paper bag; the woman leaned over the counter and took something from him. They moved around one another with the ease of people who had done this a thousand times. Put the kettle on. Set the bread out. Take the wet coat upstairs.

Elias should have found it comforting. Instead he felt an ache he couldn’t name. It was the kind of kitchen his mother had kept, always warm, always smelling of toast, right up until she went away to the hospital and didn’t come back.

How long had he been standing in the alley?

The rain began again, harder now. He went home at last, telling himself that was enough.

By midnight the river had climbed over the towpath. The local radio advised people in the lowest streets to move upstairs. Elias listened from his room, where the windows rattled in their frames. He could have stayed there, dry and safe. His own street was higher. No one had asked him to help.

But he thought of the house with the blue door. He thought of the woman glancing back.

At one in the morning he put on his boots and went out.

The streets were slick and shining. Water already covered the gutters, carrying cigarette packets and small leaves toward the river. Elias walked with his hands in his pockets. It wasn’t curiosity, not exactly. He wanted to make sure they were safe. A reasonable thing to want, if one was nearby.

At the corner, water lapped over the curb. It came up cold around his ankles as he crossed. Farther down, a parked car sat in the flood to its wheel hubs. The couple’s street sloped toward the river, and the water there had reached the front steps.

The blue door stood shut. No light showed at the ground-floor windows.

Maybe they had gone upstairs. Of course they had. They weren’t fools. He should leave.

A pale rectangle glowed above the front door. The curtains were drawn, but the thin line of light beneath them made the upstairs room seem awake. Elias moved closer. The water rose around his boots.

There she was.

The woman stood in the upstairs window, her red scarf gone, her hair loose over one shoulder. Behind her, the man was dragging something across the floor. A mattress, perhaps. Preparing to sleep upstairs. Sensible. She watched him, her head tilted slightly, the way people watched a familiar task.

Elias could not see her face clearly through the glass. Yet he knew she was not looking at the man. She was looking down at the street.

At him.

No. The glass was dark. She could be looking anywhere. The man came up beside her, said something, and touched her shoulder. She leaned toward him. They stood together at the window, a pair of figures in a bright square.

Water struck Elias’s thighs as something bumped his leg.

The chair.

It had broken free from the curb and floated down the street, turning slowly in the current. Its green fabric was almost black now. The burned hole yawned in its seat. As it spun, its ruined arm lifted and dipped, like a hand asking for help.

Elias reached for it without thinking. His fingers brushed the wet wood, and the chair pulled away. It drifted to the house and bumped against the front steps, caught there by the rush of water.

Above him, the woman moved away from the window.

He watched the lit room. The man seemed to be speaking, his hands moving as he pointed toward the back of the house. Then the woman sat down.

Elias frowned. Where had she found a chair? The room behind her was bare, or seemed bare, except for a dark shape near the wall. She settled into it sideways, her left shoulder raised, one arm resting where an armrest should have been. The posture was so familiar that Elias forgot to breathe.

He saw the green upholstery. The scorched hollow in the seat. The single blackened arm, the other one missing.

It was only a trick of distance. Only shadow and rain and the dark glass. The chair was below him, still caught against the steps. He could see it, couldn’t he? It was right there.

He looked up again.

The woman had not moved. Her head rested against the wall, and her face was turned toward the window. One hand hung beside her thigh, fingers loose. A dead hand, Elias thought, then hated himself for thinking it. She was alive. She was in her home, with her husband, safe upstairs.

Safe. Unless the water climbed higher.

The man vanished from the window. A moment later, the woman stood. For a breath she appeared to have no left arm at all. Then she lifted it and laid her palm flat against the glass.

The flood pushed the chair hard against the steps. It scraped along the wood with a sound Elias could feel in his teeth.

She had looked at it on the street. She had known it. That small pause, the shake of her head. She had left it there, or someone had left it for her. Perhaps she had once sat in it every night. Perhaps the burned place had come from a dropped cigarette, or from a lamp knocked over in some long-ago accident.

Perhaps the woman had burned with it.

The thought did not seem frightening. It seemed inevitable, like the river’s rise. He looked from the chair to the window and found that the woman was no longer behind the glass.

The man returned alone. He looked down at the street, then turned away.

Elias stood waist-deep now. The current pressed against his coat and tugged at his legs. He could go. He could wade back to the higher street and leave the couple to their own life. He had done enough. More than enough.

The chair came free of the steps and drifted toward him.

He put both hands on its wooden frame. The current turned it. For a moment he saw himself reflected in the dark window: a man in a soaked coat, clutching a burned chair in the middle of a flooded street. A ridiculous sight. A lonely one.

Behind his reflection, the woman sat in the chair.

Elias let go.

The current carried it on, away from the house. As it passed beneath the streetlamp, the scorched seat showed red—not glowing, exactly, but the deep red of a coal hidden under ash.

Upstairs, the light went out.

He listened for a sound from within the house, a cry, a door opening, the woman calling after her husband. Nothing came. The flood kept moving, steady as a thought that had finally reached its conclusion.

The chair turned once more in the water, its empty seat facing him.

It was only a chair. Someone had burned it and thrown it away. The woman was only a woman, safe in her home with the man she loved.

He could go home now.

He began to wade back, and the chair followed him.
