### The Chair in the Water

The chair was already at the curb when I first noticed it.

It stood beneath the dead streetlamp on Bellweather Avenue, a dining chair with a high, curved back and a seat gone black with fire. One of its legs had burned shorter than the others, so it leaned toward the gutter as if listening there. Rain had polished the charred wood until it shone.

I passed it on my way to the grocer and thought, as one does, that someone would take it away.

On the way back, the couple were standing beside it.

They were not touching. The man held a paper bag against his chest, and the woman was fastening the top button of his coat. She was small and pale, with dark hair tucked behind her ears. The man watched her with a look of such ordinary patience that I found myself slowing down.

“Too tight?” she asked.

“No,” he said. “Just right.”

She gave the button a little tug anyway.

They went on. They didn’t look at the chair. I did.

Then I followed them.

I had never followed anyone before. At the next corner I could have turned toward my apartment, where the radiator hissed and the kettle needed descaling. Instead, I kept the couple in sight as they walked beneath the wet plane trees. They moved at the same pace, but not quite in step. Sometimes she leaned closer to him; sometimes she drifted toward the curb. He never appeared to notice. When she fell behind, he stopped at shop windows and pretended to examine the displays until she caught up.

They were not young, though neither looked old. The man had a little gray at his temples. The woman’s face seemed unfinished in the dim light, as if the rain had softened its edges. I couldn’t say what had caught me. It was not beauty. It was the way they occupied the street together, as if they had been walking through it for a very long time and had only just arrived.

At the end of Bellweather they turned onto St. Oran’s, where the pavement sloped down toward the river. I stayed across the road. They stopped beneath an awning while the man unfolded a newspaper and shook it open. The woman stood beside him, looking at the rain.

I could see the river from there, thick and brown under the bridge. It had been rising for two days, and the city had begun to move its cars to higher ground. Sandbags sat in lopsided piles outside the bank. A siren sounded upriver, then went silent.

The couple stepped out from beneath the awning and kept walking.

Their house was on a narrow street of brick terraces, one of whose ends met the river wall. A blue door, a brass knocker shaped like a hand. The man let them in. Before closing the door, the woman turned back to look down the street.

I pressed myself into the shadow of a doorway. She looked in my direction, but I didn’t think she saw me. Her gaze moved past, over the wet bricks and the shuttered shopfronts, then settled somewhere beyond me. For a moment she seemed to be waiting for someone.

The door closed.

I went home.

That night the water reached the bottom step of my building. I slept badly, listening to the rain and the distant clanging of the flood barriers. In the morning, the radio advised everyone below the old railway line to leave. I made coffee, put on my coat, and walked back toward the river.

The chair was still at the curb.

The water had reached its feet. It curled around the short leg and lifted a film of ash from the grain. I stood under the streetlamp, though it wasn’t lit, and watched the chair rock once in the current. It had not been carried away.

I thought of the woman’s pale face in the doorway. Then I went to find the house.

By noon, the street had become a canal. The water lapped at the garden walls and carried a crate, a traffic cone, and one white shoe toward the river. I stood on the steps of a closed bakery, across from the blue door, with water halfway up my shins. Behind me, the city was emptying itself uphill. Ahead, every window on the street was dark.

Every window except theirs.

The man had lit a lamp in the front room. Through the wavering glass I could see him moving from one side of the room to the other, carrying things. Books, perhaps. A folded blanket. He stacked them on a table and went upstairs. The woman remained by the window.

She was facing out.

The water made her look as though she were standing at the bottom of a lake. I couldn’t see her clearly, but I could make out the slim line of her shoulders and the dark oval of her head. She did not move when a branch bumped against the door. She did not turn when a bottle rolled past in the current.

I raised one hand. I don’t know why. Perhaps I thought she might recognize me.

She stepped away from the window.

The room went dark, then bright again as lightning flickered over the river. In its brief white glare, I saw the chair.

It stood in the middle of the room, between the woman and the window. Its curved back, its scorched seat, its shortened leg. The same chair I had seen at the curb. I knew it was the same, although I couldn’t have explained how it had got inside. Perhaps the water had carried it there. Perhaps the couple had brought it in before the flood. Perhaps I had mistaken some other chair for it.

The woman sat down.

The man came downstairs. He stood beside her, one hand resting on her shoulder, and leaned to say something. I saw his mouth move. She looked up at him, and he touched the side of her face.

Then he lowered himself onto the floor in front of her and began to unfasten her shoes.

The gesture was so tender that it made me ashamed to watch. I looked away, toward the opposite house, where a curtain flapped in an open upstairs window. When I looked back, the man had gone out of sight. The woman remained on the chair.

The water rose another inch.

All afternoon I watched the house. Sometimes the woman stood. Sometimes she sat. Once she placed both hands on the window glass, and I thought of the way she had fastened the man’s coat button, giving it that little tug. She held her palms there, flat and pale, as if she were trying to feel something on the other side.

The chair leaned toward her. That was impossible, but from where I stood, it did. Its burned back curved around her shoulders. Its one short leg made it list, and whenever the floor shifted under the flood, it seemed to bow closer.

At dusk the man appeared again. He was carrying a kettle. He set it on the table, then stood behind the chair with his hands at his sides. The woman looked down at them. He touched the burnt wood with one finger.

For the first time, I wondered whether the chair had been burned in a fire. The thought seemed foolish. Of course it had. But I wondered whether the woman had been there when it happened. Whether the blackness on the seat was only smoke, or something else. Whether that was why she had stopped beside it on the street, without looking at it.

The man knelt and gripped the chair’s shortened leg. The woman put one hand on his shoulder. They stayed that way while the water pressed against the house.

Night came early under the storm. My legs had gone numb. I should have climbed to higher ground. I knew this. I had heard the warnings. But I kept waiting for something to happen, though I didn’t know what.

At last the man opened the front door.

Water rushed into the hall, swirling around his shoes. He stood in the doorway and looked up the street, then down toward the river. Behind him, the woman sat in the chair, her hands folded in her lap.

He looked at me.

It was the first time I was certain that one of them had seen me. He didn’t appear angry. He didn’t beckon. He only looked tired, as if he had recognized me from somewhere and was trying to remember where.

Then the woman rose.

The chair was empty.

She came to the doorway and stood beside him. Her hair hung loose against her cheeks. In the lamplight her face was clear at last: not unfinished, but worn very smooth, like wood handled for years. She looked past the man, past the water, toward the curb where the chair had stood.

A piece of blackened wood drifted into the street. It turned slowly in the current and caught against the bakery steps. The chair’s curved back broke the surface. Its shortened leg pointed toward the house.

The woman raised a hand.

The man reached for her, but she had already stepped down into the water.

She moved toward the chair without struggle. The current parted around her. At the corner she stopped and looked back once. There was no accusation in her face, no plea. Only that same expression of waiting, as if she had expected me to follow her all along.

Then she turned and disappeared into the rain.

The man remained in the doorway. He didn’t call after her. He simply closed the door against the flood.

I stood until the water lifted the chair from the steps and carried it toward the river. In the morning, people would say the flood had taken it. They would say a woman had gone missing from the lower streets. They would say many things, and I would say nothing.

But for weeks afterward, whenever I passed a chair left out in the rain, I looked at its legs to see if one was shorter than the others. And if the wood was black, I waited to see whether it leaned toward me.
