### The Chair in the Water

The chair was the first thing I noticed.

It stood at the curb outside the shuttered bakery, its legs crooked, its arms blistered black. Someone had dragged it out after a fire and left it beneath a handwritten sign that said FREE, though nothing about it looked usable. Rain had darkened the wood, but the seat still held a gray crust of ash. On the back, one pale patch remained unburned: the shape of a small head and two narrow shoulders.

I was watching from my window because there was nothing else to do. The river had climbed over its banks, and the city had been issuing warnings all afternoon. The street below was empty except for the chair and the rain.

Then the couple came along.

They were neither young nor old, dressed in the kind of clothes people wear when they expect to be home soon. The man had a brown coat buttoned to his throat. The woman wore a yellow scarf, bright as a daffodil in the gray street. They walked close together but didn’t touch. Their steps fell in the same rhythm. When the man looked up at a sagging power line, the woman looked up too. When he turned his face from the rain, she turned hers.

They stopped beside the chair.

The woman reached out and placed her palm on its blackened arm. She left it there for several seconds. The man watched her, not the chair. Then she took her hand away, and they continued down the street.

I should have let them go. Instead, I pulled on my coat and followed.

The water was already running along the gutters, carrying leaves and cigarette ends toward the river. I kept half a block behind them, ducking into doorways whenever they slowed. They never hurried. At the corner of Bell and Mercer, the woman stopped to look at a dark shop window. The man waited beside her. In the glass, their reflections stood shoulder to shoulder, and for a moment the three of us seemed to be looking at the same thing.

Then she turned and looked straight at me.

I stepped behind a telephone pole. When I looked again, the couple had gone on.

Their house stood at the end of a narrow lane near the river, a two-story brick place with white trim and a little iron gate. It was the sort of house that had survived several renovations without becoming more attractive. The man unlocked the front door. The woman entered first. He closed it behind them, and a warm square of light appeared in the front window.

I ought to have gone home. Instead, I found a place beside the gate, under the drooping branches of a lilac bush. From there, I could see into the front room.

The man took the woman’s yellow scarf from her neck and hung it on a peg. She stood perfectly still while he did it, her arms at her sides. He stepped back to admire his work, though there was nothing to admire. Then he crossed to a table and set out two plates.

They moved about the room with the same quiet precision they’d shown on the street. He placed a glass. She turned on a lamp. He pulled out a chair. She sat. When he returned with a bowl, she had not changed position.

The chair she sat in was a plain wooden one. Its back was straight; its arms were narrow. From where I stood, the woman’s shoulders seemed to meet the top of it exactly.

I thought of the burned chair at the curb. I thought of the pale shape on its back.

A gust of wind pushed rain through the gate, and water swirled around my shoes. I checked my watch. The hour was later than I’d thought. I was about to leave when the man rose from the table and went out of sight. The woman remained alone in the window.

She lifted one hand and touched the side of her neck.

Even through the rain, I could see a pale crescent there, just below her ear. The skin inside it was smooth and light. Around it, a darker patch spread across her throat and vanished beneath her collar. I told myself it was a shadow. I told myself a great many things.

The water crept higher. It filled the gutters, covered the curb, and lapped at the gate. The lilac leaves hung low enough to brush my hair. I could have left then, but the lane behind me was already a stream, and the river was pushing steadily against the street. I climbed onto the stone step by the house and held the ironwork to keep my balance.

Inside, the man returned with a small framed photograph. He placed it on the table and turned it toward the woman. She looked at it for a long time. The photograph showed a room I couldn’t make out, all light and smoke. The woman reached for it. The man withdrew it.

They exchanged no words that I could hear. He put the photograph away and stood behind her chair, resting his hands on its back. She tilted her head, as if listening to someone behind her.

The lamps flickered.

I looked toward the street. The burned chair had come loose from the curb and was floating past the corner, turning slowly in the flood. It bumped against a trash can, drifted free, and came down the lane toward me. Its blackened legs rose and fell in the water. One arm had broken away.

When it reached the house, it turned broadside to the window. The pale patch on its back faced me.

Inside, the woman stood.

She did it so suddenly that I gripped the gate. The man stayed behind her, his hands still resting on the empty chair. The woman crossed the room and stopped at the window. Her face was calm, almost curious. The pale crescent on her neck glowed in the lamplight.

Then she lifted both arms and bent them at the elbows.

It was a small movement, nothing more than the gesture of someone settling into a seat. Yet her shoulders sloped, her head leaned, and her hands curved inward in the exact outline of the pale patch on the burned chair. For one breath, she seemed to fit the charred shape as perfectly as a person fits a shadow.

The chair knocked against the house. The sound was hollow and soft.

The woman looked past me, down at the water. The man turned toward the window too. His expression was not angry or frightened. It was tired. He raised one hand and pressed his palm to the glass.

The water reached my knees.

I tried the gate, but it had jammed under the current. The chair bumped against the sill again, lifting on a wave. A strip of blackened wood scraped the brick. Inside, the woman leaned forward, and the man put his arm around her waist. For the first time, I saw them touch.

The window darkened. Either the lights had gone out, or the water had risen high enough to cover the glass. I saw only the couple’s reflections, the man’s arm around the woman and her face turned toward mine. Then the current swung the chair away.

I climbed the gate, slipped, and fell into the water. It took me only a few seconds to reach the higher street, though it felt longer. By the time I looked back, the house was gone behind rain and darkness.

The flood receded over the next two days. The couple’s house was empty when I returned. Its front door hung open. The room inside had been stripped by the water, and the floor was scored with a black line where the flood had stood. The little iron gate had twisted inward.

I found the chair wedged against it. Its broken arm was missing, and the ash had washed from the seat, leaving the wood the color of old bone. On the back, the pale patch remained.

I touched it with one finger. The wood was dry.

Then I saw the mark on my hand: a small black crescent, like soot, though I had washed it several times. I rubbed at it until the skin reddened. It did not come off.

For a long while I stood beside the chair, listening to the water drain from the gutters. I kept thinking I heard footsteps in the house behind me—two people walking side by side, perfectly in time.
