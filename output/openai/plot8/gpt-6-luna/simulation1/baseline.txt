### The Blue Bowl

The last time I saw my mother before the heat took her, she was standing on a chair to reach the top shelf.

“Get down,” I said, dropping my overnight bag in the hall.

“I’m looking for the good bowl.”

“The blue one?”

“The blue one is the good bowl.”

She was eighty-two, but she still treated furniture as a set of suggestions. I went to steady the chair while she leaned past me and brought down a china bowl painted with tiny white flowers. Then she handed it to me as if I’d been the one who’d found it.

We had planned to spend the day together in Bellwether, the small town where she’d lived since she was twenty-three. I had driven in the evening before, arriving late enough that she’d left the porch light on and a plate of toast under a tea towel. Now, in the kitchen, the morning sun lay across the linoleum in bright, warm squares. The clock above the stove was seven minutes slow, as it had been for years.

“You’re early,” she said.

“I’m late. You’re early.”

She gave this some thought. “I suppose we’ve both got our habits.”

She made coffee in the dented percolator. I offered to help, and she passed me the sugar bowl without looking. I knew where everything was, even though I hadn’t lived in that house since I was seventeen. The spoons were in the second drawer. The good tea was behind the flour. The drawer with the rubber bands, spare buttons, birthday candles, and batteries that might still work was the third one down.

We ate toast at the kitchen table. She spread hers with butter all the way to the crusts, then cut it into four neat squares. When I was a child, I’d complained that the crusts were too hard. She’d cut them off and eaten them herself. Now I watched her chew the crisp edge and wondered how many small kindnesses I’d mistaken for rules.

After breakfast she insisted we go to the market.

“I can drive,” I said.

“You can, but you don’t know where you’re going.”

“I grew up here.”

“You grew up being driven around here.”

She took her canvas shopping bag from the hook by the back door. It had a green apple printed on one side and a hole in the corner she’d mended with blue thread. I carried it, and she let me, which seemed to please her.

The town had changed in the ways small towns change: the hardware store had become a pharmacy, the pharmacy had become a café, and the café had been there so long that people still called it the pharmacy. The old movie theater was boarded up. The clock on the courthouse had stopped at 2:10. My mother said it had stopped the week she and my father got engaged, although I knew the clock had been repaired twice since then.

At the market, she chose peaches by touch, pressing each one gently with her thumb.

“Not that one,” she said when I reached for a large, unblemished peach. “Too proud of itself.”

She bought four tomatoes, a paper sack of potatoes, and a jar of pickles from a woman she’d known since school. They talked about somebody’s grandson, somebody else’s roof, and the price of eggs. I stood beside them, smiling at intervals, while the years folded up around us like a familiar tablecloth.

On the walk back, we passed the vacant lot where my father used to park his truck. It was now a patch of grass with a sign advertising apartments.

“Your father would’ve hated those,” she said.

“He hated the truck, too.”

“He loved the truck. He hated paying for it.”

She laughed, and I heard the girl she’d been for an instant: quick, delighted, slightly surprised by her own humor. I reached for her arm when we stepped off the curb. She let me keep hold of it for half a block.

At home, she started lunch. She made tomato sandwiches with mayonnaise, salt, and pepper, the way she had when I was small and too impatient to wait for anything cooked. We ate on the back porch. The garden was mostly flowers now, with tomatoes growing in wooden planters near the fence. The sun was already hot on the back of my neck.

“You should wear a hat,” I said.

“I’m in the shade.”

“You’re sitting in the sun.”

“I’m sitting near the shade.”

I found a straw hat hanging by the screen door and put it on her head. She removed it and set it on the chair beside her.

“Now I’m wearing it.”

“Mother.”

“I know, I know. You’ve become sensible.”

She said it gently, but I heard the complaint tucked inside. I had become sensible in the way people do when they begin to count the hours left in a visit.

We spent the afternoon in the living room, where the curtains kept the worst of the heat outside. She brought out a tin of photographs. Most were black and white, though she had arranged them in no particular order: me in a school uniform, my father holding a fish, my mother standing in front of a car with her hair pinned up. There was one of the three of us at the lake, my arms wrapped around her waist, my father squinting into the sun.

“You were afraid of the water,” she said.

“I was not.”

“You clung to me like a barnacle.”

“I was six.”

“You were a very determined barnacle.”

We argued over the year the picture was taken. She insisted it was 1978. I said 1979. Neither of us knew. She told me about the picnic—how my father had forgotten the sandwiches, how she’d bought peaches from a roadside stand, how I’d fallen asleep in the back seat with juice on my shirt. I’d heard the story before, but I let her tell it again, including the part where the peaches were “the best in the county.” She still said this as if the county might dispute it.

Around four, I began to feel the pressure of the drive home, though I had no reason to hurry. I checked my phone. There were messages from work, and I answered two of them. My mother watched me over the edge of the photograph tin.

“You’ve got someplace to be?” she asked.

“No.”

“Your face says otherwise.”

I put the phone away.

She told me she’d been thinking about clearing out the hall closet. I said we could do it together next time. She nodded, not quite believing me, and began sorting the photographs into piles. I offered to take some home.

“No,” she said. “They belong here.”

I was embarrassed by how relieved I felt.

Before I left, she packed the peaches into a paper bag and put them on the passenger seat. She also gave me a jar of pickles, two tomatoes, and a stack of old postcards addressed to no one.

“You don’t need to send these,” she said. “They’re just pretty.”

At the door, she hugged me longer than she usually did. Her sweater smelled of laundry soap and the faint sweetness of the drawer where she kept her handkerchiefs.

“Call when you get home,” she said.

“I will.”

“You say that every time.”

“I know.”

I did call. She answered before the second ring and asked whether I’d eaten one of the peaches yet.

A month later, a neighbor named Mrs. Bell phoned to tell me that my mother had been taken to the hospital with sunstroke. She’d been outside in the garden during the hottest part of the day, trying to move the tomato planters. Mrs. Bell had found her sitting on the ground beside the fence, confused and flushed, her straw hat in the dirt.

By the time I reached Bellwether, my mother had been in the hospital for four days. She was awake and recovering, though the nurse said she tired easily. I sat with her for an hour. She held my hand and asked if I’d eaten the peaches. I told her I had.

“Were they good?”

“They were the best in the county.”

She smiled, then closed her eyes.

When I went to her house the next morning, I planned to collect clothes and a few things she might want at the hospital. The rooms seemed to have been waiting for me. In the kitchen, the clock was still seven minutes slow. The blue bowl sat upside down in the dish rack, a clean crescent of light caught along its rim.

On the table lay the photograph tin, open. The pictures were sorted into three careful piles. Beside them was a crossword puzzle, partly filled in, with the word *lake* written in the wrong direction and crossed out. Her reading glasses rested on top of the paper. I picked them up, then set them down exactly where they’d been.

A grocery list was tucked beneath the sugar bowl. PEACHES, TOMATOES, PICKLES, it said. Below that, in her looping handwriting, was *blue thread*.

I carried the list into the living room and found the green-apple shopping bag hanging by the door. The mended corner had begun to fray again. I could see the blue thread, uneven but strong, knotted on the inside.

The back door stood open to the garden. Sunlight stretched across the porch boards, reaching toward the empty chair where my mother had sat. Her straw hat lay on the seat. I took it inside and hung it beside the door.

Then I returned to the kitchen and closed the curtain against the heat. The room dimmed, but it didn’t become dark. The bowl still gleamed faintly in the rack. The clock still ticked, patiently losing seven minutes.

I made tea the way she did, finding the good tea behind the flour. I sat at her table and looked at the photographs, the list, the puzzle. Around me, the house held the shape of our last day together: a bag by the door, peaches gone but remembered, small talk still warm in the rooms. For a while I did nothing except listen to the clock and let the day come back to me.

When the hospital called to say she was awake again, I was still at the table. I looked at the open tin and the unfinished crossword, and thought of how she had always left a little work for later—not because she expected anything, but because she trusted there would be a later.

“I’ll be right there,” I said.
