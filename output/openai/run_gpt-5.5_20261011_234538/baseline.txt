Barbara would always remember the color of the light.

It came through the high windows of the synagogue in pale winter bars, laying itself across the polished wood, the prayer books, the shoulders of men in dark suits and women with gloved hands folded neatly in their laps. Outside, February had hardened the city into snowbanks and gray streets. Inside, there was warmth, the hush of hundreds breathing together, the familiar rise and fall of sacred words.

She sat near the aisle, one hand resting on the great roundness of her belly. The baby shifted now and then, a private answer to the rabbi’s voice.

Rabbi Adler stood at the front, beloved and commanding without seeming to try. Barbara had heard him speak of grief, of justice, of the stubborn hope required to build a life. That morning, his words moved gently over the congregation, and she found herself imagining the child she carried hearing them too, as if faith could be learned before birth.

Then came the sound.

At first her mind refused to name it. A crack, sharp and wrong, splitting the sanctuary. A pause. Another.

People turned. Someone screamed. The rabbi staggered.

For one suspended second, Barbara saw everything with terrible clarity: the fallen prayer shawl, the shocked faces, the young man with the gun trembling in his hand. He looked less like a monster than a boy lost beyond calling back. His eyes were wide, empty, full of some storm no one else could see.

Barbara rose without thinking.

“Stop,” she tried to say, though the word barely left her throat.

Her body was heavy, awkward, but she stepped into the aisle. She did not know what she meant to do—only that someone had to move toward him, someone had to speak, someone had to remind him he was still among the living.

But the congregation had become a tide.

Panic surged from the front rows, bodies pushing toward the exits. A shoulder struck hers. Hands grabbed at coats, hats, children. The aisle vanished beneath motion and fear. Barbara felt herself swept backward, turned, carried helplessly away from the young man and the fallen rabbi and the terrible center of the room.

“Please,” she cried, one arm wrapped around her belly. “Please, wait!”

No one could hear her. Or everyone heard only their own terror.

Near the doors, an older woman caught Barbara by the elbow and held fast. Together they stumbled into the cold afternoon, where people spilled onto the steps sobbing, praying, calling names. Sirens began somewhere far off, growing louder.

Barbara looked back at the synagogue doors.

Inside, another shot rang out.

The crowd fell silent in waves.

Years later, when her child asked about the day before he was born, Barbara never began with the gun. She began with the light. She told him about the winter sun in the sanctuary, about a rabbi whose voice made people sit taller, about a room full of ordinary souls gathered in peace.

And then, only then, she told him that sometimes history enters through a door no one is watching.

She told him she had tried to walk toward the brokenness.

“That matters,” her son once said.

Barbara touched his face, the face of the life that had moved inside her on that terrible day, and nodded.

“Yes,” she said softly. “I have to believe it does.”
