Barbara had chosen the blue dress because it still fit, more or less, and because blue seemed decent for Shabbat—calm, respectful, the color of a sky that did not know it was February.

By then the baby had become a country of its own. It pressed against her ribs, rolled beneath her hands, made of her body a place with weather and borders and sudden announcements. In the synagogue in Southfield, with the coats steaming faintly from the cold outside and the murmurs rising and falling like water, Barbara sat carefully, one hand beneath her belly, one hand on the prayer book. Just get through the service, she told herself. Then lunch. Then a nap, if God was merciful and her mother did not telephone.

Rabbi Adler’s voice carried as it always did—warm, grave, intelligent. People leaned toward it. Even Barbara, whose back ached and whose ankles had become strangers, felt herself quieted. There were men like that, she thought, men who could make a room remember what it was meant to be.

Then the young man stood.

At first she noticed only the interruption. A shape where no shape should be, movement against the order of things. Someone turning. A whisper clipped in half. The young man had a face too pale for the dark suit he wore. Had she seen him before? Perhaps in the vestibule, perhaps near the back, perhaps not at all. There were always young men one half-recognized—sons home from college, nephews, boys who had grown tall while you were busy marrying and swelling with children.

The gun appeared with a horrible simplicity.

No, Barbara thought, not here.

The first shot cracked through the sanctuary, and for an instant no one understood it. A book dropped. A woman gasped as if scalded. Rabbi Adler moved—or fell—Barbara could not later separate the motion from the sound. The room, which had been one body listening, broke into a hundred bodies trying to live.

The baby lurched inside her.

Barbara rose because someone had to do something. That was the thought, though it was hardly a thought. Someone had to go to the boy, take his arm, say stop, darling, enough, whatever it is, enough. He was young. His hand was shaking. Surely a shaking hand could be reached. Surely a voice could cross the space between them.

She stepped into the aisle.

“Wait,” she may have said. Or perhaps she only opened her mouth.

People surged around her. Elbows, shoulders, the scrape of shoes, the hot animal terror of wool coats and perfume and breath. Someone struck her side; someone seized her sleeve; someone cried out for their mother, though whether a child or an old woman, Barbara never knew. She tried to move forward, but the crowd took her backward, sideways, anywhere but toward the young man with the gun.

But he’s alone, she thought, absurdly, as if loneliness were the emergency. He’s all alone up there.

Another shot—different, final, swallowed almost at once by screaming.

Barbara’s feet went out from under her and came back again. Hands caught her, not gently but enough. The baby tightened inside her, a fist within a fist. She was carried with the others through the aisle, past overturned prayer books, past hats crushed underfoot, past faces emptied of Sabbath. The doors opened to the cold.

Outside, February rushed into her lungs. People stumbled onto the pavement, weeping, praying, calling names. Barbara stood beneath the gray sky in her blue dress, both hands spread over the child who had gone still inside her.

Move, she begged silently. Please. Just move.

After a moment—one second, or ten, or a lifetime—the baby turned.

Barbara bent over the movement, sobbing with relief and shame and a grief she did not yet know how to name. Behind her, inside the synagogue, the holy place had become a place where people would count who was living. In front of her, cars sat glittering with frost. The world looked unchanged, which was the cruelest thing.

She had tried to reach him. Hadn’t she? She had risen. She had meant to go.

For years afterward, that would be the part that returned: not the sound, not even the panic, but the small distance between her and the young man, the few yards of carpet she had believed she could cross, carrying a child beneath her heart as if that alone might make her harmless, as if mercy could be made visible if only she could get close enough.
