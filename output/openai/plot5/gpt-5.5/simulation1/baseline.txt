Daniel began dreaming of the flat in November, when the rain came every evening like an old habit.

It was always the same flat: third floor, end of a dim corridor, pale green door with brass numbers half-polished by other people’s hands. In the dream he had a key, though he never saw it. He simply knew it was in his pocket. He would stand before the door, feeling the weight of the key against his thigh, and understand with the particular certainty of dreams that the flat belonged to him.

Not to him alone. To him and Rachel.

The odd thing was that he and Rachel had never owned such a place. Their marriage had been lived in rented rooms and one narrow terraced house near the station, where the pipes knocked at night and the kitchen window looked directly into the neighbor’s brick wall. They had planned many things—a garden, a dog, a trip to Lisbon—but never a secret flat.

In the dream, he never opened the door.

Sometimes he heard Rachel inside. Not her voice, exactly, but the suggestion of her: a kettle switched off, a chair moved, the soft clatter of a cup set down too hard. Once, he heard her laugh, and the sound made him wake with a breath caught in his chest.

Beside him, Isabel slept on her back with one arm across her forehead, as if she were shielding herself from a light only she could see. Daniel would lie still, listening to the rain press at the windows of their real flat, their shared flat, the one with Isabel’s books stacked in crooked towers and his bicycle leaning guiltily in the hallway. He would tell himself there was nothing disloyal about a dream. Dreams were weather. They passed through whether invited or not.

Still, after the fifth or sixth time, he stopped sleeping well.

“You’re up again,” Isabel said one morning.

Daniel was standing in the kitchen before dawn, staring into the toaster as if it might explain something. He had forgotten to put bread in it.

“I didn’t want to wake you.”

“You didn’t,” she said, tying the belt of her robe. “The lack of you woke me.”

He smiled faintly. Isabel had a gift for sentences that seemed casual until they lodged somewhere tender.

“I’ve been having strange dreams.”

“About work?”

“No.”

She waited. Isabel was a solicitor and had trained herself not to fill silence. It was one of the qualities Daniel admired in her and, at times like this, feared.

“Rachel,” he said.

There. The name landed between them, not heavily, but with the unmistakable sound of an object set carefully on a table.

Isabel reached for the kettle. “What kind of dreams?”

“Not like that,” he said quickly.

“I didn’t assume.”

“I know. Sorry.” He rubbed his eyes. “There’s a flat. An empty flat, or unused. I think it’s ours—mine and Rachel’s, I mean. But it never was. I stand outside the door, and I can’t go in.”

“Can’t?”

“Don’t,” he said. “Maybe don’t.”

Isabel spooned coffee into the cafetière. “How long has this been going on?”

“A few weeks.”

“And you didn’t mention it because?”

He looked at her, then away. Rain moved down the kitchen window in crooked lines. “Because I didn’t want you to think I was… I don’t know. Still there.”

“In the marriage?”

“In the corridor.”

At that, Isabel looked up. Her expression softened, though not entirely. There was hurt there, too, not from the dreams but from being kept outside them.

“I don’t think dreams are evidence,” she said. “But secrecy can feel like evidence.”

“I’m sorry.”

She poured boiling water over the coffee and pressed the lid down without plunging it. “Do you miss her?”

It was a fair question, which made it difficult.

Daniel considered giving the easy answer—no, of course not—but he had known Isabel long enough to understand that easy answers cost more later.

“I miss who I was when I thought I knew how my life would go,” he said. “I don’t know if that’s the same thing.”

Isabel nodded slowly. “That’s honest.”

“Is it all right?”

“I haven’t decided.”

They drank coffee standing up, which was how their most serious conversations often began. Isabel told him, after a while, about a man named Tom whom she had loved in her twenties. Daniel knew the outline: long relationship, shared friends, bad ending. But that morning she gave him more than the outline. Tom had been brilliant at beginnings—new restaurants, spontaneous train journeys, poems copied into birthday cards. But he had no talent for staying. He disappeared into work, into moods, into other women’s attention. For years Isabel had mistaken unpredictability for depth.

“When I dream of him,” she said, “he’s usually waiting for me somewhere. A station, a hotel lobby. I know if I reach him, he’ll explain everything. I wake before I get there.”

“Do you still want the explanation?”

“Sometimes. Less often now.” She looked at him. “Maybe part of us is always queuing for answers from people who left before giving them.”

Rachel had not left in any dramatic sense. There had been no affair, no smashed plates. Instead, their marriage had thinned until light showed through it. They had become polite administrators of a joint disappointment. Rachel wanted movement—new cities, risk, a child perhaps, though never when Daniel was ready to discuss it. Daniel wanted steadiness, though he had called it responsibility. Their arguments were weary and well-rehearsed. By the end, they spoke to each other with the caution of hikers crossing ice.

When she moved out, she took the blue chair, the good knives, and the cat, which had preferred her from the beginning. Daniel had kept the terraced house for another six months, living among the spaces left by missing things.

He met Isabel at a friend’s summer barbecue where both had taken refuge near the potato salad. She laughed at his joke about the host’s experimental sausages. He noticed first her hands, expressive even when still. Their relationship had begun carefully, two people checking for cracks in the ice before stepping forward.

Now, two years later, he was dreaming of a door he would not open.

Over the following days, the dream changed in small ways. A pot plant appeared beside the green door, dusty and dead. Once, an envelope lay on the mat with his name on it in Rachel’s handwriting, though when he bent to pick it up, he found his fingers were numb. Another time, he heard music inside—not a song he recognized, but the muffled rhythm of someone living without him.

He told Isabel each time. Not at length, not dramatically, but because they had agreed that unspoken things grew odd shapes in darkness. Sometimes she listened with curiosity. Sometimes with patience. Once, when he described Rachel humming behind the door, Isabel looked away.

“I know it isn’t rational,” he said.

“I’m not jealous of a dream,” she replied. Then, after a pause: “I am a little jealous of the room you go into without me.”

“But I don’t go in.”

“No,” she said. “You just stand outside it every night.”

That stung because it was true.

On a cold Saturday in December, they decided to clear the hall cupboard, a task they had postponed since moving in together. The cupboard had become a museum of practical failures: a broken umbrella, three tins of paint in slightly different whites, tangled extension cords, a camping stove with no gas canister, and a rolled-up yoga mat neither of them claimed.

Isabel attacked it with black bin bags and moral purpose. Daniel sorted through a cardboard box labeled Misc., a word he distrusted. Under old bank statements and a manual for a printer they no longer owned, he found a small ceramic bowl wrapped in newspaper.

Inside were keys.

Not their current keys, not spares for friends. These were older: a Yale with a red plastic cap, two small brass keys, a mortice key heavy and darkened with age. On the ring hung a fob shaped like a canoe, green paint chipped along its side.

Daniel sat back on his heels.

Isabel, who had been wrestling a deflated air mattress into submission, noticed his stillness. “What is it?”

He held up the keys. They jingled with a sound at once ordinary and ceremonial.

“Old ones?”

“Yes.” His throat tightened unexpectedly. “From before.”

“Rachel?”

He nodded.

Isabel came and sat beside him on the hallway floor. For a moment neither spoke.

“I thought I’d thrown these away,” Daniel said. “Or given them back. I don’t know.”

“What are they for?”

“The house. The shed. Her mother’s back door, I think.” He touched the canoe-shaped fob. “This was from a holiday in Wales.”

As soon as he said it, the memory opened—not like a dream door, but like a window unlatched after years of paint.

He and Rachel, younger, thinner in the face, had gone to the Wye Valley for a week because neither could afford France. It had rained for two days, and they had argued in a pub over nothing: whether to walk back before or after pudding, whether Daniel had sounded dismissive when Rachel suggested applying for a job abroad, whether she always turned every conversation into a referendum on courage.

On the third day, the sun appeared with theatrical generosity. They hired a canoe from a man with a red beard who gave safety instructions while eating a sandwich. Rachel sat in front, Daniel behind. At first they bickered about paddling.

“You’re steering us into the bank,” Rachel called.

“I’m steering us away from the bank.”

“Well, the bank seems very involved.”

Then the river widened. The water carried them with a patience neither possessed. Trees leaned over the surface, their leaves turning silver in the wind. A heron rose from the shallows like a piece of folded paper taking flight. For nearly an hour, they did not discuss the future. They pointed out kingfishers, laughed when Rachel splashed herself, drifted sideways and corrected course. At one bend, Rachel turned around, face bright under her sunhat, and said, “This is the first time you’ve looked happy all week.”

“I am happy,” he had said.

“Remember how?”

He had thought she meant remember this river, this light, us in this unlikely harmony. So he had bought the key fob at the hire shop: a small green canoe with WYE VALLEY printed in white letters. A souvenir of peace.

Later, of course, peace had become harder to reach. You could not paddle a marriage through every rapid by admiring the scenery. But the memory was still there, intact and sunlit, not proof that they should have stayed together, only proof that they had once been kind to each other on a river.

Daniel told Isabel all this while sitting among dust and discarded manuals.

She listened, knees drawn up, chin resting on them. “It sounds lovely.”

“It was.”

“Does that make you sad?”

“Yes,” he said. “But not in the way I expected.”

“How, then?”

“Like finding an old photograph of someone else’s holiday. I remember being there, but I can’t quite inhabit him.”

Isabel reached into the bowl and lifted the canoe fob. “Maybe the dream flat is all the unused life. The version where you and Rachel bought a place and kept going.”

“Maybe.”

“And you’re standing outside because you don’t actually want to live there. You just want to know what’s inside.”

Daniel looked at the keys in his palm. For weeks he had imagined the unopened door as a sign of unfinished love, a betrayal in architecture. But now, holding keys that belonged to locks long changed or demolished, he felt something loosen. Not everything that could be opened needed opening. Not every key was an invitation. Some were evidence that you had lived somewhere and then left.

That night, he dreamed of the flat again.

The corridor was brighter. The dead plant was gone. The green door stood before him, numbers gleaming. He felt in his pocket and found the key, cold and exact.

Inside, Rachel laughed.

Daniel took out the key. For the first time, he saw it clearly: not a Yale, not a mortice key, but the little brass key to the shed behind the old terraced house. It would not fit the flat door. It had never been meant to.

He began to laugh, quietly at first, then with relief. The sound inside stopped.

“Daniel?” Rachel called from behind the door, though her voice was younger than it should have been.

He did not answer. He placed the key on the mat, turned around, and walked down the corridor toward the stairs. With every step, the walls grew lighter, until he woke to morning.

Isabel was awake already, reading emails on her phone.

“I left,” he said.

She turned to him. “The corridor?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He waited, but she did not ask what Rachel had said, or whether he felt cured. Isabel had a respect for thresholds.

After breakfast, Daniel searched online while Isabel watered the basil plant that was always near death and always somehow surviving.

“What are you doing?” she asked.

“Looking at canoe hire.”

She raised an eyebrow. “In December?”

“We could book for spring. Or summer. Somewhere gentle. No rapids, no heroics.”

“You hate organized outdoor activity.”

“I hate being cold and underprepared. This is different.”

“Is it?”

He closed the laptop halfway. “I found those keys, and I remembered something. Not Rachel, exactly. Not us. A feeling. Being on water. Having to move together or go in circles.”

Isabel leaned against the counter. “That sounds like a metaphor with wet shoes.”

“It probably is.”

“I’m not very good in boats.”

“Neither am I.”

“That may be a problem.”

“It may be the point.”

She considered him with the careful amusement he loved. “Are you inviting me to paddle down a river because you’ve had an emotional breakthrough involving a cupboard?”

“Yes.”

“Well,” she said, “when you put it like that.”

They booked a weekend in May on a quiet stretch of river not far from the city. For the rest of winter, the plan sat ahead of them like a small lamp. Daniel still thought of Rachel occasionally. Sometimes he wondered where she lived now, whether she had become the person she had been trying to reach. He hoped so. The dreams did not return, though once he dreamed of a hallway filled with sunlight and woke feeling neither longing nor fear.

In spring, they drove out on a Saturday morning beneath a sky patched with blue. Isabel packed too much food and three kinds of waterproof layer. Daniel brought the old canoe key fob, not attached to any keys now, just slipped into the pocket of his jacket like a charm whose magic was mostly administrative.

At the hire place, a teenage instructor explained paddling technique with grave authority. Isabel listened as if preparing for litigation. Daniel nodded and understood about half.

When they pushed off, the canoe wobbled violently.

“Absolutely not,” Isabel said, gripping the sides.

“We’re fine.”

“We are not fine. We are a headline.”

“Small local couple perish in decorative stream.”

She made a sound that was half laugh, half warning. They drifted backward into some reeds.

“Forward,” Isabel said. “I believe the concept is forward.”

“I’m trying.”

“Try symmetrically.”

For the first twenty minutes, they zigzagged. Daniel overcorrected. Isabel paddled too hard on the left. The bank approached and retreated with comic menace. A duck watched them with obvious judgment.

Then, gradually, they found a rhythm—not perfect, not graceful, but theirs. Daniel learned to say, “Left a bit,” without sounding like a man issuing commands. Isabel learned that stillness was sometimes steering. The water took them under a low bridge where their voices echoed, then past a field of yellow flowers. The city loosened behind them. Branches trailed in the river, sketching circles on the surface.

At one point, Isabel rested her paddle across her knees. “Tell me what you’re thinking.”

Daniel looked at the moving water. Years ago, Rachel had turned in a canoe and asked him to remember happiness. For a long time he had thought remembering meant preserving, keeping a room ready for the past to return and sit down. But perhaps memory was more like a river: not a place to live, but a current that carried fragments forward, changing them as it went.

“I’m thinking,” he said, “that I’ve spent a lot of time being afraid of what old things mean.”

“And what do they mean?”

“That they happened.”

Isabel smiled. “Profound.”

“I’m working up to profound.”

“Take your time.”

They floated in companionable silence. Daniel reached into his pocket and closed his hand around the small green canoe. Its paint was chipped; the printed words had almost worn away. He thought of the keys at home in the ceramic bowl, no longer hidden but not displayed either. Objects, like dreams, could be allowed to be what they were.

Ahead, the river bent out of sight.

“Ready?” Isabel asked, lifting her paddle.

Daniel dipped his into the water. “Ready.”

They moved together—not straight, not fast, but forward.
