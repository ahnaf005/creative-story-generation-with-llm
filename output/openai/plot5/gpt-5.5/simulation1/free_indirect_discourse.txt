For three weeks Daniel dreamed of the flat.

It was always on the third floor, though the building changed. Sometimes it stood above a launderette on a road he half remembered from the first year of his marriage to Rachel, when they had lived on takeaways and enthusiasm and could still make a novelty of damp walls. Sometimes it appeared at the top of a clean municipal staircase, the kind with a window at each landing and rubber nosing on every step. Once it was in a Georgian terrace, absurdly grand, with a brass plate by the door bearing no name.

But the flat itself was always the same. One bedroom. A narrow kitchen. A sitting room with a dead fireplace and a window looking onto nothing in particular: a courtyard, an alley, a blank wall. It was unfurnished except for a roll of carpet tied with string and standing in the corner like a body prepared for storage. Dust lay on the skirting boards. The electricity did not work. The air had that shut-up smell of places waiting patiently to be claimed.

And Rachel was always there.

Not in a dramatic way. Not crying, not accusing. She would be standing by the window, or sitting on the floor with her back against the wall, wearing the blue jumper he had once shrunk in the wash and never forgiven himself for because she had liked it so much. In the dreams she looked younger, but not young. There was no soft-focus mercy. Her hair was tied up anyhow. Her face had the alert, faintly tired expression she had worn when checking whether he had really listened to something important.

“You’ve never used it,” she said in one dream.

“I didn’t know we had it,” he answered.

“You did know.”

Then he would wake beside Isabel, heart moving too quickly, his mouth dry, the bedroom dark except for the digital glare of the alarm clock. 3:17. 4:08. Ridiculous times. Times when a man could be undone by a flat that did not exist.

At first he said nothing. Dreams were dreams. If a person began reporting every strange night-thing to his partner, the house would fill with them—half-formed rooms, dead relatives, teeth falling out, exams missed decades ago. Isabel slept on her side, knees slightly drawn up, one hand under her cheek. In the morning she would wake promptly and practically, swinging her feet to the floor as if she had received instructions in her sleep. She did not need Daniel adding Rachel to the breakfast table between the marmalade and the butter dish.

Besides, he and Isabel had been careful about exes. Not evasive, exactly; mature. That was the word people used when nobody wanted to look too closely. Isabel had a man called Martin in her past, a photographer with a talent for disappearing into projects and returning with stories in which Isabel appeared only as someone who had watered his plants. Daniel had Rachel, a marriage of nine years and an ending so civilized people had praised them for it, as though the absence of smashed plates proved the absence of harm.

They had been together four years now, Daniel and Isabel. They owned too many mugs, argued about the thermostat, took turns buying coffee. Their life had a reliable shape. On Thursdays Isabel had yoga. On Saturdays Daniel went to the market and bought vegetables with unrealistic intentions. It was not passionless. No, that would be unfair. It was warm, companionable, decent. A word like decent should not feel like an accusation.

The dream came again on a wet Tuesday in November. This time the flat was above a parade of shops. He climbed the stairs knowing Rachel would be there, and found her in the kitchen, opening cupboards that contained nothing but curled shelf paper.

“It could have been useful,” she said.

“For what?”

“For us.”

When he woke, Isabel was awake too. She had propped herself on one elbow, hair falling over her face.

“You made a noise,” she said.

“Sorry.”

“A frightened noise.”

He stared at the ceiling. There was a crack there they had been meaning to fill since spring. Not a crack, Isabel would say, a hairline fracture, which sounded worse and more precise.

“It was a dream.”

“I gathered.”

He could have said, Just nonsense. He could have turned over, arranged his breathing into sleep-shapes, let morning sand it away. But he was tired of the flat, tired of being summoned to it like a tenant in arrears.

“I keep dreaming about Rachel,” he said.

Isabel was quiet. He felt her stillness beside him. There it was, then. A foolish confession at four in the morning. Well done, Daniel. Excellent timing.

“What happens?” she asked.

Her voice was level. Not cold. Not warm either. A voice holding a cup very steadily.

“There’s a flat. Empty. We seem to own it, or rent it, or we’re meant to be using it. I don’t know. She’s there. She tells me I never used it.”

“Used it for what?”

“That’s the thing. I don’t know.”

Isabel lay back down, but not away from him. They remained side by side, staring upward as though the answer might be written on the ceiling crack.

“Do you think it’s about Rachel,” she said, “or about the flat?”

He almost laughed. Only Isabel would separate them so neatly. The person and the property. The wound and the square footage.

“I thought dreams were supposed to be about sex.”

“Sometimes they’re about paperwork.”

He did laugh then, too loudly for the hour. She smiled; he heard it rather than saw it.

“Did you have a place you didn’t use?” she asked.

“No.”

“Not literally.”

He considered this. With Rachel there had been plans. A garden. A child, perhaps, though perhaps had hardened and softened so many times that by the end it was less a possibility than a bruise. A holiday in Greece. A canoe, oddly; that came to him then with the force of an object dragged from deep water. They had once taken a canoe out on the Wye, early in their marriage, and he had become captivated by the idea of buying one. Weekends on rivers, picnics, damp sandwiches, the two of them moving in rhythm. Rachel had indulged it for a while. They had looked at second-hand canoes online, argued lightly over colors. Then work had become frantic, her mother ill, money tight. Later, when money was less tight, they were already paddling in different directions without admitting it.

“Maybe,” he said.

Isabel turned her head. “Maybe what?”

“There were things we didn’t do.”

“There always are.”

This sounded like consolation, but it wasn’t entirely. Isabel had her own rooms of unused life; he knew that. With Martin she had nearly moved to Lisbon. She had once mentioned it while chopping onions, as if the city had been a sofa they decided not to buy. A studio flat above a bakery, Martin claimed; light like milk in the mornings. Then a gallery opportunity in Glasgow, a sick father, a lost passport, who knew. The plan dissolved. Later, so did they.

Daniel had never asked whether she regretted not going. He had assumed that not asking was kindness. Or cowardice dressed as kindness. Mature again, that smug word.

Over coffee the next morning, Isabel brought it up herself.

“I used to dream about Martin’s cameras,” she said.

Daniel looked up from buttering toast too vigorously.

“Did you?”

“After we split. I’d find them everywhere. In cupboards. Under the bed. Inside the fridge once. All of them pointing at me.”

“That sounds subtle.”

“It was very subtle. My unconscious has the light touch of a brick.” She stirred her coffee though she took it black. “I think I felt watched by the version of myself I’d been with him. The woman in his photographs, always waiting for him to notice the light.”

Daniel tried to imagine her younger, waiting. Isabel seemed to him incapable of waiting for long. She acted. She rang plumbers. She booked train tickets. She did not drift beautifully in other people’s light.

“Do you miss him?” he asked.

The question slipped out too bluntly. He regretted it, then didn’t. Why shouldn’t it exist in the room? They were grown people. They had survived worse than a sentence.

“No,” she said. Then, after a pause, “I miss who I thought I might become. That’s different.”

He nodded, though the difference was not as clean as she made it sound.

That evening, coming home from work, he found himself taking a detour past the road where he and Rachel had first rented a flat. The launderette was now a vape shop with a purple sign. Their old doorway had been repainted an optimistic green. He stood across the street in the drizzle feeling absurdly conspicuous, a middle-aged man loitering opposite his own history.

What had he expected? Rachel at the window? A dusty unused flat wedged above the shop, waiting for him to unlock it? He had no key. He had no right.

Rachel lived in Bristol now, he thought. Or near Bristol. They exchanged messages on birthdays, not always on time. Her profile picture showed her with a dog and a man Daniel had never met, all three of them windswept on a beach. She looked happy, or as happy as anyone could be required to look in a small square on a phone.

At home Isabel was making soup. The kitchen windows had steamed up, and the house smelled of garlic and thyme. He almost told her where he’d been but stopped. Not secrecy, exactly. He was tired of narrating himself. Instead he chopped bread, badly.

“You’re thinking very loudly,” she said.

“Sorry.”

“I didn’t say stop.”

So he told her. About the detour, the green door, the ridiculousness of standing in the rain like a detective investigating his own failure.

She ladled soup into bowls. “And?”

“And nothing. It’s a vape shop.”

“Powerful symbolism.”

“Everything’s symbolism if you’re desperate enough.”

They ate at the small table by the window. Outside, rain stitched the glass. Isabel said, “When Martin and I split up, I went to look at the street we nearly lived on in Lisbon. Online, I mean. Street View. I moved along it one click at a time like a ghost.”

He pictured her sitting at a laptop, dragging herself through a city that had never been hers.

“What did it feel like?”

“Odd. Sad. Also boring. There was a pharmacy, a bin lorry, a woman with shopping. I wanted it to be enchanted. It was just a street.”

That night he did not dream of the flat. Nor the next. For several days he slept heavily and woke only when the alarm insisted. Perhaps confession had done it. Perhaps Isabel, with her quiet questions, had opened a window and aired the place out.

Then, on Sunday, they tackled the cupboard under the stairs.

It was Isabel’s idea, which meant it would happen. Daniel’s preferred method for household disorder was geological: let things settle into layers and become too ancient to disturb. But Isabel had bought clear plastic boxes and labels. He suspected the labels meant business.

They found batteries, three umbrellas, two broken picture frames, a camping stove neither remembered buying, and a box of cables belonging to extinct technologies. At the back, behind a paint tin, Daniel discovered a biscuit tin with robins on the lid.

“What’s that?” Isabel asked.

“Treasure, probably.”

Inside were keys. Dozens of them. Old Yale keys, tiny suitcase keys, a bike lock key with a cracked red fob, keys whose locks had vanished years before. They lay in a dull metallic heap, cold and intimate. He tipped them into his palm.

And there it was: a brass key on a cork float.

For a moment the cupboard, the plastic boxes, Isabel kneeling beside him with dust on her jeans—all of it thinned. He was on a river under a pale summer sky, water slapping softly against the side of a canoe. Rachel sat in front of him, hair escaping from her ponytail, laughing because he had over-corrected and sent them into reeds. The cork float swung from the key in the ignition of a borrowed boathouse locker. No, not ignition—ridiculous, canoes did not have ignitions. Locker key. They had locked their clothes, shoes, wallets in a wooden shed by the landing stage. The float had been printed with the name of the canoe hire place: KINGFISHER CANOEING. Blue letters, now faded almost white.

Rachel had held it up and said, “If we drop this, at least it will bob about accusingly.”

He had said, “Like most of our possessions.”

They had paddled badly at first, out of sync, blades knocking, the canoe slewing sideways. Then they found a rhythm. Not perfect, but enough. Dip, pull, lift. Dip, pull, lift. The river carrying them and resisting them, both at once. At lunch they pulled up on a bank and ate cheese rolls flattened in a rucksack. Rachel lay back in the grass and declared this the beginning of their river life. He had believed her because belief was easy then. Because the future had seemed like an empty room they would furnish together.

“What is it?” Isabel asked.

Daniel realized he had been holding the key too tightly.

“Canoeing,” he said, which was not an answer and yet was the whole answer.

He told her about the Wye, the hired canoe, the plan to buy one. He had not thought of that day in years, or had avoided thinking of it because it hurt in a strangely wholesome way. Not the sharp hurt of betrayal or anger. The ache of seeing two people before they knew what would become of them. There they were, sunburned and competent enough, paddling toward a future that would not arrive.

Isabel took the key from him and turned it over in her fingers.

“Kingfisher,” she read faintly. “Do you want to go?”

“Where?”

“Canoeing.”

He laughed. “Now?”

“Not this minute. I don’t think the cupboard is navigable.”

He looked at her. She was serious, or serious enough. Her face was open in the way it became when she was offering something without wanting to appear generous. He had seen that expression when she suggested he take the last piece of cake, when she offered to drive him to the hospital to visit an uncle he didn’t much like, when she asked if he wanted to talk after a bad day and pretended not to mind if he said no.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Of course he knew. He wanted to and did not want to. He wanted the clean river air, the remembered rhythm, the chance to take an old dream out of storage and see if it still floated. He did not want to drag Rachel into a canoe with Isabel seated in front of him. Or behind him. Where did one put the dead marriage? Middle seat? Extra ballast?

Isabel seemed to hear all this without him saying it.

“It wouldn’t be with her,” she said.

“No.”

“And it wouldn’t be instead of her.”

“No.”

“It would just be us in a boat, probably going in circles.”

He smiled.

The next Saturday was cold but bright. They drove west with a thermos and sandwiches Isabel had made, because she trusted neither riverside cafes nor Daniel’s concept of lunch. Frost held in the shadows of hedges. The sky was a thin hard blue. At the canoe hire place, a young man in a wool hat gave them buoyancy aids and instructions delivered with the sleepy authority of someone who had repeated himself all morning.

“Ever done this before?” he asked.

“Once,” Daniel said.

“Years ago,” Isabel added.

The river was higher than he remembered, darker, threaded with quick silver where the light struck it. The canoe rocked under him as he stepped in, and he felt briefly foolish, old, overdressed. Isabel settled at the front, paddle across her knees.

“If we capsize,” she said, “I want you to know I’ll blame you forever.”

“Fair.”

They pushed off.

At once the canoe angled toward the bank. Daniel over-corrected. Isabel paddled on the wrong side, or perhaps he did. A branch scraped the side. Somewhere behind them the young man called advice they could not decipher.

“This is going very well,” Isabel said.

“Extremely.”

They laughed, and the canoe wobbled dangerously. Then, slowly, through trial and embarrassment, they improved. Daniel watched Isabel’s paddle enter the water ahead of him. Dip, pull, lift. He matched her. The canoe straightened. The bank slipped by: bare willows, a flash of bird, fields rising pale beyond the trees. The cold reached his fingers and nose, but his body warmed with movement.

He thought of Rachel, inevitably. Not as she had appeared in the dreams, stationed in that empty flat, guardian of unused rooms. He thought of her laughing in reeds, accusing cork key held aloft. He hoped she had rivers of her own. He hoped she had used things he knew nothing about.

The thought came without the old tug of possession. It passed over him like the shadow of a bird.

Ahead, Isabel turned her head slightly. “All right back there?”

“Yes,” he said.

And he was. Not cured, not absolved, not made new by a canoe, for heaven’s sake. Life did not work so neatly. But the flat in his dreams seemed, from here, less like an accusation than an invitation misunderstood. An empty room was not only what had been neglected. It was also space. It could remain empty. It could be left behind. Or one could open the windows, sweep out the dust, and decide—without witnesses, without ceremony—what belonged there now.

Isabel’s paddle rose, scattering bright drops.

“Daniel,” she said, “you’re drifting.”

He looked up. They were turning broadside to the current.

“Sorry,” he called, and dipped his paddle.

Together, awkwardly, laughing, they brought the canoe around. The river took them forward.
