The first time Daniel dreamed of the flat, Rachel was standing in the corridor outside it with her hand on the handle, smiling as if he had arrived late to a dinner party.

“You have the keys,” she said.

He looked down. In his palm lay a ring of keys, though in the dream he knew at once that none of them would fit. Dreams were efficient like that, cruelly informed. The corridor smelled of dust, old paint, and central heating that had not worked for years. A bicycle leaned against the wall. Somewhere a radio played a song from a summer he could not place.

Rachel turned the handle. The door opened onto darkness.

He woke with his heart beating hard, Isabel asleep beside him, one arm flung above her head in the dramatic posture of someone surrendering to an invisible army. The room was blue with early morning. Their room, their bed, their old sash window rattling when buses passed. It was ridiculous to feel guilty. One could not be blamed for dreams. Still, he lay very still, as if Isabel might wake and read the evidence on his face.

The dream returned three nights later, then twice the following week. Always the same flat, though sometimes the details changed. In one version the walls were covered in damp, the paper hanging in strips. In another it was perfectly clean, furnished but unused, with a sofa in stiff grey fabric and a vase of dry reeds on a table. Rachel was not always there. Sometimes he heard her in another room, humming. Sometimes the rooms were empty, and it was the emptiness itself that seemed to be waiting for him.

By the end of the month Daniel had begun to dread sleep. It was absurd, undignified at forty-six, to be haunted by a property he had never owned. During the day he catalogued the dream with the weary practicality he brought to work emails and broken boilers. Perhaps the flat was grief, or regret, or the marriage itself—uninhabited, kept locked, still somehow costing him money. Very neat. Very suitable for a magazine article. None of which stopped him from waking with the sense that he had forgotten to do something important.

Rachel had been gone from his daily life for seven years and from his marriage for nine, depending on how one measured such things. The divorce had been civil in the way accidents were sometimes described as minor: everyone survived, but the road was closed for hours. She had moved to Bristol, married a man called Tom who made furniture, and sent one careful email when Daniel’s mother died. He had replied with equal care. Since then, silence.

Isabel knew all of this, or enough of it. She had her own past, naturally—Michael, the photographer, who still sent birthday messages too artful to be innocent; Leon, who had wanted children and certainty in equal measure. They had compared histories early on, over wine, as people did now, laying out their ruins to prove they were no longer living in them.

And yet Daniel did not tell her about the dream.

At first it seemed too small to mention. Then, because he had not mentioned it, it became too large.

One Saturday in March, rain pressed against the kitchen windows while Isabel made coffee and Daniel stood uselessly with the compost caddy. She was wearing his jumper, the green one with a fraying cuff. Her hair was pinned up badly, which always moved him; Isabel elegant at work could be admired, but Isabel with a pencil stuck through her hair and toast crumbs on her sleeve could undo him completely.

“You’re miles away,” she said.

“No, I’m not.”

“Daniel.”

He scraped coffee grounds into the caddy, missing slightly. “I’ve been having strange dreams.”

She looked at him over the rim of her mug. “Strange how?”

And then, because the rain had made the kitchen feel sealed off from consequence, he told her. Not everything at first. Just the corridor, the flat, the keys. Then Rachel, since leaving her out made the story worse, gave it a shape it did not deserve.

Isabel listened without moving much. She had the kind of stillness that could be either generosity or judgment; he had not yet learned to tell which.

When he finished, she said, “Do you want to see her?”

“No.” Too quickly? He took a breath. “No, I don’t think so.”

“You don’t think so?”

“I mean, no. I don’t want to see her.”

Isabel nodded, but her eyes had sharpened. “Do you miss her?”

There it was, the crude instrument lowered onto the table. He wanted to object to the word. Miss. As if the past were a train one had failed to catch.

“I miss things,” he said. “Not her exactly.”

“What things?”

He leaned against the counter. He could feel himself becoming defensive, which was unfair, because she had asked kindly enough. What things? Their first flat in Tufnell Park. Sunday newspapers. Being thirty-two and foolish with money. Rachel reading recipes aloud and never making them. The way they had once believed their problems were temporary because they were young and therefore everything must be temporary.

“The usual,” he said. “Youth. Possibility. My knees.”

Isabel smiled, but not fully. “You can have my knees. They’re not what they were.”

Later that morning they walked to the market under one umbrella, and the conversation seemed to have dissolved. They bought leeks, fish, a loaf of sourdough too expensive to taste as ordinary as it did. Still, Daniel sensed something had shifted, some plank loosened underfoot.

That evening Isabel said, “I used to dream about Michael’s studio.”

They were on the sofa, the television paused because neither of them had been watching. Daniel turned to her, surprised.

“After we split up,” she went on. “For years, actually. I’d be in his studio, but all his photographs were of rooms I’d never been in. Or women I didn’t know wearing my clothes.”

“That’s unsettling.”

“It was. And obvious, probably.”

He waited.

“I didn’t want him back,” she said. “But I missed being the person I had been around him. Not always. He was vain, and he made me feel provincial for liking curtains. But sometimes I was brave with him. Or thought I was.”

Daniel looked at the frozen television image: a woman in a crime drama leaning over a body with professional sadness. So Isabel too had kept locked rooms. Of course she had. Why had he imagined himself uniquely encumbered?

“I’m sorry I didn’t tell you sooner,” he said.

She rested her head against the sofa. “I’m glad you told me at all.”

The dreams did not stop, but they changed. Rachel appeared less often. The flat became more detailed. There was a narrow kitchen with yellow tiles, a bathroom with a cracked mirror, a bedroom containing only a mattress rolled against the wall. In the sitting room, always, was a window overlooking water. Not the sea. A canal, perhaps, or a slow river edged with reeds. In the dream Daniel would stand at the window and feel an ache so specific it seemed less like longing than recognition.

He began to suspect the flat was not invented.

For days he walked through his memories like a man searching drawers for a receipt. Had he and Rachel once viewed such a place? There had been many viewings in those years: garden flats with subsidence, maisonettes above takeaways, one ex-council place full of orchids and cat hair. They had never bought anything together. Not for lack of trying. They had been forever almost ready—almost secure, almost solvent, almost certain.

One Sunday in April, Isabel decided they should clear the cupboard under the stairs.

“It’s become a moral problem,” she announced.

“It’s storage.”

“It’s a cave system. There may be bats.”

They spent the morning pulling out paint tins, dead umbrellas, an electric heater, two tennis rackets neither of them remembered acquiring. Daniel found a box labeled MISC in Rachel’s handwriting.

The sight of it was like hearing his name called in a crowd.

He had thought all those boxes were gone. After the divorce there had been a distribution of objects, some practical, some punitive. Books, pans, the good lamps, the table bought in a fit of optimism from a shop they could not afford. This box must have followed him through two moves, unnoticed and unopened, surviving by being dull.

Isabel, kneeling among dust and carrier bags, saw his face. “Do you want to do that alone?”

“No,” he said, and meant it after a second. “Stay.”

Inside were old bills, instruction manuals for appliances long dead, postcards, a cassette tape, a matchbook from a restaurant where they had celebrated their second anniversary badly. At the bottom lay a small envelope. Daniel tipped it into his hand.

Keys.

Four of them on a rusted ring, with a plastic tag gone cloudy with age. On it, in Rachel’s writing, were the words: LITTLE VENICE?

The question mark pierced him.

Not Tufnell Park. Not anywhere they had lived. Little Venice—yes. The viewing. The flat by the canal. How had he forgotten?

And then memory opened, not cleanly but in a rush.

They had been late. Rachel had been furious because Daniel had taken a work call outside the tube station, pacing and saying, “I understand, but realistically,” while she stared at him with the pure contempt reserved for people who use words like realistically when they mean no. The estate agent had been young, pink-faced, apologetic about the smell in the stairwell. The flat had belonged to an old woman who had moved into a home, or died—Daniel could not remember which, perhaps the agent had not known. It was empty except for a wardrobe, a lamp without a shade, and a calendar from three years before.

But the window. Yes, the window.

The sitting room overlooked the canal. A narrowboat had moved slowly past while they stood there, painted red and green, its chimney smoking. On the towpath a man carried a canoe over one shoulder, absurd and graceful, like someone bearing half a bright blue shell.

Rachel had said, “Imagine living here.”

And Daniel had imagined it. Not just the flat, but a life in which evenings widened instead of collapsed. They would walk by the water. They would buy a secondhand canoe. Why not? They would become people who did such things. Calm people, outdoors people, people with thermoses and waterproof bags. Rachel had laughed at him when he said this, but fondly. For ten minutes they had been happy in an empty room they could not afford.

Then the offer had fallen through, or perhaps they had never made one. Work intensified. Rachel’s father got ill. They began to argue about money, then about time, then about the tone in which one mentioned money or time. The flat vanished, not because it was unimportant, but because it belonged to the version of them that had vanished first.

“Daniel?” Isabel said.

He realized he had been sitting back on his heels for a long time, keys cupped in his palm.

“We viewed a flat,” he said. “Years ago. It was by the canal.”

“With Rachel.”

“Yes.”

“And these are the keys?”

“No. I don’t know. Maybe copies from the agent? Or keys she thought belonged to it.” He laughed softly. “We probably forgot to return them. Very criminal.”

Isabel took the tag between finger and thumb. “Little Venice?”

“We stood at the window and watched someone with a canoe.” He looked up at her. “I think that’s what I’ve been dreaming about. Not Rachel. Not really.”

“What, then?”

He considered. The answer felt delicate; handled roughly, it would turn sentimental and false. “A door I didn’t go through.”

Isabel nodded. Dust clung to one knee of her jeans. She looked tired, patient, real. Not a symbol. Not an interpreter of dreams. His present life, sitting among dead umbrellas.

“We could go,” she said.

“To the flat?”

“To the canal.”

So they went the following weekend, because Isabel was often literal in ways that saved him.

Little Venice was brighter than memory. The water flashed under a thin spring sun; narrowboats lined the banks with names like Patience and Juniper; tourists photographed themselves on bridges. Daniel had expected melancholy to rise and claim him, but instead he felt faintly foolish. This was not a sacred site. It was a pleasant London afternoon with overpriced coffee and children dropping crumbs for ducks.

They walked without speaking much. At one point Isabel slipped her arm through his, not possessively, not consolingly. Just there.

“Do you know which building?” she asked.

“I thought I would.”

But he did not. The streets resembled one another; white stucco, black railings, basement steps. The past, denied exact coordinates, lost some of its authority.

Near the water, they found a canoe hire sign: PADDLE THE REGENT’S CANAL. Daniel laughed before he could stop himself.

“What?” Isabel said.

“This feels heavy-handed.”

“Life is rarely subtle when it’s trying to help.”

“I haven’t been in a canoe since I was twelve.”

“Good. Then you’ll have no bad habits.”

They hired one for an hour. The young man at the dock gave instructions in a tone that implied he expected them to ignore all of them and capsize immediately. Isabel stepped in first, wobbled, cursed, then sat down with exaggerated dignity. Daniel followed, clutching the sides. The canoe rocked beneath him, alarmingly responsive.

For the first few minutes they were terrible. They zigzagged. They drifted into weeds. Isabel accused him of steering like a committee. He told her she paddled with the violence of someone killing eels. A narrowboat passed, and its owner watched them with the calm pity of the competent.

Then, gradually, they found a rhythm. Daniel’s paddle entered the water; Isabel’s followed. Pull, lift, drip. Pull, lift, drip. The city rearranged itself at water level. Bridges became tunnels of shadow. Gardens leaned over them. On a balcony, someone watered geraniums. A heron stood on one leg with judicial severity.

Daniel felt his breathing slow.

This, then. Not recovery exactly, not revelation. No dream dissolved into sunlight, no final key turned in a lock. Only this narrow craft, this woman in front of him glancing back to say, “Left a bit,” this water accepting each stroke and closing after it.

He thought of Rachel without pain sharp enough to draw blood. She would have liked this, perhaps. Or hated the damp seat and pretended otherwise. He hoped she was well. The thought came and went like the reflection of a branch.

“Are you all right?” Isabel asked.

“Yes,” he said.

“You went quiet.”

“I’m concentrating.”

“Miracle.”

He smiled. Ahead, the canal curved out of sight. They would have to turn back soon, return the canoe, go home, make dinner, answer messages, remember to buy milk. The ordinary world waited with all its blunt demands. But for now they moved forward together, awkwardly and well enough, carrying no luggage that could not be balanced, no past that could not sit behind them for a while and watch the water.
