Daniel had been dreaming again about the flat with the blue door.

In the dream, it was always late afternoon, though he never saw the sun. The corridor outside the flat was lit by a high window and smelled faintly of damp plaster, polish, and old heating pipes. The door was not exactly blue, more the color of a storm cloud seen from indoors. Its paint was chipped around the keyhole, and the brass number—3B—hung slightly crooked.

He always had the keys.

They were heavy in his hand, two long skeleton keys and one small modern one with a red plastic tag. He never knew how he had got them. He never questioned it. In dreams, responsibility appeared already assigned.

He would unlock the door and step into the flat.

It was unused, but not empty. There were rooms full of covered furniture, the sheets floating white and patient in the dimness. A wooden table stood near the kitchen window with a bowl on it, clean except for a single brown leaf. In the bedroom was a wardrobe he never opened. In the living room, an armchair faced the blank wall as if someone had once sat there watching an invisible television.

And Rachel was always somewhere inside.

Not in an alarming way. She did not appear bloodless or accusing, as she might have in a cheaper dream. Sometimes he would hear her humming in the kitchen. Sometimes he would see the back of her red coat pass across a doorway. Once she was sitting on the floor of the bedroom, sorting through old photographs, and when he came in she looked up and smiled at him as if he had only been gone a few minutes.

“You found it,” she said.

“What is this place?” he asked.

But dreams did not permit useful answers. Rachel only looked down at the photographs again. When he woke, the image that stayed with him was not her face but the flat: its withheld rooms, its mute promise, its sense of being owned by him and neglected by him.

The dreams came three times in one month. Then five times in two. He did not tell Isabel at first.

This was partly because he had learned, during his marriage, that dreams were a hazardous currency. Rachel had collected them. She used to sit cross-legged at breakfast, asking him what he had dreamed, then interpreting with a seriousness that made him uneasy. “A train means a decision,” she would say. “Teeth mean fear of losing control. A house is always yourself.”

“And what’s a shed?” he had once asked.

“A shed is a smaller, more embarrassing self.”

He had laughed, but she had not. Rachel rarely laughed at theories.

Isabel was different. She treated dreams as weather: interesting, perhaps beautiful, rarely significant. If he told her he had dreamed of being chased by swans through an airport, she would say, “That sounds tiring,” and hand him coffee.

Still, he did not mention the flat or Rachel. He and Isabel had been together nearly three years, long enough to know where the spare batteries were, not long enough for everything to be forgiven in advance. She knew about Rachel in the broad biographical sense: married young, divorced at thirty-eight, no children, seven years apart before he met Isabel at a friend’s garden party where they both stood under the same inadequate umbrella.

Isabel had a past, too, of course. A man named Martin, who had been “kind but underwater,” as she put it; and before him someone called Celia, about whom she said almost nothing except that she had beautiful hands and terrible timing.

They were both in their forties. They had arrived in love with luggage.

The night he finally told her, rain rattled at the kitchen skylight like rice thrown at a wedding. Isabel was making soup, chopping carrots into bright coins. Daniel sat at the table with a stack of invoices he was pretending to read.

“You’re doing the thing,” she said.

“What thing?”

“The thing where you stare at paper as if it betrayed you.”

He put down the invoice. “I’ve been having these dreams.”

She slid the carrots into the pot. “Good dreams or administrative dreams?”

“Rachel dreams.”

The knife stopped. Only for a second. Then she laid it neatly beside the board. “Ah.”

He hated that small word. Not because it was hostile, but because it held itself carefully, like a cup filled to the brim.

“It’s not romantic,” he said quickly.

“I didn’t say it was.”

“No, I know. I just—she’s there, but it’s more about this flat.”

“A flat?”

He told her then. The corridor, the blue door, the keys, the white sheets, the bowl with the dead leaf. Rachel humming somewhere inside. Rachel saying, You found it.

Isabel listened without interruption, one hand resting on the counter, the other on her hip. The soup began to simmer behind her.

When he finished, she said, “Did you and Rachel ever have a flat like that?”

“No. We had the place in Tufnell Park with the terrible bathroom. Then the house in Walthamstow.”

“Was there somewhere you almost lived?”

Daniel shook his head, then stopped. “We did look at a flat once. Early on. Before we married. It had a blue door, maybe. Or maybe I’m inventing that because of the dream.”

“What happened?”

“It was too expensive. Or damp. Or both.” He frowned. “Rachel loved it. It had big windows. I said it was impractical.”

“And was it?”

“Almost certainly.”

Isabel smiled faintly. “The defense rests.”

He looked down at his hands. “I don’t know why I’m dreaming about her now.”

“Maybe you’re not,” Isabel said. “Maybe you’re dreaming about an unused room in your life and she happens to have the keys.”

He looked up. “That sounds very Rachel.”

“I contain multitudes.”

They ate soup at the kitchen table while rain blurred the dark glass above them. For a while they spoke about ordinary things: a leak in the bathroom ceiling, Isabel’s difficult client, Daniel’s sister’s birthday. But the dream remained between them, not like a wall, more like a third chair pulled up to the table.

Later, in bed, Isabel said, “Do you miss her?”

Daniel had known the question would come, and yet it still startled him. He turned on his side. Isabel’s face was half-lit by the streetlamp outside, her hair silvered at the edges.

“No,” he said, then reconsidered. “Not in the way you mean. I miss things sometimes. Our old cat. A café we used to go to. The version of myself who thought being right was a form of love.”

“That’s a very specific version.”

“He was insufferable.”

“I’ve met his cousin.”

Daniel laughed softly. “Fair.”

Isabel was quiet. Then she said, “I miss Martin’s garden sometimes.”

“Not Martin?”

“Sometimes Martin-in-the-garden. Not Martin-at-airports. Not Martin-after-two-drinks. But he grew tomatoes like a man apologizing to the earth.”

Daniel smiled into the pillow.

“And Celia?” he asked, before he could stop himself.

Isabel inhaled slowly. “Celia is more like weather.”

“I thought dreams were weather.”

“Some people are weather too.”

He waited.

“She made me feel awake,” Isabel said. “And also homeless. It’s a confusing combination.”

Daniel reached for her hand under the duvet. She let him take it.

“I don’t want to be a flat with a blue door,” she said after a while.

“You’re not.”

“Good.”

“You’re more of a converted boathouse.”

She turned her head and looked at him. “Is that meant to be flattering?”

“Very. Light. Water views. Structurally surprising.”

She laughed then, and the sound moved through him with a relief so strong it was almost pain.

After that, the dreams changed.

Rachel still appeared, but less often. Sometimes the flat was empty. Sometimes Daniel stood outside the blue door and found he had no keys. Once he was inside with Isabel, and she was opening all the windows.

In waking life, he and Isabel began talking more—not constantly, not theatrically, but in the small useful ways that had somehow thinned over the past year. They spoke while brushing their teeth, while unloading the dishwasher, while walking to the market on Saturdays. They told each other stories they had assumed the other already knew.

Daniel told her about the night Rachel threw a mug against the wall, not at him, she had emphasized, but near enough that the distinction had felt academic. He told her how he had once spent four hours assembling a wardrobe only to realize he had built it facing the wall, and Rachel had laughed until she cried, then cried until she locked herself in the bathroom.

Isabel told him Martin used to leave notes in library books. Not romantic notes—warnings, recipes, opinions about the ending. She told him Celia had once driven them to Wales at midnight because she wanted to hear rain on a caravan roof. “It was wonderful,” Isabel said. “It was also a Tuesday. I had a meeting at nine.”

They were not confessions exactly. More like excavations. Evidence of prior civilizations.

Then, in early April, Daniel found the keys.

They were not dream keys. There were no skeleton keys, no red plastic tag. Just three ordinary keys on a tarnished ring, wrapped in a receipt so faded it had become nearly blank. He found them at the back of a drawer in the hall while looking for picture hooks.

At first he assumed they belonged to nothing. Then he saw the small paper label attached to the ring, folded and soft with age. On it, in Rachel’s handwriting, were the words: Stoke Newington flat.

He sat down on the floor.

The hall was narrow and cluttered with shoes. Afternoon light fell through the glass panel above the door. From the kitchen, he could hear Isabel talking on the phone, using her work voice—warm, precise, almost musical.

He turned the keys over in his palm, and memory opened.

Not a dream this time. A Saturday morning fifteen years before. Rachel in a green scarf, hair pinned badly, impatient with the estate agent who was late. Daniel standing beside her outside a house divided into flats. The front door had been blue—yes, blue, but brighter than the dream door, almost cheerful.

The flat was on the third floor. It smelled of paint and dust and someone else’s shampoo. There were high windows, a sloping ceiling in the bedroom, and a sitting room with just enough space for a small table if they did not own too many chairs.

Rachel had stood in the middle of the room, arms folded, eyes shining.

“Imagine Sunday mornings here,” she said.

Daniel had imagined the service charge. The damp patch above the skirting board. The commute. The cost of repairing the roof. He had been twenty-nine and already practicing for middle age.

“It’s not sensible,” he said.

Rachel turned to the window. “Not everything has to be sensible.”

“No,” he said. “But homes do.”

The estate agent arrived, apologetic, jangling keys. They walked through the rooms again. Rachel lingered in the sitting room. Before they left, the agent gave them the spare keys by mistake, thinking they had put in an offer. Daniel discovered the error later and meant to return them, but the office closed, and then the week got busy, and then the flat was sold to someone else.

For years, apparently, the keys had followed him from drawer to box to drawer. Silent witnesses to a life not chosen.

Daniel closed his fist around them.

That evening he showed Isabel.

She held the keys carefully, as if they were archaeological finds. “So it was real.”

“Apparently.”

“Do you want to go there?”

The question made him nervous. “To the flat?”

“To the building. The street. Whatever’s there now.”

He thought about it. He expected longing, or fear, but what he felt was milder: curiosity with a bruise inside it.

“Yes,” he said. “Maybe.”

They went the following Sunday.

The street was leafier than he remembered. A bakery had replaced the laundrette on the corner. The blue front door was now black, glossy and severe. Someone had planted lavender in two square tubs by the steps. There were entry buzzers where the old bell had been.

They stood on the pavement, not touching.

“Do you feel anything?” Isabel asked.

Daniel looked up at the third-floor windows. One was open. A child’s drawing had been taped to the glass: a yellow sun, a lopsided house, three people holding hands.

“I feel,” he said slowly, “as if I’ve been sending letters to an address that no longer exists.”

Isabel nodded.

“I was afraid it meant I wanted something back,” he said. “But I think maybe I wanted to apologize to the person I was. Or to Rachel. Or to the rooms.”

“The rooms accept,” Isabel said solemnly.

He slipped the keys from his pocket. They were useless now. The locks had changed long ago, and perhaps the doors too. He imagined trying them anyway, the absurd click of metal against metal, the building refusing him politely.

Instead, he placed the keys in his palm and held them out to Isabel.

“What should I do with them?”

“Not give them to me,” she said. “I have enough symbolic objects.”

He laughed.

They walked to the park and sat by the water, where a group of people in red life jackets were learning to paddle canoes. The instructor stood on the bank shouting cheerful corrections.

“Left! Other left, Marcus! That’s the lake edge. Try not to court it.”

A canoe drifted sideways into the reeds. Its occupants, two women in wool hats, were laughing so hard they could not paddle.

Daniel watched the blade of a paddle dip into the water, pull, lift silver into the air.

“I used to canoe,” he said.

Isabel turned. “Did you?”

“At university. Badly. A friend and I took one down the Wye over a long weekend. It rained the whole time. We capsized twice. Best holiday I’d had until then.”

“I’ve never been.”

“You’d like it.”

“Would I?”

“You like things that look calm and then require coordination.”

“That is a devastatingly accurate description of my romantic history.”

He smiled. The lake flashed under a brief opening in the clouds.

“Maybe we should try,” he said.

“Canoeing?”

“Yes.”

“Is this because of the keys?”

“Probably. Indirectly.”

“Explain.”

He leaned back on his hands. “I keep thinking about homes. Rooms. Doors I didn’t open. Doors I closed badly. But maybe I’ve been imagining life as buildings too much.”

“And canoeing is not a building.”

“Very much not.”

“It’s wet, for one thing.”

“It’s movement,” he said. “You have to pay attention to where the other person is putting their weight.”

Isabel looked at him then, and he realized he had said something more serious than he intended.

“And if one person panics?” she asked.

“Then the other tries not to. Or they both get wet.”

She considered this. “I’m willing to get moderately wet.”

So the following Saturday they booked an introductory session at the reservoir.

The morning was cold and bright. Daniel wore an old fleece that smelled faintly of cupboard. Isabel arrived from the changing room in a borrowed waterproof jacket far too large for her, the sleeves covering her hands.

“I look like a lost fisherman,” she said.

“A distinguished one.”

Their instructor was a compact woman named Priya who had the patient authority of someone accustomed to preventing middle-aged professionals from drowning in shallow water. She taught them how to hold the paddles, how to enter the canoe without turning it into a slapstick routine, how to sit with their knees braced and their bodies loose.

“Loose but attentive,” Priya said.

“Story of my life,” Isabel murmured.

Daniel sat in the stern, Isabel in the bow. At first they moved in small, embarrassing circles. Daniel overcorrected. Isabel paddled too fast. The canoe nosed toward a buoy, then away, then toward it again with comic determination.

“You’re steering us into the buoy,” Isabel called.

“I’m attempting not to.”

“Your attempt has a strong resemblance to steering into it.”

They struck the buoy gently. It made a hollow plastic bonk.

From the bank, Priya shouted, “Communication!”

“We are communicating!” Isabel shouted back.

“Try communicating before impact.”

They laughed. Daniel felt the old, unfamiliar joy of incompetence shared without blame. The reservoir opened around them, gray-green and rippled, holding the sky in broken pieces. Other canoes passed at varying levels of dignity.

Gradually, they found a rhythm. Isabel’s paddle dipped left, Daniel’s right. The canoe slid forward. Not fast, not elegant, but forward. Daniel watched the back of Isabel’s hood, the small strands of hair escaping at her neck. He felt the boat respond to both of them: every shift, every hesitation, every recovered balance.

“Are you all right up there?” he asked.

“I’m deciding whether I trust you.”

“And?”

“So far I trust you on calm inland water under supervision.”

“I’ll take it.”

They paddled toward the far side, where trees leaned over the bank and the city seemed briefly to have misplaced them. A heron lifted from the reeds, slow and prehistoric. Isabel stopped paddling.

“Oh,” she said.

The canoe glided on in silence.

Daniel thought of the dream flat: the covered furniture, the bowl with the dead leaf, Rachel moving from room to room. He thought of the real flat, now belonging to strangers, the black door, the child’s drawing in the window. He thought of all the keys people carried after they no longer opened anything.

He had not thrown the old keys away. They were in his coat pocket now, wrapped again in their fading receipt. He did not know why he had brought them. Perhaps because letting go, like steering, was easier when done gradually.

“Daniel?” Isabel said.

“Yes?”

“We’re drifting.”

He looked up. They were indeed drifting, slowly but confidently, toward the reeds.

“Right,” he said. “Paddle left.”

“Your left or my left?”

“The shared left.”

“There is no shared left in a canoe.”

They sorted it out badly, then better. The canoe turned, water chuckling along its sides. Isabel began to paddle again, and Daniel matched her stroke.

On the way back, the wind rose. Small waves slapped the bow. Isabel whooped when cold spray hit her face.

“This is ridiculous,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Why is it nice?”

“I don’t know.”

But he did know, partly. It was nice because there was no room for ghosts in the canoe. No covered furniture, no unused rooms, no blue doors waiting to be opened. There was only water, balance, weather, and the person in front of him trusting him just enough to keep going.

When they reached the pontoon, Isabel climbed out first, wobbled, and grabbed his arm. For one alarming second they leaned together over the water, both laughing and nearly falling in.

“Loose but attentive,” Daniel said.

“Don’t quote Priya at me.”

On the bank, they returned their paddles and life jackets. Their jeans were damp. Their hands were cold. Isabel’s cheeks were flushed, and Daniel felt absurdly proud, as if they had crossed an ocean rather than circled a reservoir under supervision.

As they walked back to the car, he stopped by a rubbish bin.

Isabel noticed. “Are you sure?”

He took the keys from his pocket. They lay in his palm, ordinary and dull. He imagined Rachel at twenty-nine in the almost-flat, eyes bright with Sunday mornings. He imagined his younger self, already armored in practicality, unable to say that he was frightened of wanting something too much.

“I don’t think they belong to me anymore,” he said.

Then he dropped them into the bin.

The sound they made was small, nearly nothing.

Isabel did not speak. She slid her cold hand into his.

That night, Daniel dreamed of water.

Not a flood, not a storm. A wide quiet river under a pale sky. He was in a canoe, and someone was seated in front of him. He could not see who it was at first. The current carried them gently past trees, past houses, past a blue door standing alone on the bank like a prop from a play.

As they drew level with it, the door opened. Behind it was only sunlight.

Daniel looked down. There were no keys in his hand. Only a paddle.

“Ready?” Isabel called from the bow.

He dipped the blade into the river.

“Yes,” he said, and together they moved on.
