Daniel began to dream of the flat in late autumn, when the city had taken on the colour of wet cardboard and the evenings seemed to arrive before he had properly finished lunch.

In the dream he was always looking for the same door.

Sometimes it was at the end of a corridor in a block of council flats where he had never lived. Sometimes it was tucked behind the staircase of an old Georgian house, its brass number polished by hands that never opened it. Once, absurdly, it was halfway along the landing of his childhood home, between the bathroom and his parents’ bedroom, as if it had been there all along and only he had failed to notice.

The key was in his pocket. That, too, was always part of it. He could feel its particular shape through the lining of his coat: long, old-fashioned, slightly warm. He would take it out, lift it toward the lock, and then Rachel would appear.

Not dramatically. Not with thunder or accusation. She would simply be there, standing beside him with her hair cut as it had been in the last year of their marriage, too short for her face but somehow defiant, as if she had cut it that way to prove there were still things she could decide.

“Are you sure?” she would ask.

Then Daniel would wake.

He never got inside the flat. After three weeks of this, that fact irritated him more than the presence of Rachel. What was the point of a recurring dream if it wouldn’t recur properly? A person was entitled, at the very least, to development. He would lie in the dark beside Isabel, listening to the buses hiss on the road below, and feel cheated. A door, a key, an ex-wife. It was symbolism of the most basic kind. His brain, once capable of thinking in footnotes, had become a student essay.

He did not tell Isabel at first.

There was no reason not to tell her, which was precisely why he didn’t. It would sound larger than it was. Mention an ex-wife in bed at six in the morning and the name would not remain just a name; it would sit up, stretch, make itself coffee. Besides, Isabel had never been jealous of Rachel in any ordinary way. That made it harder. If she had been unreasonable, he could have reassured her. If she had asked sharp questions, he could have answered them and felt innocent. But Isabel’s interest in Rachel had always been calm, almost anthropological. She wanted to know the shape of what had come before her, not so that she could compete with it, but because she believed people were made of earlier weather.

Daniel disliked this view, though he could not deny its usefulness. He preferred to think of himself as a man who had learned, altered, emerged. Rachel belonged to another country. He had left it. There had been paperwork.

Still the dream continued.

By November the unused flat had begun to affect his waking hours. Passing an estate agent’s window, he found himself stopping before photographs of empty rooms: staged sofas, bowls of fake lemons, light falling democratically across laminated floors. At work, while a colleague explained a delay in the audit, Daniel pictured a kitchen with no cups in the cupboards, a bed with no indentation in the mattress, dust gathering in the silence of an unopened life.

What would be inside? In the dream he expected something, though he never knew what. Evidence, perhaps. Of what? Waste. Cowardice. Some version of himself that had been waiting patiently behind the door, surviving without him.

One Saturday morning Isabel found him in the hallway holding his old winter coat.

“That’s a tragic expression,” she said. “Has it betrayed you?”

He looked down. He had been feeling in the pockets again, though he had no idea what he expected to find. Receipts, tissues, a mint fossilised in its wrapper. Not the key; of course not the key.

“I keep having a dream,” he said.

Isabel, who had been tying her hair up with a pencil because she could never find clips when she wanted them, paused with both arms raised. “What kind of dream?”

He considered making it funny. There was a version in which he said he was being chased by enormous invoices. Another in which Rachel became “someone from the past” and the flat became “a room.” But Isabel was looking at him in that direct way she had, not demanding seriousness but leaving space for it, which was worse.

“There’s a flat,” he said. “I’m outside it. I’ve got the key, but I never go in.”

“Why not?”

“Rachel’s there.”

“Ah.”

“Not like that,” he said too quickly.

Isabel lowered her arms. The pencil remained crooked in her hair. “I didn’t say it was.”

“I mean, it’s not romantic.”

“Dreams don’t usually file their paperwork.”

He laughed, relieved and annoyed by the relief. “She just asks if I’m sure.”

“Sure about what?”

“That’s the thing. I don’t know.”

They went into the kitchen. Isabel put the kettle on, and Daniel sat at the table, which was scarred with the evidence of their shared life: a burn mark from a pan he had once set down too quickly, a faint blue streak from when Isabel had addressed envelopes with a leaking pen, three circular ghosts of plant pots from her basil experiments. Not unused, then. Thoroughly used. Proof against the dream.

Isabel leaned against the counter. “What does the flat feel like?”

“What does it feel like?”

“Yes. Is it frightening? Tempting? Sad?”

Daniel disliked questions that sounded therapeutic, except when Isabel asked them, because she asked as though she genuinely needed the answer and not as though she already knew it.

“Wasteful,” he said at last. “Like it’s been paid for and never lived in.”

She nodded. “That is sad.”

He had expected—what? A joke, perhaps. Or a neat pronouncement about unfinished business. Instead she poured tea into two mugs and looked out at the rain.

“After Tom left,” she said, “I used to dream about supermarkets.”

Daniel knew about Tom, Isabel’s former almost-husband, though she rarely spoke of him. He had been a sculptor of large, impractical objects and small, practical cruelties. Their separation had been long and theatrical, featuring reclaimed furniture, mutual friends, and one disputed cat.

“Supermarkets?”

“Always at night. Bright lights, no staff, shelves full of things I didn’t want. I had a trolley, and I knew I had to buy something, but I couldn’t choose.” She smiled into her mug. “Very subtle, my subconscious.”

“What happened?”

“In the dreams? Nothing. I wandered around until I woke up furious.”

“With Tom?”

“With myself, mostly. For spending so long trying to want what was available.”

Daniel sat with that. Outside, rain tapped at the window in fussy, irregular bursts. He imagined Isabel younger, moving under fluorescent lights between aisles of cereal and washing powder, pushing an empty trolley through abundance and feeling starved. The thought made him tender toward her in a way that also made him ashamed. How easy it was to know the headline of another person’s pain and mistake it for having read the article.

“Did you ever tell him?” Daniel asked.

“Tom? God, no. He would have made a sculpture called Aisle Seven.”

Daniel laughed properly then, and Isabel did too. The pencil fell out of her hair and clattered on the floor.

After that, the dream changed, but only slightly. Rachel still appeared. She still asked if he was sure. But now Daniel sometimes answered.

“No,” he said once.

Rachel looked unsurprised. “Then why are you here?”

He woke before he could reply.

He began thinking more about the last flat he and Rachel had shared: a narrow place above a dry cleaner’s in Highbury, where the radiators clanked and the bedroom smelled faintly of steam and chemicals. They had painted the sitting room yellow, a decision that seemed cheerful in theory and jaundiced in practice. Rachel had hated it almost immediately. Daniel had insisted it would improve with curtains. Curtains: as if fabric could rescue colour, marriage, weather.

Their worst arguments had happened there, though not the loudest. The loudest had occurred earlier, when both of them still believed volume might alter facts. In the yellow room they had become quieter and more skilled. Rachel could ask, “Will you be late again?” in a voice so level it entered him like a blade. He could answer, “I said I’d try,” and make of those four words a locked gate.

He had loved her. This remained inconveniently true. Even at the end, when love had become mostly memory and obligation, there had been flashes of the old thing: her laughing with her mouth full of toast, her hand on his neck in a crowd, her habit of reading the last page of a novel first because life had enough suspense. But they had exhausted each other. No villain, no grand betrayal. Just two people who had mistaken endurance for devotion until endurance itself rebelled.

Was Rachel the flat, then? Ridiculous. Too simple. And yet the dream-Rachel did not seem to want him back. She seemed to want him accurate.

One evening in December, Daniel and Isabel went to dinner with his friend Mark, who had recently separated from his wife and spoke of it with the stunned politeness of someone describing a minor traffic incident in which all passengers had died.

“It’s the stuff,” Mark said, turning his wineglass by the stem. “That’s what no one tells you. Not feelings. Stuff. Who gets the blender. Why do we own three extension leads? What am I supposed to do with half a tent?”

Isabel said, “What kind of tent?”

“A bad one.”

“Then you burn it ceremonially,” she said.

Mark looked grateful, as if this might be legally binding.

On the way home, Daniel thought about possessions outliving intentions. Wedding glasses in cupboards. Spare keys in drawers. Canoe paddles in sheds.

The paddles came to him so abruptly that he stopped walking.

Isabel, two paces ahead, turned. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“No one stops in the middle of the pavement for nothing unless they’ve remembered a murder.”

“We used to canoe,” he said.

“You and Rachel?”

“Years ago. Before we were married, actually. There was a place in Norfolk. We hired this terrible green canoe and nearly capsized every ten minutes.”

He could see it now: Rachel at the front, shouting instructions over her shoulder though she had no greater knowledge than he did; reeds whispering on both sides; a heron lifting itself out of the shallows with prehistoric disdain. They had paddled badly and laughed until the boat rocked with it. Later they had eaten chips from paper by the river, sunburned and damp, and Rachel had said, “We should be people who do this.” He had agreed. Yes, absolutely. They would be river people, outdoor people, people with thermoses and maps in waterproof sleeves.

They had gone twice more. Then work became busy, then winter came, then the idea of themselves as people who did this was packed away with the buoyancy aids.

“I didn’t know that,” Isabel said.

“No. I’d forgotten.”

And he had. Or had chosen not to remember, which was a more active form of forgetting and therefore more shameful.

A week before Christmas, they cleared the hall cupboard.

This was Isabel’s idea. She had a theory that domestic order could encourage spiritual renewal. Daniel had a theory that cupboards existed to protect human beings from the consequences of ownership. Nevertheless, he knelt among old umbrellas and reusable bags while Isabel sorted through a box of cables with the grave attention of an archaeologist.

At the back of the cupboard, beneath a collapsed backpack, he found a small tin printed with faded blue flowers. His mother had once used it for biscuits. Now it rattled.

“What’s that?” Isabel asked.

“Probably screws.”

Inside were keys.

Not one key, but many: keys with coloured caps, tiny padlock keys, a car key from a vehicle he no longer owned, two Yale keys he could not place. And beneath them, attached to a cork float shaped like a fish, was a long brass key darkened with age.

Daniel sat back on his heels.

The key was not the dream key. Of course it was not. It was smaller, flatter, entirely ordinary. But his body recognised it before his mind did: the slight roughness at the teeth, the ridiculous cork fish, orange once and now nearly brown.

“What is it?” Isabel said.

He lifted it from the tin. “Boat shed.”

The words opened something.

Not a flat. Not Rachel at a threshold. A shed by the river in Norfolk, rented with the holiday cottage for one bright week when he and Rachel were twenty-eight and still believed adulthood could be arranged around pleasure if only one were firm enough. The shed had contained two warped deckchairs, a rusting barbecue, and the green canoe. The owner had given them the key with exaggerated solemnity. “For your vessel,” he’d said, and Rachel had saluted.

Daniel had kept it by accident. After the holiday, after the return to London, after the wedding and the yellow room and the long diminishing, the key had travelled with him. From drawer to box, box to cupboard, cupboard to this kitchen floor. An unused permission.

He could smell the river then: mud, weed, sun on old wood. He could feel the ache in his shoulders after paddling, the pleasant uselessness of his office hands. Rachel turning around in the canoe, her sunglasses crooked, saying, “Daniel, if you steer us into those nettles, I will divorce you before we’re married.”

He laughed, but it came out strangely.

Isabel moved closer, sitting cross-legged among the bags. “Tell me.”

So he did. Not all of it at first, then more. The canoe, the chips, Rachel’s wet hair, the plan to become different kinds of people. How they had failed not in one dramatic break but through innumerable postponements. How sad it was, really, to have been happy and not understood that happiness required maintenance. Not worship, not panic. Just maintenance. A rinsed cup. A returned call. A Saturday protected from errands. A canoe taken out more than three times.

Isabel listened without rescuing him from himself. That was one of her sternest kindnesses.

When he finished, she picked up the cork fish and turned it in her hand. “Maybe the flat is a place where you stored all the lives you meant to live.”

He wanted to resist this. Too neat again. But the sentence settled in him with the quiet authority of something almost true.

“With Rachel?” he asked.

“With Rachel. Without Rachel. Before me. With me.” She looked at him. “We all have them, don’t we? Little locked rooms full of better intentions.”

Daniel thought of the flat in the dream, unfurnished or overfilled, waiting behind its door. He thought of Rachel asking if he was sure. Sure enough to enter? Sure enough to leave? Sure enough to admit that an unlived life could haunt you without wanting to be lived now?

“I don’t want a locked room with you,” he said.

Isabel smiled, but not triumphantly. “Then we’ll need to be careful. Rooms lock themselves when you ignore them.”

On Boxing Day, Daniel booked a canoe.

It was absurdly easy. That was almost insulting. Years of not doing a thing, and then a website accepted his card in under two minutes. No thunder, no permission from the dead, no certificate of readiness. He booked a two-hour hire on a slow river outside the city for the first Saturday of January and presented it to Isabel as if it were both gift and apology.

“We may freeze,” she said.

“Possibly.”

“We may argue about steering.”

“Almost certainly.”

She studied the confirmation email. “Good.”

The morning was cold enough to make the air glitter. At the boathouse, a teenager in fingerless gloves handed them buoyancy aids and gave instructions in a tone suggesting he had already watched many adults overestimate themselves. Isabel looked magnificent and ridiculous in her orange vest, her cheeks pink, her hair escaping her hat. Daniel felt suddenly shy. This was not a date exactly, nor a ritual, though it borrowed from both. It was only canoeing. It was everything he could manage.

They pushed off badly. The canoe drifted sideways. Isabel, seated in front, said, “Are you steering?”

“I thought I was.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

A duck observed them with contempt.

They found a rhythm eventually. Not a graceful one, but serviceable. Paddle left, pull, lift, drip. The river was brown and patient. Bare trees leaned over the banks, their branches scribbling on the pale sky. Somewhere a dog barked with insane commitment.

Daniel watched the back of Isabel’s hat move with each stroke. Here she was: not a symbol, not a second chance in the cheap sense, not proof that he had become wiser. A woman with cold hands who had chosen to spend her Saturday in a rented canoe with a man who sometimes dreamed of his ex-wife. How strange and generous the present was, how easily insulted by inattention.

“Daniel?” she said.

“Yes?”

“Are we about to hit that branch?”

He corrected too late. The canoe brushed through low twigs, showering them with icy drops. Isabel shrieked, then laughed, and the sound flew back to him over the water.

For a moment the years folded—not collapsed, not erased, but folded like a map. Rachel laughing in a green canoe; Isabel laughing now; Daniel younger, Daniel older; all the rivers he had entered and left. He felt no disloyalty in it. Memory was not a country one betrayed by living elsewhere. It was more like water under the boat, bearing them whether seen or not.

That night he dreamed of the flat again.

The corridor was dim, the key warm in his hand. Rachel stood beside the door.

“Are you sure?” she asked.

Daniel looked at her. She was neither young nor old, neither forgiving nor wounded. Only Rachel, or the part of him that had borrowed her face.

“No,” he said. “But I’m going in.”

He put the key in the lock. It turned easily.

Inside was not a flat. There were no covered chairs, no accusing dust, no alternate Daniel waiting in the gloom. There was a room full of light, and in the middle of it a river, impossible and narrow, passing silently through the floorboards. On the far bank stood Isabel, holding two paddles.

“Well?” she called.

Daniel stepped inside, and this time he did not wake until morning.
