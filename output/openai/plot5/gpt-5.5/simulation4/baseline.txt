Daniel began dreaming of the flat in February, when the rain came down in long silver threads and the mornings smelled of damp wool and old leaves.

It was always the same flat: second floor, narrow stairwell, a brass number on a green door—though the number changed each time, sliding from 6 to 14 to 3B as if the place could not decide where in the world it belonged. In the dream Daniel had keys in his hand. He knew, with that deep, unreasonable certainty dreams allow, that the flat was his. Not rented, not borrowed, not waiting for a tenant. His.

And yet he had never used it.

Inside, the rooms were empty but not abandoned. Pale rectangles on the walls showed where pictures might have hung. A curtain moved softly in a room with no open window. The kitchen cupboards were bare. The bedroom contained only a folded blue blanket on the floor, and sometimes, in the dream, he could hear Rachel humming somewhere behind a closed door.

His ex-wife had not hummed in real life, at least not often. She had been a whistler. She used to whistle tuneless fragments while chopping onions, checking emails, tying the laces of her running shoes. Daniel had found it charming for the first two years, irritating in the third, and heartbreaking after she left.

In the dreams, though, she hummed, low and tender, like a person soothing a child.

He would stand in the hallway, keys biting into his palm, knowing he should call out to her and knowing with equal force that he should not. Then he would wake beside Isabel, his current partner, in their bedroom with the leaning bookcase and the laundry chair and the small crack in the ceiling shaped like a river delta.

For several weeks he said nothing.

Isabel was an observant sleeper. She noticed disturbances the way a cat notices weather. A shift of breath, a hand pulling away, the sudden stiffness of a body pretending to rest.

“You’ve been somewhere else lately,” she said one morning.

Daniel was buttering toast. The knife paused. “Have I?”

“That’s not a denial.”

“I’m tired.”

“You can be tired here,” she said. “You don’t have to travel for it.”

He smiled despite himself, but she didn’t smile back. Isabel had a face that could become very still without becoming hard. It was one of the first things he had loved about her: the calm attention she gave to a conversation, as if words were objects being placed carefully on a table between them.

They had been together for four years. Long enough to know the sounds of each other moving through a room. Not long enough, Daniel sometimes thought, to stop wondering what histories were being left politely untouched.

He put down the knife. “I’ve been having dreams.”

“About what?”

“A flat.”

“A flat?”

“One I own. Or think I own. But I’ve never used it.” He looked toward the window, where rain striped the glass. “Rachel is there, sometimes. Or not there. I hear her.”

Isabel took this in. She lifted her mug, held it near her mouth, then set it down without drinking. “How long?”

“A few weeks.”

“And you didn’t tell me because…”

Because I was ashamed, he thought. Because I didn’t want you to think I was still in love with her. Because maybe part of me is not in love with her but in love with the version of myself who was young enough to believe that love, once made legal and public, became permanent.

Aloud he said, “I didn’t know what it meant.”

Isabel nodded. “Do you now?”

“No.”

She looked at him for a while. “Tell me about the flat.”

So he did.

He described the green door and the changing numbers, the empty rooms, the folded blue blanket. He told her about the humming. He told her that in the dream he was always about to open a door but woke before he did. As he spoke, the dream seemed both more foolish and more important. The flat acquired corners, smells, light. Isabel asked practical questions: Did it feel like somewhere he’d lived? Was it in London? Was it frightening?

“No,” he said. “That’s the thing. It feels sad. But not frightening.”

“And Rachel?”

“She doesn’t speak.”

“Did you want her to?”

He considered lying, but the morning had gone too quiet for it.

“Yes,” he said.

Isabel looked down at her hands. She had beautiful hands, square-palmed, capable, often ink-smudged from the school where she taught art. “When I first moved in with Mark,” she said, “I used to dream about my old bedroom at my mother’s house. Not the one I actually had. A better one. Bigger. Yellow curtains. A desk by the window. In the dream I had left something there, and I was always trying to go back for it.”

Daniel knew some things about Mark, Isabel’s former partner: a photographer, charming in public, carelessly cruel in private. He had not hit her; Daniel knew this because people always seemed to need that clarified before deciding how sorry to be. Mark had instead specialized in erosion. He misplaced her confidence, then accused her of being forgetful. He laughed at her paintings in front of friends. He borrowed money and repaid it with apologies so elaborate they became gifts she was expected to accept.

“What was it?” Daniel asked. “The thing you left?”

“I never found out.” She smiled faintly. “Maybe a spine.”

He reached across the table. She let him take her hand.

After that, the dreams became a subject between them, not a secret. They discussed them in bed, on walks to the shops, while cooking. Sometimes they were gentle with each other; sometimes not.

“Do you miss Rachel?” Isabel asked one evening, chopping carrots with unnecessary force.

“I miss being certain,” Daniel said.

“About her?”

“About anything.”

The knife stopped. “That’s worse, you know.”

“I know.”

Another night he asked Isabel whether she still thought about Mark.

“Of course,” she said. “The way you think about a place where you broke your arm. You don’t want to live there, but you remember the pavement.”

They both laughed, and then, unexpectedly, Isabel cried. Daniel held her awkwardly at first, then less awkwardly. He realized he had spent years trying not to be like Mark or like Rachel—who had left with a careful kindness that somehow made abandonment feel well organized—without always noticing who Isabel actually was beside him.

Spring arrived uncertainly. The rain thinned. Crocuses appeared under the plane trees near the station. Daniel’s dreams continued, but changed. In one, he found dust on the windowsill and wrote his name in it, only for the letters to rearrange themselves into Rachel’s. In another, he opened the kitchen cupboard and found a pair of canoe paddles leaning upright in the darkness, wet and shining.

He woke from that one with a sharp tug of memory, then lost it.

The real discovery came on a Saturday in April, during an argument about storage.

The cupboard under the stairs had become a graveyard for things neither of them wanted to claim: paint tins, old chargers, a broken lamp, Daniel’s box of paperwork from the flat he had shared with Rachel, still sealed with brown tape from the move after their divorce. Isabel wanted shelves. Daniel wanted, vaguely, for the cupboard door to close and for life not to require archaeology.

“You can’t keep entire eras in cardboard,” Isabel said.

“I’m not keeping an era. It’s bank statements.”

“From 2011.”

“Historically significant bank statements.”

She gave him a look.

So they pulled everything out. Dust rose. The broken lamp proved more broken than remembered. An umbrella contained a spider of impressive confidence. At the back, behind the paperwork box, Daniel found a small ceramic bowl wrapped in newspaper. Inside lay three keys on a rusted ring, tied with a faded blue ribbon.

For a moment, the house withdrew around him.

The keys were ordinary: two Yale, one small silver one, perhaps for a shed or padlock. But the ribbon—blue, cheap satin, fraying at the ends—opened a door in him so suddenly that he had to sit on the bottom stair.

“Daniel?” Isabel said.

He held up the keys. “I know these.”

She sat beside him, dust on her jeans. She did not take the keys from him.

“They’re from the boat club,” he said slowly. “Years ago. Before Rachel and I got married.”

“You were in a boat club?”

“Not exactly. We rented a canoe from a place by the river. Outside Oxford. We went one weekend because Rachel had this idea that we should become outdoorsy people.” He laughed once. “We were terrible. Truly terrible.”

The memory came not as a picture but as weather: sunlight broken on water, the smell of river mud, Rachel in a red waterproof jacket, swearing because the canoe would not go straight. They had paddled in circles for twenty minutes while ducks observed with cold judgment. Daniel had overcorrected, Rachel had accused him of “steering like a philosopher,” and then they had both laughed so hard the canoe rocked dangerously.

Later they found a quiet stretch where willows trailed their fingers in the water. They had stopped trying to be good at it. The canoe drifted. Rachel took off her shoes and put her feet up on the thwart. Daniel remembered the silver key opening the padlock where they had stored the paddles overnight, because they had decided, extravagantly, to rent the canoe for the whole weekend.

“We kept the keys by accident,” he said. “I meant to post them back.”

“Did you?”

“I suppose not.”

“What happened?”

He turned the key ring over in his palm. The blue ribbon had been Rachel’s, tied there so they wouldn’t lose them. “Nothing happened. That’s what’s strange. It was just a good weekend. Before everything became… annotated.”

Isabel leaned her shoulder against the banister. “Annotated?”

“You know. Before every memory had a footnote explaining how it all went wrong.”

She was quiet.

Daniel looked at the keys and understood something about the dream—not fully, not in the satisfying way stories pretended understanding came, but enough. The unused flat was not Rachel. Not exactly. It was a room in himself he had locked after the divorce, then avoided. Inside were not only grief and failure, but ordinary happiness, ridiculousness, sunlight, the version of him who had once been able to drift without immediately checking for leaks.

“I think,” he said, “I’ve been treating my past like it’s contaminated.”

Isabel’s mouth softened. “Isn’t some of it?”

“Yes. But not all.”

She nodded, and after a moment said, “I think I did that too. With Mark. I threw away a green dress because he once said I looked provincial in it. I loved that dress. It had pockets.”

“Tragic.”

“Deeply.”

He smiled. She smiled back.

They spent the rest of the afternoon sorting. They threw away the bank statements, recycled instruction manuals for appliances they no longer owned, and kept a few photographs neither of them felt ready to display but no longer wanted to bury. Daniel placed the boat keys on the kitchen table. They sat there all week, small and accusatory.

On Thursday, he searched online and discovered that the boat club still existed, though under new management, with a website featuring cheerful people in buoyancy aids. On impulse, he emailed them an apology for keys kept nearly fifteen years and asked if he might return them. He did not mention the dreams.

The reply came from someone named Moira, who wrote: *Goodness, those locks are long gone, but bring them if you like. We’ll add them to the drawer of mysterious river relics.*

Daniel read the email to Isabel.

“A drawer of mysterious river relics,” she said. “That’s where all our personalities should go.”

“Do you want to come with me?”

“To return stolen property?”

“To canoe.”

She looked at him over her glasses. “Have you forgotten the part where I don’t like water?”

“You like baths.”

“Famously not the same.”

“We don’t have to.”

She studied him, then the keys on the table. “Do you want to go because of Rachel?”

“Yes,” he said, then added, “But not to be with Rachel.”

“That is a very Daniel answer.”

“I mean I want to take something back from the memory. Or give something back to it. I’m not sure.”

Isabel removed her glasses and folded them. “If we capsize, I’m leaving you for a man with a steady job on land.”

“Fair.”

They went the following Sunday.

The boat club stood at the end of a lane bordered by nettles and cow parsley. The river was brown-green and patient, sliding past with the indifference of something that had carried kings, leaves, beer cans, wedding rings, and the occasional beginner in a hired canoe.

Moira turned out to be a wiry woman with cropped white hair and a laugh that startled nearby pigeons. She accepted the keys ceremoniously.

“Ah, vintage guilt,” she said, dropping them into a tin behind the counter. “We get all sorts.”

Daniel waited for a pang when the keys left his hand, but felt instead a curious lightness, as if he had been carrying not metal but an unfinished sentence.

They rented a canoe for two hours. Isabel put on her buoyancy aid with the expression of someone preparing to defuse a bomb.

“If I die,” she said, “delete my search history and burn the cupboard.”

“You’re not going to die.”

“You don’t know that. You steer like a philosopher.”

Daniel froze.

“What?” Isabel said.

“Nothing.” He laughed. “Rachel once said exactly that.”

“Oh.” Her face changed, just slightly. “Sorry.”

“No. Don’t be.” He pushed the canoe gently from the bank. “It’s a good line.”

They got in with only minor panic. For the first ten minutes they zigzagged wildly, the canoe nosing toward reeds, then spinning toward open water. Isabel accused Daniel of sabotage; Daniel accused the river of bias. A swan regarded them with aristocratic contempt.

Then, gradually, they found a rhythm. Not a perfect one. Daniel paddled too hard on the left; Isabel forgot to switch sides; now and then their paddles knocked together with a hollow clack. But the canoe moved forward.

The river widened. Trees leaned over the banks, new leaves bright as wet paint. Somewhere beyond the fields a dog barked. Isabel’s shoulders lowered. Daniel watched the dip and lift of her paddle, the small furrow that appeared between her eyebrows when she concentrated, the fleck of mud on her cheek.

“This is almost pleasant,” she admitted.

“Careful.”

“I said almost.”

They drifted under a low bridge where the air turned cool and echoing. Daniel let his paddle rest across his knees. For a moment, he thought of Rachel—not as a ghost in an unused flat, not as a closed door, but as a young woman in a red jacket laughing so hard she nearly tipped them both into the river. He felt affection, sadness, gratitude, and regret, all braided together but no longer tightening around his throat.

Then the canoe bumped the bank.

“Philosopher,” Isabel said.

“Sorry.”

She dipped her paddle and pushed them away. “Tell me something good about her.”

He looked at Isabel. “Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

He considered. “She was brave about beginnings. New cities, new food, strangers at parties. She could step into things.”

Isabel nodded. “That is good.”

“Tell me something good about Mark.”

She groaned. “Must I?”

“No.”

She watched the water for a while. “He taught me how to look at light. Before he made everything about himself, he really did see things. Shadows. Reflections. The color of late afternoon on brick.” She hesitated. “I hated that he gave me that, because I wanted to hate all of him.”

“Do you still?”

“Not all. Enough.”

Daniel smiled. “Enough is healthy.”

They paddled on.

Near the halfway point, they found a small inlet sheltered by willows. Daniel felt the old memory rise, but it no longer replaced the present. It overlapped it, translucent. Rachel’s bare feet on the thwart; Isabel’s hands gripping a paddle. The past did not vanish. It made room.

“Can we stop here?” Isabel asked.

They let the canoe drift among the willow shadows. Isabel leaned back carefully, testing the boat’s balance. “I understand your dream, I think.”

“Do you?”

“The flat is the life you didn’t have.”

Daniel looked at the water. “Maybe.”

“Or the life you think you wasted.”

“That’s cheerful.”

“I’m an artist. We specialize in cheerful.” She nudged his shoe with hers. “But it was unused, wasn’t it? Not ruined. Just unused.”

He thought of the empty rooms, the folded blue blanket, the humming behind the door. “What do I do with that?”

“Stop visiting it alone.”

The answer was so simple that he almost dismissed it. Instead he sat with it. A moorhen called from the reeds. Sunlight moved over Isabel’s face, broken by leaves.

That night, the dream came again.

The green door stood at the top of the stairs, brass number shining: 2. Daniel held the keys, but they were not the boat keys now, nor any keys he recognized. He opened the door.

The flat was still empty. Pale walls, bare cupboards, blue blanket folded on the bedroom floor. From behind the closed door came the humming. He walked toward it, and this time he did not feel afraid of waking.

He opened the door.

Inside was not Rachel but a room full of light. Dust turned slowly in the air. On the floor lay two paddles, crossed like an old emblem. The window was open, and beyond it he could see the river, moving steadily under a spring sky.

When Daniel woke, Isabel was still asleep beside him, one hand curled near her mouth. The crack in the ceiling looked less like a river delta now and more like a map of tributaries, all the small waters meeting.

He did not wake her. Not yet.

Instead he lay quietly in the early light and listened to the house: the pipes ticking, a car passing outside, Isabel breathing. Present things. Familiar things. Not permanent, perhaps. Nothing was. But real, and near, and asking to be entered.

Later, when she woke, he would tell her about the dream. They would make coffee. They would argue mildly about whose turn it was to buy milk. Perhaps next weekend they would canoe again, or perhaps never; perhaps canoeing would become one of those enthusiasms that lasted exactly two Sundays and lived forever afterward as a joke.

It didn’t matter.

The river had been enough. The keys had been enough. Isabel, beside him, was enough—not as a cure for the past, not as proof that nothing had been lost, but as a person with her own locked rooms, her own relics, her own difficult light.

Daniel reached under the duvet and found her hand.

Still half asleep, she squeezed back.
