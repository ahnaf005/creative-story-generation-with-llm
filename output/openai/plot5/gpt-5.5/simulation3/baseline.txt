Daniel dreamed of the flat again on a Tuesday night, after rain had been ticking at the bedroom window for hours and the gutters spoke in their wet, uncertain language.

In the dream he was always alone at first. He stood on a landing in a block of flats he almost recognized, with a paper bag in one hand and a ring of keys in the other. The hallway smelled of plaster dust, old carpet, and cabbage. Somewhere below, a door clicked shut; somewhere above, a television laughed.

The flat was on the third floor. Number 17.

He knew this before looking at the door.

The key was long and brass, with a round head worn smooth by other people’s thumbs. It slid into the lock easily, too easily, as if the door had been waiting. Inside, the flat was empty but not abandoned. The walls were newly painted a pale yellow. There were curtain rods but no curtains, light fixtures but no bulbs. In the kitchen, the cabinets were open, revealing clean shelves and nothing else. In the sitting room, the windows looked onto a canal lined with poplars, their leaves shining silver in the wind.

And in the center of the bare floor stood Rachel.

Not as she had been at the end—thin with exhaustion, hair pinned up carelessly, her voice sharp from all the things they had failed to say—but as she had been when he first loved her. She wore the green coat he’d bought her at a market in York, the one with a missing button she never replaced. Her hair was loose. She looked at him with something like patience.

“You forgot,” she said.

“What?”

“The flat.”

Then Daniel would look down at the paper bag in his hand, and inside it there would be two mugs, wrapped in newspaper. He knew, with the certainty peculiar to dreams, that they had meant to move in here. Or that they had already lived here. Or that it was a place they had chosen and then abandoned before it could become part of their history.

“I don’t understand,” he always said.

Rachel would turn toward the window. “No,” she said. “You never did.”

That was usually when he woke.

This time, he woke with his heart pounding and Isabel’s hand resting lightly on his shoulder.

“Dan?” she murmured. “You were talking.”

He lay still, listening to the rain. Their room was dark except for the pale strip of light under the door from the hallway. Isabel’s breathing slowed beside him, but he knew she was awake.

“What did I say?”

“Something about a key.” She paused. “And Rachel.”

He closed his eyes.

For months, perhaps longer, he had kept the dreams to himself. Not because he believed dreams were betrayals, but because speaking them aloud gave them shape. He and Isabel had been together nearly four years; they had survived each other’s cautiousness, the death of her mother, his father’s stroke, a bad winter of money worries, and the ordinary erosion of working too much. They had learned how to occupy the same kitchen in the morning without irritation. They knew each other’s preferred silences.

Still, Rachel had remained a room in him with the door closed.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

Isabel propped herself on one elbow. In the darkness he could not read her face. “You don’t have to apologize for dreaming.”

“I keep having the same one.”

“About her?”

“About a flat,” he said. “She’s in it.”

The mattress shifted as Isabel sat up. “Tell me.”

So he did.

He described the landing, the brass key, the empty rooms, the canal beyond the window. He told her about Rachel standing there in the green coat, accusing him of forgetting something he could not remember ever having. As he spoke, the dream seemed absurd, almost embarrassing. A flat that did not exist. A woman who had left him eight years ago. Mugs in a paper bag.

When he finished, Isabel was quiet.

“Well,” she said at last, “it sounds very organized for a nightmare.”

He laughed despite himself. “It’s not frightening.”

“No. Just… furnished with guilt.”

He turned his head toward her. “Is that what you think?”

“I think most dreams are furnished with whatever we’ve got in storage.”

The rain strengthened, then softened again. In the distance, a car hissed along the road.

“I don’t want you to feel—”

“Jealous?” Isabel asked. “I don’t, exactly. Not of a dream version of your ex-wife wearing a symbolic coat.”

“It was a real coat.”

“That makes it worse, obviously.”

He smiled. Then, after a moment, he said, “I think I did forget things.”

“We all do.”

“No. I mean with Rachel. Important things. Not anniversaries or shopping lists. I forgot to notice when we stopped being happy. I forgot to ask the right questions. I forgot that leaving someone alone can look a lot like giving them space.”

Isabel drew her knees to her chest under the duvet. “Did she say that?”

“Not in those words.”

“But you heard it anyway.”

Daniel stared at the ceiling. “At the end, everything she said sounded like an accusation. Maybe because I knew she was right.”

“Or because you were both exhausted.”

He could hear an old tenderness in Isabel’s voice, the tone she used when discussing people who had tried and failed without meaning to. It was one of the things he loved about her.

“What about you?” he asked.

“What about me?”

“Do you dream about Martin?”

Isabel sighed, not unhappily. Martin had been her almost-husband, a photographer with beautiful hands and a talent for leaving rooms emotionally untidied. He appeared now and then in conversation, usually attached to some logistical catastrophe or minor revelation.

“Sometimes,” she said. “Not for a while.”

“What happens?”

“We’re usually in a supermarket. He’s putting things in the trolley that we don’t need. Olives. Light bulbs. A melon in winter. And I’m trying to explain we already have all that at home, but he can’t hear me.”

“That sounds like Martin.”

“It was Martin.” She lay down again, facing him. “The thing is, I used to think the dream meant I missed him. Then one day I realized I was just still angry about all the things I carried while he wandered around choosing fruit.”

Daniel laughed softly.

“I loved him,” she said. “I really did. But when I remember him, I often remember myself more clearly. How tired I was. How determined to be reasonable. How proud of myself for needing so little.”

He reached for her hand under the duvet. “You don’t need little.”

“No,” she said. “I’ve become quite demanding. I require tea in the morning and emotional availability at least three times a week.”

“I’ll put it on the calendar.”

“Please do.”

They fell asleep holding hands, but the dream followed Daniel into the next night and the next. Sometimes Rachel said the same thing—You forgot. Sometimes she said nothing. Once he arrived at the flat and found only the green coat hanging from a nail by the window, moving slightly as if someone had just passed through it.

Talking about it changed something, though. The dream became less like a secret and more like a weather report. Over breakfast Isabel would ask, “Any property developments?” and Daniel would say, “The kitchen has acquired a tap,” or, “Rachel has upgraded to looking disappointed in the hallway.” They began, strangely, to talk more freely about the dead ends behind them.

Daniel told Isabel about the rented house he and Rachel had shared in Leeds, where the boiler broke every January and they had painted the bedroom dark blue because Rachel said sleep should feel like falling into the sea. He told her about the year they tried to be people who entertained, bought too many wineglasses, and discovered they both hated hosting. He told her about their last holiday, in Northumberland, when they walked along a beach in separate moods while a storm gathered over the water.

Isabel told him about Martin’s habit of turning every disagreement into a discussion about tone, until she no longer knew what she was trying to say. She told him about the red shoes she had bought the week after he left, not because she needed them, but because he had always disliked bright colors. She told him about the enormous relief of spending a Sunday alone and realizing no one would arrive late, cheerful and apologetic, to ruin it.

Their conversations did not wound them as Daniel had feared. Instead, they seemed to clear small spaces in the air.

Then, on a Saturday in May, Daniel found the keys.

They were clearing the cupboard under the stairs, a place where objects went to await either usefulness or burial. There were old paint tins, a broken fan, two tennis rackets, a box of tangled fairy lights, and Daniel’s black backpack from his years with Rachel. He recognized it by a scorch mark near the zip from a camping stove mishap.

“I thought you threw that away,” Isabel said, passing him a dustpan.

“So did I.”

Inside were maps soft with age, a packet of tissues, a torch without batteries, and a small tin that had once held mints. When Daniel opened it, a ring of keys slid into his palm.

He knew them before memory caught up.

Not the brass dream key exactly, but close: three ordinary keys, a blue plastic fob, and a smaller key for a padlock. The fob had faded letters printed on it.

BOATHOUSE 3.

For a moment he was not in the hallway of the house he shared with Isabel. He was standing beside a canal on an evening years earlier, the air green with summer, Rachel laughing as he tried to lift a canoe from a rack without dropping it on his foot.

“That’s not the end that goes in first,” she had said.

“There’s an end that goes in first?”

“There is if you don’t want us to travel in circles.”

They had joined a canoe club one spring because Rachel had declared they needed “a shared activity that didn’t involve assembling furniture or arguing in supermarkets.” For three months they went every Thursday evening. Daniel had forgotten this completely. Not misplaced it: erased it through neglect.

He remembered now the slap of water against the hull, Rachel in front, setting a rhythm with her paddle. He remembered the first time they stopped under a stone bridge because rain had started suddenly and violently, turning the canal white. They had sat there laughing, soaked through, the world beyond the bridge blurred and shining. Later, they had drunk hot chocolate from a thermos and eaten crisps with cold fingers.

He had loved her then. He had been happy.

And then they had stopped going. Work got busier. Rachel had a bad cough. The club changed its schedule. They said they would return in the summer, then in the autumn, then never. The keys must have ended up in his bag, waiting without accusation.

Isabel was watching him. “What are they?”

“Not the flat,” he said slowly. “A boathouse.”

“A dream boathouse?”

“A real one.”

He sat back on his heels and told her.

The memory loosened others. Rachel buying waterproof trousers and wearing them once. The club secretary, a retired teacher called Moira, who could reverse a canoe more gracefully than Daniel could walk forward. The smell of river mud and wet rope. The particular peace of moving over water with another person, both of you facing the same direction.

“That sounds lovely,” Isabel said.

“It was.” He closed his hand around the keys. “I don’t know how I forgot.”

“Maybe because it belonged to a life you packed away.”

“Maybe.” He looked at the faded fob. “In the dream, Rachel keeps saying I forgot the flat. But there was no flat. I think it was this.”

“A boat?”

“Not exactly. The feeling of it. Doing something together before we became two people managing disappointment.”

Isabel leaned against the wall. “And now?”

“Now I’ve apparently been hoarding stolen canoe-club property for nearly a decade.”

“That is serious. You may have to go on the run by water.”

He smiled, but something in him had shifted. The dream’s empty rooms seemed less mysterious now. They were not a message from Rachel. They were space. Unused space. A life that might have been furnished differently, or a life waiting still.

The next day he emailed the canoe club.

He expected no reply, or a polite request to return the keys by post. Instead, Moira herself answered within an hour.

Daniel! I wondered where those had got to. We changed the locks years ago, but you’re welcome to come down. Beginners’ session Sunday mornings. Bring your knees and a sense of humility.

Isabel read the message over his shoulder. “Your knees?”

“She remembers me.”

“Should I be worried?”

“About Moira? Deeply.”

The following Sunday was clear and bright, the sky washed clean after a night of rain. The canal lay at the edge of town behind a row of warehouses converted into studios and cafés. Poplars leaned over the towpath, their leaves flashing silver in the wind. For a second, as Daniel and Isabel approached the old boathouse, he felt the dream fold itself around the real world: the same trees, the same pale water, the sense of arriving somewhere he had been expected.

Moira was smaller than he remembered, or perhaps age had made her more concentrated. She embraced him as if he were a nephew returning from a foolish expedition.

“You look less terrified,” she said.

“I’ve cultivated an appearance of competence.”

“Dangerous habit.” She turned to Isabel. “And you must be the brave woman.”

“I’m mostly here to prevent him from joining a cult.”

“Too late,” Moira said. “Life jackets are on the left.”

They chose a red canoe with scratches along its sides. Daniel expected the old grief to rise when he stepped into it, some ache of Rachel and youth and all that had been lost. Instead he felt only awkwardness, then cold water dripping from his paddle onto his shoes.

Isabel sat in front, her hair tied back, shoulders tense.

“Have you done this before?” he asked.

“Once, on a school trip. I remember betrayal and pondweed.”

“Excellent foundation.”

They pushed off.

At first they moved badly. The canoe wobbled. Daniel overcorrected. Isabel paddled hard on one side and sent them nosing toward the reeds.

“Are we meant to be this close to the bank?” she called.

“We’re examining local ecosystems.”

A duck regarded them with contempt.

But gradually their motions settled. Isabel learned to dip the paddle smoothly and draw it back without splashing herself. Daniel stopped trying to steer them into perfection and followed her rhythm. The town loosened behind them. Warehouses gave way to gardens, then allotments, then a stretch where hawthorn grew thick along the bank and the water held pieces of sky.

They passed beneath a low bridge, and the air cooled. Daniel remembered the rainstorm years before so vividly he almost heard Rachel laugh. The memory did not hurt as it once might have. It belonged to him, but it did not require him to live inside it.

On the far side of the bridge, Isabel paused. Their canoe drifted.

“Is this strange for you?” she asked without turning around.

“Yes,” he said. “But not in the way I expected.”

“What way is it?”

He considered. “Like finding a room in your house you forgot existed. And discovering it isn’t haunted. It just needs the windows opened.”

She rested her paddle across her knees. Sunlight caught the water and threw trembling reflections onto the underside of the bridge.

“I’m glad you told me about the dreams,” she said.

“So am I.”

“I don’t want to compete with your past.”

“You don’t.”

“I know. But sometimes I think we behave as if loving someone means arriving empty. No old keys. No strange flats. No supermarket dreams.”

He smiled. “That would make us very boring.”

“It would make us liars.”

The canoe turned gently with the current. Ahead, the canal widened, bright and inviting.

Daniel looked at Isabel’s back, at the familiar line of her shoulders, the loose strand of hair at her neck, the life jacket buckled slightly crooked because she had refused help. He felt a tenderness so plain it startled him. Not the first rush of love, not the dramatic ache of losing it, but something steadier: the wish to continue, to learn the rhythm, to stop pretending he could steer alone.

“Shall we go on?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said. “Let’s.”

They paddled out from under the bridge, a little unevenly but together, into the widening light.
