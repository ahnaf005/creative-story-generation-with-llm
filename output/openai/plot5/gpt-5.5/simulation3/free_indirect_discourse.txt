The first time Daniel dreamed of the flat, Rachel was standing in the kitchen, holding a bunch of keys as if they were a bouquet.

She had her hair shorter than it had ever been in life, cut bluntly at her jaw, which made her look both younger and more severe. Behind her was a window he did not recognize, tall and narrow, with rain sliding down it in lines. The kitchen was bare except for a sink, a cooker, and a yellow plastic bucket. There was no table. No chairs. No sign that anyone had ever boiled an egg there, argued there, waited for toast to burn.

“You never used it,” Rachel said.

Used what? The flat, apparently. Their flat. His flat. Some place he had forgotten to inhabit.

When he woke, Isabel was asleep beside him with one hand tucked under her cheek, her breathing soft and practical. The bedroom was full of early grey light, the familiar wardrobe, the familiar pile of books by Isabel’s side of the bed, the familiar chair on which Daniel had hung yesterday’s shirt and Isabel had hung nothing because Isabel hung things where they belonged. He lay still, bothered by an emotion too faint to name. Not grief, exactly. Not longing. More like the unease of having missed an appointment years ago and only now remembering it.

The dream came again a week later, then two nights after that, then with the vulgar persistence of a tune heard in a supermarket. Each time the flat altered. Once it had three rooms, all unfurnished, with carpets the colour of wet sand. Once it was up six flights of stairs in a building with no lift, the hallway smelling of dust and hot metal. Once Rachel opened a cupboard and inside was nothing but an old canoe paddle, cracked down the middle.

“You could have kept it,” she said.

He did not tell Isabel at first. Dreams about ex-wives were not, he felt, suitable breakfast material. Not because Isabel was jealous—she wasn’t, or not in any common way—but because dreams gave off a ridiculous heat when exposed to daylight. Say them aloud and they became both too important and too stupid.

He was fifty-two years old. He had been divorced from Rachel for eleven years. He had lived with Isabel for four. He was not a haunted man. Haunted men drank whisky at two in the morning and kept photographs in drawers. Daniel drank chamomile tea because Isabel said alcohol disturbed sleep, and he had no photographs of Rachel except, possibly, in boxes in the loft, mixed in with expired warranties and old phone chargers.

Still, he began to dread going to bed.

“You’re restless,” Isabel said one morning.

“Am I?”

“You keep turning over like a rotisserie chicken.”

“Romantic.”

“I’ve never promised romance before coffee.”

They were in the kitchen. Their real kitchen: warm, narrow, faintly cluttered, with basil dying on the windowsill and a calendar from the dentist pinned beside the boiler. Isabel was spooning grounds into the cafetière. Daniel watched the neat, decisive movement of her wrist. He loved that wrist, he thought, which was an odd thing to think. He loved the visible tendons, the small scar from some childhood accident she had once described and he had half forgotten. He loved her competence, though at times he mistook it for judgment.

“I’ve been dreaming about Rachel,” he said.

There. The sentence landed on the floor between them, not exploding, not even cracking.

Isabel poured hot water into the cafetière. “Ah.”

“It’s not that kind of dream.”

“What kind would that be?”

“You know.”

“I’m not sure I do.”

She put the kettle back on its base and looked at him. Her face was composed, but not closed. Isabel’s face was rarely closed; it was usually open in a way that made evasion feel childish. She had been a lawyer before retraining as a landscape designer, and she still possessed the courtroom gift of waiting through silence.

“She’s in a flat,” Daniel said. “An empty flat. I think it’s mine, or ours. I mean mine and Rachel’s. But not one we actually had.”

“And what happens?”

“Nothing much. She says things.”

“What things?”

“You never used it. You could have kept it.”

Isabel pressed the plunger slowly. “And what do you think it means?”

Of course she would ask that. Isabel believed dreams were the mind sorting itself like laundry: whites, colours, delicates, the occasional ruined sock. Daniel believed dreams were mostly indigestion wearing masks. At least he had believed that before Rachel began taking him around imaginary properties at night.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Maybe I regret something.”

“Do you?”

He almost said no too quickly. Instead he took his mug from her and stood in the small steam between them. Did he regret Rachel? The marriage? The divorce? The years during which they had lived side by side with all the warmth of neighbouring filing cabinets? Regret was too crude a word. It suggested one had arrived at a clean junction and chosen the wrong road. But life had not been like that. It had been more like failing to notice a leak until the ceiling came down.

“I regret how we did it,” he said.

“The divorce?”

“The marriage.”

Isabel nodded, as if this were not an insult to her, not a threat, but a simple piece of weather.

Later that day, at work, Daniel tried to concentrate on a report about drainage costs for a development outside Reading. Numbers swam politely in their columns. He had once liked precision. Culverts, gradients, projected volumes of water: these things obeyed rules. Human beings, less so. Rachel had hated how he talked about practicalities when she wanted recognition of feeling. He had hated how she called his practicalities avoidance. There they were again, the old courtroom speeches. Was he to go on cross-examining a woman who had remarried a dentist in Bristol and sent him, two Christmases ago, a card signed “Rachel and Tom” with a picture of a dog in antlers?

At lunch, he searched online for “dream empty flat ex wife meaning,” then closed the browser in disgust after two results. Unresolved emotional space, one site suggested. Untapped potential, said another. For heaven’s sake. The internet was a machine for making one’s foolishness communal.

That evening Isabel asked him about Rachel as they chopped vegetables for soup.

“Did you two ever have a place you didn’t move into?”

“No.”

“Nearly buy somewhere?”

“No.”

“Sublet? Inherit? Break into?” She smiled.

“No flat,” he said. “We had the terrace in Crofton Road. Before that the rented place over the hairdresser’s.”

“With the purple bedroom?”

“You remember that?”

“You told me. The landlord had painted the radiators purple too.”

Daniel laughed. He had forgotten telling Isabel and forgotten, almost, the radiators. Rachel had said it was like sleeping inside a bruise. In those days they could still make jokes together. In those days, they owned two saucepans, one duvet, and a secondhand canoe they stored upside down in the communal yard, to the annoyance of the hairdresser.

“You canoed with Rachel,” Isabel said.

“A bit.”

“You always say ‘a bit’ when you mean something mattered.”

Did he? Infuriating woman. Observant woman. Beloved, infuriating, observant woman.

“We did it most weekends for a while,” he said. “Canals. Slow rivers. Nothing dramatic.”

“Why did you stop?”

He sliced a carrot into uneven coins. Why had they stopped? Weather, work, Rachel’s new job, his mother’s illness, the canoe developing a crack, the weight of it on the roof rack, the sheer nuisance of pleasure. There was always a reason not to do a thing that required lifting.

“I suppose life got in the way.”

Isabel made a small sound.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“No, say it.”

“I was just thinking life doesn’t get in the way. Life is what we call the things we choose instead.”

He wanted to object. It sounded like something printed on an expensive notebook. And yet there was the canoe, green and scratched, smelling of river water and plastic, and Rachel laughing as they failed to steer under a low bridge near Bath. There was his younger self, bare forearms brown in the sun, shouting left, left, no your other left, while she splashed him on purpose. Had life got in the way? Or had he merely turned, by increments, into a man who preferred dry shoes?

That night the dream returned. This time the flat had furniture under dust sheets. Rachel stood in the sitting room, though sitting room was generous; it was a small square space with a fireplace blocked by newspaper. She lifted a sheet from a sofa, and beneath it was not a sofa but their old canoe, impossibly clean, balanced on trestles.

“You gave everything away,” she said.

“I didn’t,” he told her.

But the dream-Rachel only looked at him with the weary affection she had used near the end, when he denied not facts but atmospheres.

In the morning, Daniel went up to the loft.

He told himself he was looking for the spare bulbs for the bathroom light, which had been flickering. The loft hatch yawned above the landing, and he climbed the ladder into the dry mineral smell of insulation and cardboard. There were Christmas decorations, suitcases, a broken fan, boxes labelled in Isabel’s handwriting—TAX, FABRIC, PHOTOS—and in his own older, less disciplined scrawl: MISC.

Misc was dangerous. Misc was where the past went to lose its edges.

He opened one box, then another. Manuals for appliances long dead. A Nokia charger. A photograph of his father holding a pint and squinting. Beneath a stack of old maps he found a small tin that had once held mints. Inside were keys.

Not many. Five or six, on a ring stiff with age. A brass Yale key. A square-headed one for a padlock. A tiny key, perhaps for a suitcase. Attached to them was a faded plastic fob from a boat hire place on the Kennet and Avon Canal: MALLORY’S BOATS — PLEASE RETURN.

For a moment the loft dissolved.

He was thirty-one, maybe thirty-two, standing with Rachel in rain so heavy it bounced off the towpath. They had hired a canoe because theirs was being repaired, or because they had sold theirs—no, not sold, lent? It came back to him in pieces, bright as broken tile. Mallory’s Boats had also rented out storage lockers for regulars. They had taken one for a season, intending to keep their waterproofs, paddles, buoyancy aids there. Maybe even save themselves the weekly argument about loading the car.

A little room, not a flat. A long, narrow room above the boathouse, reached by an outside staircase. Empty except for hooks on the wall and a cracked vinyl floor. Rachel had called it their pied-à-terre, their riverside apartment, their weekend home without walls. They had joked about putting in curtains. Once, when the rain trapped them there, they had sat on the floor eating cheese sandwiches from foil and drinking coffee from a flask, listening to water knock against the boats below.

“You never used it,” she had said then, not accusingly but teasing him, because after paying for the locker they had come only twice. Work had surged. His mother had fallen. Rachel had begun working Saturdays. The canoe, whether borrowed or their own, had become a problem awaiting decision. The key had stayed in his pocket for months, then in a bowl, then, apparently, in a mint tin, then in the loft, patient as a seed.

Daniel sat back on his heels. Dust tickled his nose. So that was all. Not an omen, not a symbolic apartment in the suburbs of his soul. A locker above a boat hire place. An unused room in which, briefly, he and Rachel had been happy.

Was that all? Of course not. Nothing that returned in dreams was ever only itself. The mind had taken a little room and furnished it with disappointment. It had given Rachel the keys because she had always been better at noticing what they had abandoned.

He brought the tin downstairs.

Isabel was in the garden, kneeling by a bed of winter herbs, cutting back dead stems. He watched her through the kitchen window before going out. She wore an old blue jumper of his, sleeves pushed up, hair tied untidily at her neck. Her concentration was complete. Isabel gave herself wholly to the thing in front of her. A plant, a client, a pan of onions, him when he allowed it.

“I found the flat,” he said.

She turned, secateurs in hand. “In the loft?”

“Sort of.”

He sat on the low wall and told her about Mallory’s Boats, the locker above the boathouse, the keys, the rain, the sandwiches, the unused season. Isabel listened without interrupting. When he finished, she took the keys from him and turned them over in her palm.

“Are they still there, do you think? Mallory’s?”

“No idea.”

“You could look.”

“I did,” he said. “On my phone. Closed years ago. It’s a café now. Or was. Hard to tell.”

Isabel handed the keys back. “Do you want to go?”

“What, to the café?”

“To the river.”

He looked at her. The question seemed simple, and therefore frightening.

“I haven’t been in a canoe for twenty years.”

“Good. Then you’ll have no bad habits.”

“I’ll have all bad habits.”

“Even better. We can be terrible together.”

We. Such a modest word, such a large offer. Daniel felt the old impulse to hedge. It would be cold. They would need equipment. His back was not what it had been. Canoeing was one of those things people imagined doing while actually preferring lunch. And yet he could see Isabel on the water, brow furrowed in determination, demanding clear instructions and ignoring half of them. He could see himself laughing—not remembering laughter, but doing it.

“You’d hate it,” he said.

“Possibly.”

“You like being dry.”

“I like being alive more.”

“That’s dramatic.”

“I’ve never promised not to be dramatic after coffee.”

They went two Sundays later.

The hire place was not Mallory’s, but a newer operation farther along the canal, with bright kayaks stacked like boiled sweets and a young man in a wool hat who called Daniel “mate” with devastating sincerity. The morning was cold and thinly sunny. Isabel wore waterproof trousers she had borrowed from a neighbour and regarded the buoyancy aid as though it were an unflattering legal obligation.

Daniel expected to feel foolish. He did feel foolish. His knees objected to lowering himself into the canoe; his hands, gripping the paddle, seemed to belong to someone auditioning for competence. But then they pushed away from the bank. The boat rocked, Isabel swore, the young man in the wool hat pretended not to hear, and Daniel felt the water take them.

There it was: the narrow glide, the slight drag of the paddle, the tiny correction of balance through hips and spine. His body remembered before his mind did. On the towpath a dog barked. Bare branches stitched black lines across the sky. Isabel sat in front, very upright, paddling with grim and comic seriousness.

“Am I doing it wrong?” she called.

“Yes.”

“Tell me how.”

“You’re digging too deep. Let it skim more. Like—yes, that. Softer.”

“Softer is not one of my gifts.”

“It could be acquired.”

She twisted enough to give him a look, and the canoe lurched.

“Forward!” he said, laughing. “Face forward, for God’s sake.”

They moved slowly, badly, then better. Past moored narrowboats with smoking chimneys, past reeds whispering at the edges, past a heron that lifted itself with prehistoric disdain. Daniel felt the morning enter him. Not cleanse him; that was too grand. It simply made space. The dreams had not vanished, exactly, but they had changed shape. Rachel was no longer waiting in a flat with keys. She was where she belonged: part of the water already travelled, not denied, not returned to, present only in the way every current remembered its source.

At a bend, Isabel misjudged a stroke and splashed muddy water over his sleeve.

“Sorry,” she said, not sounding sorry.

“You did that deliberately.”

“I’m developing bad habits.”

“Fast learner.”

They drifted under a bridge where the air cooled and their voices echoed. For a few seconds neither paddled. The canoe carried itself forward on what remained of their effort.

Daniel looked at the back of Isabel’s head, at the loosened strands of hair escaping her hat. He could tell her now, perhaps, that he had been afraid the dreams meant he wanted something back. Rachel, youth, an unwasted room. But maybe what he wanted was not back there at all. Maybe it was here, in the fragile craft of choosing, again and again, not to leave the season unused.

“Isabel,” he said.

“What?”

“Let’s do this again.”

She did not turn round this time. Sensible woman.

“All right,” she said. “But next time we bring sandwiches.”

The bridge released them into sunlight. Daniel dipped his paddle, softer now, and watched the blade enter the water cleanly, making its brief dark opening before the surface closed behind it.
