For three weeks, Daniel dreamed of the flat.

It was always evening there, though he never saw a window. The light came from somewhere high and yellow, a bulb shaded by dust, and the walls were that particular rental white which was not white at all but a history of other tenants: nail holes, bluish shadows, the ghost of a poster. In the dream he stood in the narrow hall holding a bunch of keys he did not recognize. One key was large and brass, theatrical, almost ridiculous. Another was small and silver, the kind that opened a mailbox or a diary.

At the end of the hall was a door.

Behind it, he knew, was Rachel.

Not Rachel as she was now—wherever she was now, with her cropped hair and clever mouth and the laugh he had once mistaken for consent to be happy—but Rachel as she had been in the first year of their marriage. Bare feet. Green jumper. One hand holding a mug, the other pushing hair behind her ear. She would be waiting in the unused room, the room they had paid for and never entered, the room that existed only because they had not yet decided what to do with it.

A study, she had said.

A nursery, his mother had said, too soon and too brightly.

Storage, he had said, and Rachel had looked at him for longer than storage required.

In the dream Daniel never opened the door. He only stood there with the keys growing warm in his palm while Rachel moved quietly on the other side, not knocking, not calling, only waiting in a way that accused him of having made her wait before.

He woke each morning with a feeling of having forgotten an appointment.

Beside him, Isabel slept with one arm bent above her head like someone surrendering peacefully. She slept heavily and without apology. It was one of the first things he had loved about her: the trust of it, the animal certainty. Rachel had slept lightly, always surfacing, asking what was that, did you hear that, are you awake? Daniel had often lied and said no.

On the fourth Friday of the dreams, Isabel found him sitting at the kitchen table at six in the morning, holding a cup of coffee he had not drunk.

“You’re doing that thing,” she said.

“What thing?”

“Looking as if you’ve just come back from identifying a body.”

He smiled because he was supposed to. “Bad night.”

She came and leaned against the counter, her dressing gown tied badly, one knee visible. She was not beautiful in the way Rachel had been beautiful, with sharpness and arrangement. Isabel was beautiful in patches and weather: a freckled shoulder, a line between her brows, dark hair collapsing from its clip. Daniel often thought she was most herself before she had assembled herself for other people.

“Work?” she asked.

He might have said yes. Work was a generous cupboard; nearly anything could be shut inside it. But he was tired of the flat, tired of its stale light and its patient door.

“I keep dreaming about Rachel,” he said.

Isabel did not move. Only her eyes changed, narrowing not in anger, exactly, but in the way a person narrows their eyes toward bright light.

“Oh,” she said.

“It’s not—” He stopped. Not what? Not sexual? Not longing? Not my fault? Already he sounded guilty, which was absurd, because people dreamed what they dreamed. Didn’t they? The mind was a dog let loose at night.

Isabel took a mug from the shelf. “Tell me.”

So he told her about the flat, the hallway, the keys, Rachel in the room he never entered. He tried to make it sound faintly comic—the melodrama of the big brass key, the absurdity of recurring dreams at forty-three, as though his subconscious had subscribed to a serial—but Isabel did not laugh.

“Did you and Rachel have a flat like that?” she asked.

“Not exactly.”

“What does not exactly mean?”

He rubbed his thumb over a chip in the tabletop. He and Isabel had bought the table at a flea market two summers ago. It had seemed romantic then, inheriting scratches made by strangers. Now every mark looked like evidence.

“We rented a place in Bristol,” he said. “First year we were married. It had a second bedroom. Tiny. Damp. We never used it.”

“Why not?”

“I don’t know. We just didn’t.”

But he did know, or knew the outline of knowing. The room had been too full of what it might become. Rachel had wanted shelves, a desk, a lamp. She had been applying for a postgraduate course then, writing at night with her feet tucked under her. Daniel had wanted to wait. For what? Money, perhaps. Certainty. A version of himself who would not feel trapped by furniture.

Then, later, after the missed period that became a conversation, and the conversation that became a silence, the room had become impossible. Rachel would stand in its doorway as though listening for something inside. Daniel had begun stacking boxes there: winter coats, broken stereo equipment, the inflatable canoe he had bought in a fit of optimism and used once.

“You never used it,” Isabel repeated.

“No.”

“And Rachel is inside it now.”

“In the dream.”

“Yes. In the dream.”

There was no accusation in her voice, which made him wish there had been. Accusation gave a man something to defend against. Gentleness left him alone with the facts.

Isabel poured hot water over a tea bag and watched it darken. “Do you miss her?”

Daniel looked toward the window. Dawn was lifting itself over the backs of the houses, grey and reluctant.

“No,” he said, too quickly.

Isabel raised an eyebrow.

“I mean, not like that. I miss…” He searched for a word that would not injure them. “I miss who I was before I became someone who had done certain things.”

She nodded as if this made sense, and because she nodded, it did.

They sat together while the heating clicked in the walls. Isabel had been married too, briefly, in her twenties, to a photographer named Max who still sent Christmas cards featuring black-and-white pictures of goats, bridges, rain. Daniel had once asked if she minded. She had said, “The goats? A little.”

Now she said, “I used to dream about Max’s hands.”

Daniel glanced at her.

“Not in a romantic way,” she said. “He had these beautiful hands. Long fingers. Terrible man for practical purposes. Couldn’t open a jar. But in the dreams his hands were always doing something competent. Fixing a tap. Building a chair. Holding a baby.”

“You wanted children with him?”

“I wanted the version of him who could hold one without needing applause.”

Daniel laughed despite himself, and Isabel smiled into her mug.

“That stopped after a while,” she said.

“What did?”

“The dreams. When I stopped being angry that he hadn’t become somebody else.”

There it was, offered casually, like the last biscuit: wisdom, or something annoyingly close.

Over the next week Daniel kept having the dream, but now Isabel was attached to it. Not present inside it—no, the flat remained Rachel’s territory—but waiting outside the dream when he woke, asking in the dark, “Again?” Sometimes he said yes. Sometimes he pretended not to have heard.

They began speaking, awkwardly at first, about old rooms. Not just literal rooms, though there were those too: Isabel’s first flat above a bakery in York where the windows sweated sugar; Daniel’s childhood bedroom with football stickers he had never cared about but refused to remove because his father had put them up. They talked about the places past relationships kept inside them.

“Rachel always wanted clarity,” Daniel said one night as they walked home from dinner, the pavement shining after rain. “She hated things being implied.”

“And you?”

“I specialized in implication.”

“Still do, sometimes.”

He put his hands in his coat pockets. The remark was mild, but it landed.

“Do I?”

“Daniel.”

“All right.”

They passed a bus stop where two teenagers shared headphones, each leaning away while tied together by sound. He and Rachel had once been like that, hadn’t they? Not teenagers, but foolish in the same elastic way. A shared song, a shared rented kitchen, a shared future they believed would arrive properly labelled if they waited long enough.

“I think I let her do all the wanting,” he said.

Isabel walked beside him, not rescuing him from the sentence.

“She wanted the course. She wanted us to move. She wanted to talk about having children, or not having them, but properly. I wanted peace, which meant I wanted her not to want so loudly.”

“And now she’s in the room.”

“Yes.”

“What happened to the canoe?”

“What?”

“You mentioned one. In the boxes.”

“Oh.” He had not realized she had remembered. “Inflatable thing. Green. We took it out once on the Avon. Rachel laughed so much she dropped a paddle.”

“That sounds nice.”

“It was.” This surprised him. He had filed the canoe under failed purchases, along with the bread maker and the Spanish CDs. But the memory opened cleanly: Rachel in a red life jacket, her hair whipping across her face, both of them spinning uselessly near the bank while a swan regarded them with contempt. They had been happy for at least forty minutes. How had he forgotten forty minutes of happiness? Perhaps because it did not fit the story he told later, the one in which everything had been narrowing toward divorce from the beginning.

“Do you like canoeing?” Isabel asked.

“I liked the idea of being someone who canoed.”

“That’s different.”

“Very.”

She hooked her arm through his. “Maybe you should become someone who canoes.”

He snorted. “At my age?”

“At your age, you can either become someone who canoes or someone who talks about his age.”

For several days nothing came of it. Life resumed its ordinary insistence: emails, laundry, the neighbour’s cat appearing on the sill with imperial disappointment. The dreams continued but changed slightly. Now, in the hall, Daniel sometimes heard water behind the door. Not dripping. Lapping.

Then on Sunday he decided to clear the cupboard under the stairs.

It was one of those decisions that pretended to be practical and was in fact moral. The cupboard had become a compressed archive of avoidance: paint tins, extension cords, an umbrella with no fabric dignity left to it, shoe boxes containing cables for machines they no longer owned. Isabel was at the garden centre with her sister. Daniel put on old jeans and began dragging things into the hallway.

At the back, behind a rolled rug and a bag of hardened cement, he found a small blue tin.

He knew it before he opened it.

Not knew as in recognized; there was no memory at first. But his body knew. His chest tightened, and his fingers became clumsy. The tin had once held toffees. On its lid, a smiling cow offered a jug of cream.

Inside were keys.

Three Yale keys, a bike-lock key, a tiny padlock key, and one large brass key darkened with age. Not theatrical after all. Merely old-fashioned.

For a moment Daniel was back in the Bristol flat, kneeling by the front door because the lock stuck unless you lifted and pushed at the same time. Rachel behind him saying, “We live in a puzzle box,” and he saying, “All property is a puzzle box,” because he had been twenty-nine and insufferable in a way he mistook for charm.

The brass key had opened the cellar storage cage, not the second bedroom. Of course. He saw it now: descending the concrete steps with Rachel, both carrying the deflated canoe in its bag. It had smelled of damp stone and rubber. They were taking the canoe out because the sun had appeared after a month of rain and Rachel had said, “If we don’t go today, we’ll become people who own a canoe but never canoe.” He had argued mildly—traffic, parking, the pump was somewhere, wasn’t it?—but she had already found the pump. She had a gift for locating the practical object hidden beneath his hesitation.

On the river they had failed hilariously. The canoe sagged in the middle, and Daniel’s paddle technique sent more water into the boat than past it. Rachel laughed until she had to stop paddling. “We’re not moving,” he had said. “We’re absolutely moving,” she said. “Just not in any direction a map would respect.”

Afterwards they ate chips from paper near the water, both sunburnt on one side of their faces. Rachel leaned against him, heavy and warm. “We should do this again,” she said.

“Yes,” he said.

They never had.

That was the ache of it—not that the marriage had ended, not even that they had hurt each other with such precise, ordinary weapons, but that life was full of small yeses abandoned without ceremony. Yes, we should do this again. Yes, we should paint the room. Yes, we should talk. Yes, soon. Yes, when things settle. Then years later a man found keys in a tin and understood that much of a life was built from the sediment of postponed yeses.

When Isabel came home, carrying basil and a bag of compost, Daniel was sitting on the stairs with the tin beside him.

“You’ve found treasure,” she said.

“Keys.”

“To what?”

“Mostly nothing now.”

She set down the compost. “You look strange.”

“I remembered the canoe.”

“Ah.”

He held up the brass key. “This was for the storage cage. In Bristol. Not a mysterious flat.”

“Dreams are poor estate agents.”

He laughed, then found himself close to tears, which was embarrassing but not unwelcome. Isabel sat beside him. The stairs were too narrow; their shoulders pressed.

“I kept thinking Rachel was behind the door,” he said. “But maybe it was just… everything I didn’t do. Or didn’t keep doing.”

“Rachel too, perhaps.”

“Yes. Rachel too.”

Isabel took the key from him and turned it over in her palm. “Do you want to contact her?”

The question entered the hallway and stood there.

Daniel imagined writing to Rachel. He could find her email, probably. He could say he was sorry again, though they had already exchanged apologies at the end, exhausted and polite, like diplomats after a minor war. He could tell her about the dream. No. That would be unfair, a parcel sent to an address that had not requested delivery.

“I don’t think so,” he said.

Isabel nodded.

“I think I want to stop leaving rooms closed.”

She looked at him sideways. “That sounds admirable and possibly expensive.”

“I want to go canoeing.”

“With Rachel?”

“With you.”

Isabel’s face changed so quickly he almost missed it: surprise, caution, pleasure restrained in case it had misheard.

“You hate cold water,” she said.

“I hate unexpected cold water. Expected cold water I may learn to respect.”

“And do we own a canoe?”

“No. Thankfully.”

“Good. I don’t want to die in something inflatable you bought during a personality crisis.”

“We can hire one.”

She leaned her head against his shoulder. “All right.”

The following Saturday they drove to a reservoir forty minutes away, where a man in a fleece gave them buoyancy aids and spoke with unnerving confidence about wind direction. The canoe was red, plastic, and reassuringly solid. Daniel stepped into it with the care of a man entering a bathtub at sea. It rocked; he swore; Isabel laughed.

“Don’t laugh,” he said.

“I’m laughing supportively.”

“That’s worse.”

They pushed off.

At first they were terrible. Isabel paddled too fast; Daniel corrected too often; the canoe turned in lazy circles near the jetty while children in kayaks shot past them like bright insects. Daniel felt the old irritation rise, the wish to become instantly competent or else abandon the activity as beneath him. But Isabel, at the front, twisted round and grinned.

“We’re absolutely moving,” she said.

He knew she had chosen the phrase deliberately. He had told her the memory, every detail, even the chips, even Rachel leaning against him. For a second the past and present touched without cancelling each other. Rachel’s laughter on the Avon; Isabel’s grin on the reservoir. The same water, and not the same at all.

“Just not in any direction a map would respect,” he said.

Isabel dipped her paddle. “Then we’re doing it properly.”

They found a rhythm eventually. Not a graceful one, but adequate, which was better than symbolic perfection. Left, right, pause. The blade entered the brown-green water and pulled. Sun struck the surface in broken coins. Along the far bank, reeds shivered, and a heron lifted itself with prehistoric disgust.

Daniel felt his shoulders loosen. The dream flat seemed distant now, not solved exactly, but aired. Perhaps the door had never needed opening. Perhaps he had needed only to notice he was holding keys, and that a key was not an accusation but an invitation with teeth.

“Are you all right?” Isabel called back.

“Yes,” he said.

And he was, for the moment. Not redeemed. Not absolved. Those were melodramatic words, dream words. He was a man in a red canoe with a woman who had chosen, with full knowledge of his evasions and his ghosts, to paddle badly with him across a reservoir on a windy Saturday morning.

It was enough to be moving.

After a while Isabel stopped paddling and let the canoe drift. “We should do this again,” she said.

Daniel looked at the water gathering itself around the boat, at the small wake widening behind them. Once, long ago, he had answered yes and allowed the word to vanish.

This time he said, “Next weekend.”

Isabel turned. “Really?”

“Really.”

The canoe tipped slightly as she shifted, and he put a hand to the side to steady them. She smiled at him, and the sun caught the fine lines at the corners of her eyes. There would be other rooms, he knew. Other doors. He would forget things, postpone things, retreat into implication. A man did not become clear all at once because of a tin of keys and an hour on the water.

Still, he could begin with next weekend.

He could begin by paddling.
