## The Spare Key

For three weeks Daniel dreamed of the same flat.

It was always early evening in the dream, that hour when daylight has not gone but has given up trying. The flat sat on the top floor of a narrow brick building he could not place, though he knew with the unreasonable certainty of dreams that it belonged to him. Its door was painted dark green, and the brass number—7—was clouded with age. In his hand was a key. Behind him, on the landing, Rachel stood in her old blue coat, the one with the missing button she had never sewn back on.

“You never use it,” she said.

Daniel would look at her, then at the door. “Use what?”

“The flat.”

“I don’t know what’s inside.”

She smiled, not unkindly. “That’s why you don’t use it.”

Then he would fit the key into the lock and wake before he turned it.

The first time, he dismissed the dream as nothing more than a disturbance caused by too much cheese at dinner, or the detective series Isabel had insisted they watch before bed. The second time, he lay awake with a tightness in his chest, listening to Isabel breathe beside him. The third time, he got up quietly and stood in the kitchen drinking water from the tap, embarrassed by the ache the dream had left behind.

Rachel had been his wife for nine years and not his wife for six. Their divorce had been civil in the way a house fire might be called contained: no one had died, but everything smelled of smoke for a long time. They wrote careful emails about bank accounts and furniture. They split books by author. They agreed neither of them wanted the enormous orange casserole dish and then both remembered, too late, that it had been a wedding present from Rachel’s grandmother.

Now Rachel lived in York with a man who made violins, or repaired them, or perhaps merely collected them; Daniel could never remember. They exchanged a brief message at Christmas. Once, she had sent him a photograph of a dog wearing a paper crown. He had replied, “Brilliant,” and spent the rest of the afternoon unsettled.

Isabel, by contrast, lived beside him with cheerful immediacy. Her hairpins appeared everywhere: on windowsills, in coat pockets, once in the cutlery drawer. She sang while chopping onions. She read three books at a time and left them tented open on chairs. She had been married once too, to a man named Tom, who had loved cycling, clean counters, and the sound of his own certainty. She said his name rarely, but when she did, it was without bitterness, as though referring to a weather system that had once delayed her train.

Daniel did not tell Isabel about the dream. At first this was because it seemed silly. Then because it seemed less silly. Then because he had waited too long, and the waiting itself had become a secret.

The dream changed in the fourth week.

The landing was colder now. Rain tapped against a window somewhere below. Rachel looked thinner, or perhaps simply farther away. She held something out to him: a ring of keys, old ones, their labels yellowed and curling.

“You kept them,” she said.

“I didn’t know I had them.”

“You keep everything you don’t know what to do with.”

He woke with a gasp.

Isabel stirred. “Dan?”

“Nothing,” he said quickly. “Sorry. Bad dream.”

She switched on the bedside lamp. Her face emerged from the dark, creased with sleep, hair wild at one side. “Was it the one with the flat?”

He stared at her.

She blinked. “You talk in your sleep more than you think.”

In the morning, over coffee, he told her.

He expected awkwardness, or the small guarded expression Isabel sometimes wore when uncertain whether to be generous or wounded. Instead, she listened with her chin propped in her hand, brows drawn in concentration.

“Do you recognize the building?” she asked.

“No.”

“And Rachel’s just there? Like a guide?”

“Not a very helpful one.”

“Dream people never are. Mine mostly lose luggage.”

He smiled despite himself.

Isabel stirred her coffee. “What do you think the flat is?”

“I was hoping you’d tell me it’s indigestion.”

“It might be. But recurring indigestion with set design.”

He looked down at the table. A crumb rested by his wrist like a tiny accusation. “I don’t want you to think—”

“That you secretly want to move into a dream flat with your ex-wife?”

“No. Well. I don’t know. That I’m still…” He struggled for the word. In love? Caught? Ruined? “That there’s something unfinished.”

Isabel was quiet for a moment. Outside, a bus sighed at the stop below their window.

“I think,” she said, “most things are unfinished. Even when they’re over.”

That afternoon they walked through the park, the trees bare and black against a low white sky. Children in puffy coats dragged sticks along the railings. A man threw a tennis ball for a dog who had no intention of returning it.

“I used to dream about Tom after I left,” Isabel said. “For months. Nothing dramatic. We’d be in a supermarket choosing washing powder, or he’d be reading the gas meter. It made me furious. I’d finally got out, and there he was, comparing detergent prices in my subconscious.”

Daniel laughed. “Did it stop?”

“Eventually. Or I stopped minding.” She tucked her hand into his elbow. “The last dream I remember, we were at an airport. He was convinced we were late. I realized I didn’t have a ticket for his flight. That was nice.”

“I don’t know what my dream wants.”

“Maybe it wants you to open the door.”

“Helpful.”

“Maybe you don’t need to know what’s inside to admit there’s a door.”

He found this both irritating and accurate.

The dreams continued, but after he had told Isabel, they seemed less like hauntings and more like appointments. Sometimes Rachel said nothing. Sometimes she asked after his mother, dead twelve years. Once she told him the flat had good light. He never got inside.

Meanwhile, in waking life, conversations opened like cupboards.

Daniel and Isabel began talking at odd times—while waiting for the kettle, while folding laundry, while brushing their teeth side by side. He told her about the end of his marriage: not the dramatic version he had polished for friends, full of mutual respect and different paths, but the smaller, less flattering truths. How he had stayed late at work to avoid the heavy silence at home. How Rachel had begun repairing things—lamps, chair legs, curtain hooks—with a kind of violent competence, as though the marriage were a leaking tap and she might tighten it if only she found the right tool. How neither of them had been cruel, which in some ways made the failure harder to understand.

Isabel told him about Tom’s rules. Not rules he would have called rules. Preferences. Systems. The correct way to stack plates. The correct route to Devon. The correct tone in which to answer a question. “By the end,” she said one night, peeling an orange in one long strip, “I felt like a guest in a museum of someone else’s life.”

Daniel reached for her hand. Her fingers smelled of citrus.

They were happy, he thought, but sometimes happiness itself made him cautious. He loved Isabel’s laugh, her stubbornness, the way she could identify birds by sound and biscuits by texture. He loved the peace of their home. Yet there were rooms inside him he kept locked because he feared cluttering what they had built together.

One Saturday in March, rain made the windows silver and the flat feel smaller than usual. Isabel announced that they had to clear the cupboard under the stairs before it achieved consciousness.

They dragged everything into the hallway: paint tins, a broken heater, Christmas lights, a tennis racket with one missing string, shoes neither of them claimed. At the back, beneath a collapsed cardboard box, Daniel found a biscuit tin patterned with roses. It had belonged to his mother. He remembered putting old coins in it years ago.

Inside were not coins but keys.

A dozen of them lay tangled together: Yale keys, tiny suitcase keys, a rusted garage key, two with plastic tags so faded the writing had become ghostly. Daniel lifted them out, and the sound they made in his palm—metallic, intimate—sent him suddenly back twenty-five years.

He was nineteen, standing by a river in late May. The air smelled of cut grass and muddy water. Rachel, not yet his wife, not yet anything fixed, was laughing because he had dropped a paddle into the reeds. They were students then, reckless with time. A friend had lent them a canoe and a ring of keys: boathouse, storage shed, bicycle lock. Rachel wore rolled-up jeans and red canvas shoes. Her hair was escaping from a scarf. When Daniel leaned too far reaching for the paddle, the canoe tipped, and both of them fell shrieking into the brown, cold river.

Afterward they sat on the bank soaked to the skin, sharing a packet of crushed biscuits. Rachel held up the boathouse key and declared it the key to their “waterside estate.” Daniel, shivering, had said they should come back every week. For one summer they nearly did. They learned the slow logic of water: how to steer by yielding, how to move together or go in circles, how a river looked different when you traveled at its pace.

He had forgotten all of it.

No—that wasn’t true. He had stored it away in some unused flat inside himself.

Isabel crouched beside him. “What are they?”

“Old keys.” His voice sounded strange.

“To what?”

“I don’t know. Some of them.” He turned one over. A small brass key, dark with age. “This might be from a boathouse.”

“A boathouse?”

He told her the memory, haltingly at first, then with increasing warmth: the borrowed canoe, the lost paddle, Rachel’s red shoes, the grand estate made of mud and nettles. He expected pain to follow, but the memory came differently now. Not as a claim on him. Not as betrayal. Just a bright thing that had happened once.

Isabel listened, sitting cross-legged among the debris of the cupboard. When he finished, she smiled.

“I’ve never been canoeing,” she said.

“Really?”

“Tom considered it an inefficient form of travel.”

Daniel laughed so hard he had to sit down on the bottom stair.

The next weekend, Isabel booked a canoe for two at a reservoir forty minutes away. The morning was clear and cold, the sky a polished blue that seemed newly installed. Daniel worried, as he always did before planned pleasure, that the reality would collapse under expectation. Isabel arrived at the jetty wearing an orange life jacket over her coat and a woolly hat with a pom-pom.

“I look,” she said, “like a safety-conscious traffic cone.”

“You look radiant.”

“I look buoyant. There’s a difference.”

The instructor gave them a brief lesson, most of which Isabel converted into jokes. Daniel stepped carefully into the canoe, then held it steady while she climbed in, gripping the sides.

“If we die,” she said, “I’m haunting you.”

“If we die, I think that’s redundant.”

They pushed off.

At first they were terrible. The canoe wobbled dramatically. Isabel paddled with fierce, misplaced enthusiasm, turning them in a wide circle back toward the jetty. Daniel tried to correct, overcorrected, and sent them drifting sideways beneath a low branch.

“This is exactly like relationships,” Isabel said, ducking. “Humiliating and damp.”

But gradually they found a rhythm. Dip, pull, lift. Dip, pull, lift. The reservoir opened around them, quiet except for the drip of water from their paddles and the distant calls of gulls. Trees stood along the banks in early bud. Sunlight broke into fragments on the surface.

Daniel felt his shoulders loosen. There was no door to open here, no dream Rachel waiting on a landing, no flat full of whatever he had failed to sort through. There was only Isabel in front of him, her hat bobbing with each stroke, turning now and then to check that he was still there.

“Are you all right?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said.

And he was, though the word felt too small.

They paddled to the far side, where the water narrowed between reeds. Isabel stopped paddling and let the canoe drift.

“Do you miss her?” she asked.

Daniel watched the rings widen where a droplet had fallen from his paddle.

“Sometimes,” he said. “Not like wanting her back. More like… missing a country I used to live in. Even if I wouldn’t move there again.”

Isabel nodded. “That makes sense.”

“Does it hurt you?”

“A little,” she said honestly. “But not as much as you hiding it would.”

He looked at the back of her head, the loose curls escaping from under her hat. “I don’t want there to be locked rooms between us.”

“Then we’ll have to keep misplacing the keys.”

He smiled. “That sounds like us.”

That night, he dreamed again of the flat.

The landing was warmer. Dust floated in a shaft of amber light. Rachel stood by the green door, the blue coat over her arm now instead of on her shoulders.

“You found them,” she said.

“The keys?”

She shrugged. “Some of them.”

Daniel looked at the door. He still held the key, but he no longer felt the old urgency, the fear that behind it waited an accusation or a command.

“Aren’t I supposed to open it?” he asked.

Rachel smiled, and for a moment she looked exactly as she had by the river, hair damp, face bright with laughter.

“You can,” she said. “Or you can go home.”

When Daniel woke, morning was filtering through the curtains. Isabel slept beside him, one hand tucked under her cheek. On the chair near the bed, their damp clothes from the reservoir lay in a heap, smelling faintly of lake water.

He got up quietly and went to the kitchen. From the drawer where they kept batteries, takeaway menus, and other minor mysteries, he took the old ring of keys. For a moment he considered throwing them away. Instead, he removed the little brass one he thought might have belonged to the boathouse and placed it on the windowsill, where the sun caught it.

Then he filled the kettle, set out two mugs, and began planning, with surprising seriousness, where he and Isabel might paddle next.
