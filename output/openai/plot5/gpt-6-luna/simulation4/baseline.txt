### The Flat in the Dream

In the dream, Daniel was always late.

He ran through a building he didn’t recognize, though he knew where he was going. The stairs were narrow, the walls painted a tired yellow. On the third floor, a door stood ajar. Beyond it was a flat: empty, sunlit, and waiting.

He never went inside.

There was always something to do first. A phone call. A train to catch. Rachel calling from somewhere below. He would turn away, and then wake with the peculiar certainty that he had forgotten something important.

The dream came often enough that Daniel began to dread sleep. It came on Sunday nights, or after he’d heard a song Rachel used to sing in the kitchen. Sometimes it came after a perfectly ordinary day with Isabel, which seemed unfair. He’d wake beside her with the dream’s light still in his eyes, and feel as though he’d been standing at the edge of another life.

He didn’t tell her about it at first. He’d been divorced for six years; there was no sensible reason to keep dreaming about Rachel. And there was even less reason to be dreaming about a flat he couldn’t place.

One evening in March, Isabel found him standing in the hallway after midnight.

“Are you looking for something?” she asked.

Daniel blinked at the dark. “I thought I heard a door.”

“There’s a door,” she said, pointing at the bathroom.

He laughed, but not enough to make it convincing. Isabel came over and rested her hand between his shoulder blades.

“You’ve been having the dream again?”

He turned to her. “How did you know?”

“You make the same face when you wake up. Like you’ve left something in another room.”

So he told her. The yellow stairs. The open door. The empty flat. Rachel somewhere out of sight, calling him away.

Isabel listened without interrupting. When he finished, she leaned back against the wall.

“What do you think the flat is?” she asked.

“I don’t know. That’s the problem.”

“Maybe it’s just a flat.”

“Dreams aren’t usually that helpful.”

“Maybe they are and we’re the ones who insist on making them mysterious.”

They went back to bed, but sleep took a while. Isabel lay on her side, looking at him in the dimness.

“Do you miss Rachel?” she asked.

The question was gentle, which made it harder.

“Sometimes,” he said. “Not the marriage. Not how it ended. But I miss parts of being young with her. The feeling that we had time to waste.”

Isabel nodded. “I know what you mean.”

He waited.

“I don’t miss Mark,” she said. “But I miss how certain I was before I found out he’d been lying. I miss the version of me who could believe a person without checking the story twice.”

Daniel reached for her hand. She took it, though her fingers stayed cool.

They didn’t speak much more that night. In the morning, the conversation seemed to have followed them into the kitchen.

“Were you ever tempted to try again with him?” Daniel asked while they waited for the kettle.

“Mark?” Isabel made a face. “No. But I did have to learn that missing who I was isn’t the same as missing him.” She glanced at him. “Is it like that with Rachel?”

He thought about it. “Maybe. I miss the person I thought I’d become.”

Isabel looked toward the window. Rain had started to bead on the glass. “That sounds lonely.”

“It is a little.”

She touched his wrist. “You’re allowed to be lonely sometimes. Just don’t disappear into it.”

For the next few days, the dream stayed away. Daniel began to think the conversation had done its work. Then, on Saturday, he was looking for a spare phone charger in the hall cupboard when a small metal tin slipped from the shelf and hit the floor.

A handful of keys scattered across the boards.

He recognized the brass one at once. The head was shaped like a flattened leaf, its teeth dulled from years in a drawer. He sat down among the coats and dust, holding it in his palm.

“Do you know what that opens?” Isabel asked from the kitchen.

Daniel turned the key over. A faint paper tag clung to its ring, the ink almost rubbed away.

“Number twelve,” he said.

The words loosened a memory.

He and Rachel had rented a flat on a quiet street beside the canal, back when they were newly married and both convinced that every good thing began with a plan. The place had been small and a little damp, but it had windows facing west. They’d meant to move in after Rachel finished a course and Daniel found a job in the city. They paid a deposit, borrowed a sofa, and visited twice with paint samples.

Then Rachel’s mother got ill. Daniel’s job moved north. The plans changed one piece at a time until there was no plan left. They returned the keys and never spent a night there.

He had forgotten the flat entirely.

In the dream, he had been running through the building where they’d once meant to live.

“Daniel?” Isabel was crouched beside him now.

“It was ours,” he said. “For a while, anyway.”

He told her about the flat, about the borrowed sofa and the paint samples. He remembered Rachel sitting cross-legged on the bare floor, balancing a paper cup of coffee on one knee. She’d pointed to the empty corner by the window.

“We’ll put a table there,” she’d said. “And you can stop eating dinner over the sink.”

He’d promised they would. They never did.

“I think I dream about the place because we never actually went in,” he said. “We kept visiting it like it was a future.”

Isabel sat beside him on the floor. “And now you’ve found the key.”

“I thought I’d lost it.”

“Maybe you had.”

He turned the brass key between his fingers. “It’s strange. I don’t feel like I want to go back. I just didn’t like forgetting that it happened.”

“Then don’t forget it,” Isabel said. “You can remember something without moving back into it.”

He looked at her. She was smiling, but her eyes were serious.

“Do you ever worry that I’m still living with my past?” he asked.

“I worry that we both do,” she said. “In different ways.”

It wasn’t a reassuring answer, exactly. It was better than one. He set the key on the floor between them, and Isabel nudged it gently with her thumb.

That afternoon, they took the old keys to the locksmith, who removed the broken paper tag and told them the brass one was no use to anyone now. Daniel brought it home anyway and placed it in the dish beside the front door.

On Sunday morning, he asked Isabel if she wanted to go canoeing.

She raised an eyebrow. “Is this part of the dream analysis?”

“No. I just remembered I liked it.”

“You used to canoe with Rachel?”

“Once or twice. We went on a lake near her parents’ house. I was terrible at it.”

“Excellent. I’ve always wanted to see you be terrible at something.”

They rented a two-person canoe at a place on the river. The sky was clear but cold, and the water carried small broken reflections of the buildings along the bank. Daniel climbed in first and wobbled so dramatically that Isabel laughed. When she stepped aboard, the boat lurched in the other direction.

“Very reassuring,” she said.

“I’m trying to make us feel alive.”

They pushed away from the dock. For a while, neither of them managed to paddle in a straight line. They spun slowly toward the bank, corrected too hard, and drifted toward the middle again. Isabel began laughing in earnest.

“You said you were bad at this,” she said.

“I didn’t realize I’d improved.”

They found a rhythm eventually—not a perfect one, but something close enough. The canoe moved along the river beneath the bridges. A dog barked at them from a path. A cyclist rang a bell. The ordinary world went on around them, indifferent and bright.

Daniel thought of the flat: its bare floor and west-facing windows, the life he and Rachel had imagined there. He thought of himself at the dream’s open door, always turning away because another obligation was calling.

“Where did you go just now?” Isabel asked.

“Nowhere,” he said. Then he amended it. “Somewhere. But I’m back.”

She rested her paddle across her knees. “Good.”

The canoe drifted for a moment. Daniel watched the water gather and open behind them.

“I don’t want us to be another plan we visit,” he said.

Isabel considered this. “Then we should probably keep doing things.”

“Like canoeing?”

“Especially things you’re bad at.”

He smiled. She picked up her paddle and tapped its blade against his.

They began again, out of time at first, then closer. The canoe turned toward the far bank. Daniel corrected with a small movement, and Isabel followed. Together they brought it around—not perfectly, but without capsizing—and carried on downstream.
