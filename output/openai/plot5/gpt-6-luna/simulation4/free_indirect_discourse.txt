### The Spare Key

The flat was always at the end of a corridor Daniel didn’t recognize.

It had a green door, though the rest of the building seemed to have been scrubbed of color. He would walk past numbered doors and doors without numbers, hearing a radio somewhere behind the walls, and arrive at the green one with a key already in his hand. The key fit. It always fit. Inside, the flat was empty except for the afternoon light lying on the floorboards and a glass of water on the kitchen counter.

Then Rachel would appear in the doorway.

Not as she was now, he supposed—he hadn’t seen her in years—but as she had been at thirty-two, hair tucked behind one ear, wearing the old blue sweater with a loose thread at the cuff. She never accused him of anything. She only looked around the empty rooms as if she’d expected him to have done something with them.

He woke from the dream just before she spoke.

The first few times, he told himself it was stress. The fourth time, he made coffee at three in the morning and stood at the kitchen window until the streetlights switched off. By the seventh, the dream had become a kind of appointment. He dreaded it, then spent the day wondering what it meant. The mind was an irritating landlord, leaving him to pay for rooms he’d never asked to rent.

At breakfast, Isabel watched him turn his spoon around and around in his bowl.

“You’re doing the thing,” she said.

“What thing?”

“Trying to stir porridge with a spoon that’s not in it.”

He looked down. The spoon was in his hand, the porridge was in the bowl. Fair enough. “I’m fine.”

“You can be fine and still tell me what’s wrong.”

It was the sort of sentence that made him feel both cared for and faintly cornered. Isabel had a gift for making a door out of a wall.

He told her about the dream that night, after they’d turned off the television and the room had gone soft around them. He left out the details at first: the green door, the glass of water. Then she asked whether Rachel spoke, and he said no.

“Do you think the flat is hers?” Isabel asked.

“I don’t know. It doesn’t look like anywhere we lived.”

“Maybe it’s yours.”

“An empty flat I’ve never seen?”

“People have stranger things.”

He laughed, though he didn’t know what she meant. She leaned her head against the back of the sofa. “Do you miss her?”

The question was quiet. That made it harder to dodge.

“I miss who we were, sometimes,” he said. “Not being married to her.”

Isabel nodded. “That’s different.”

Was it? He had always considered the distinction a small, neat fence. On one side: Rachel, who had made him furious by leaving a wet towel on the bed and had once driven six hours through sleet because he’d forgotten his passport. On the other: the years before he knew how it ended. The country they had lived in together, which he could visit in memory but not return to. Maybe the fence was mostly there to stop him wandering.

“Do you dream about anyone?” he asked.

“My ex?” Isabel tilted her head. “Sometimes. Usually I’m explaining something to him. He’s never listening.”

Daniel smiled. “That sounds accurate.”

“Even in my dreams, apparently.” She rubbed a thumb over the seam of the cushion. “I don’t miss him. I miss thinking that if I explained things well enough, someone would change.”

Daniel looked at her. She said it without bitterness, which didn’t mean it hadn’t cost her anything. He had not known that part of her. Or he had known it and mistaken the silence for a closed door.

Over the next few days, the dream followed him into the waking hours. In the supermarket he found himself staring at rows of bottled water. At work, when someone mentioned an empty office, he thought of the green door. He wondered if Rachel had ever dreamed about him. Probably not. He had no evidence she had dreams at all, although he had once seen her cry at an advert for a dog.

Isabel didn’t ask about the flat again. Instead, they began talking about their former lives in the way people do when something has been loosened. Not a grand excavation, not one of those conversations where a person opens a sealed box and produces an entire tragic childhood. Just small exchanges, on walks or while chopping onions.

She told him about the boyfriend who’d made decisions on her behalf and then called it helping. He told her about Rachel’s habit of buying books she didn’t read, and how he had once resented the stacks on the bedside table more than he resented the silence between them.

“You resented books?” Isabel said.

“I resented the stacks.”

“Important distinction.”

“They kept falling on my side.”

“An intolerable marriage.”

He nudged her with his shoulder. She nudged back, but later, while they were waiting for the kettle, she said, “You know, when you get worried about something, you go very far away without moving.”

He could have told her that she did something similar: she made jokes, quick bright ones, and then waited to see whether anyone would notice what she hadn’t said. Instead he asked, “Have I been doing that?”

“Only a little.”

He wanted to ask if she was lonely. He wanted to tell her that he was sorry he had been there and not there, in the same way he’d once been married and not married, going through the motions while keeping a private exit in mind. But the words seemed too large for the narrow space between the kettle and the sink.

So he kissed her temple. “I’ll try to stay closer.”

She raised an eyebrow. “That sounds like something a man says in a film before walking out into the rain.”

“I’ll try to stay closer without the rain.”

“Better.”

The keys turned up on a Saturday morning. Daniel was looking for a charging cable in the hall cupboard, where old gloves and instruction manuals accumulated like sediment. His hand caught on a small metal tin, which slid forward and dropped onto the floor. Its lid popped open.

A handful of keys spilled across the boards.

There were two for doors he no longer remembered, one with a plastic tag marked STORAGE, and a long brass one dulled nearly brown. He picked it up. Something about its weight, its blunt teeth, brought the past close enough to touch.

“Found treasure?” Isabel called from the kitchen.

“Keys.”

“To what?”

“Good question.”

He turned the brass key over in his fingers. A little blue plastic diamond was still looped through its ring. He knew it now. The canoe shed at the reservoir, where he and Rachel had rented a narrow red canoe on weekends. The key had belonged to a wooden locker where they kept paddles and life jackets.

He hadn’t thought of the reservoir in years.

He saw it all at once: the blue water stippled with rain, Rachel in the front of the canoe, pretending she knew what she was doing while sending them in wide, determined circles. He had been laughing so hard he’d swallowed a mouthful of lake. She’d turned around to tell him to stop being useless, and they’d nearly tipped. Afterwards they had sat on the bank eating sandwiches, their clothes damp, Rachel’s hair curled against her jaw.

It wasn’t the marriage he remembered then. Not the last winter, when they had learned to speak to each other in the careful tones of people negotiating with strangers. Not the slow collection of grievances. Just the water, the ridiculous canoe, Rachel laughing with a fleck of bread on her lip.

How easily a memory could open a door. How little it promised beyond the room.

“Reservoir,” he said.

Isabel had come into the hall. He showed her the key.

“You used to canoe?”

“Once or twice. A long time ago.”

“Would you want to go again?”

He looked at the key, then at her. There it was: the empty room, waiting for him to decide what belonged in it. Not Rachel’s, not his old self’s. Just a space he had been afraid to enter because he didn’t know what he would find.

“Would you?” he asked.

“Absolutely. I’ve never been in a canoe.”

“Then I should warn you: they’re easier to move in a straight line if you don’t argue with the person in front.”

“I’ll bear that in mind.”

They went the following Sunday. The reservoir had been remade around the edges—a new café, a row of rental boats—but the water was much the same. Daniel felt the old unease of arriving somewhere that belonged to a past version of himself. He had brought the brass key, though it no longer opened anything. He kept it in his coat pocket, where it knocked against his knuckles.

The canoe rocked as they stepped in. Isabel grabbed the sides.

“Steady,” Daniel said.

“I am steady. The boat is being unreasonable.”

He laughed and held out a hand. She took it. For a moment, the two of them balanced there, not quite afloat, not quite on land.

Then they pushed off.

They did go in circles at first. Isabel blamed the paddle. Daniel blamed the wind. The canoe turned slowly, showing them the shore from every angle, as if the reservoir wanted them to inspect what they’d come from before allowing them forward.

“Are we doing it wrong?” Isabel asked.

“Probably.”

“Good. I’d hate for us to be naturally good at this.”

They found a rhythm. Not a perfect one. Daniel pulled too hard; Isabel’s stroke was uneven. But the canoe moved, and the shore began to slip behind them. The water opened around the bow, dark and bright in patches. He could feel Isabel’s attention in front of him, not on the destination but on the paddling, the cold spray, the work of staying balanced.

He thought of Rachel, then—not with the old ache of a wound reopening, but with the affection he might feel for someone on the opposite bank. She had been real. They had been real. That didn’t mean every new happiness had to be a verdict on the old one.

The dream flat came to him too: the clean floor, the glass of water, Rachel in the doorway. Perhaps it was not a flat he had failed to fill. Perhaps it was simply a place his mind had made, and kept making, because he was afraid of what might come next. An empty room could be a threat. It could also be space.

“Daniel?” Isabel called.

He had stopped paddling. The canoe had drifted, turning in the late-morning light.

“Sorry.” He dipped the paddle in.

“You’re far away again.”

“Not this time.” He looked at the back of her head, at the small curl escaping beneath her hat. “I was just thinking how glad I am you’re here.”

She glanced over her shoulder. “That’s a suspiciously nice thing to say. Are you about to ask me to paddle harder?”

“I’m about to ask you to paddle harder.”

She laughed and did.

The canoe swung forward. Daniel followed her stroke, then she followed his, and between them the boat found a line across the water. Nothing dramatic. No revelation that would last forever. Just the steady pull of the paddles, the cool air in his lungs, and Isabel beside him, going where they were going together.
