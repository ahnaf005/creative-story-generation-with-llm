### The Unused Flat

In the dream, Daniel always arrived too late.

The building stood on a narrow street he didn’t recognize, though it had the peculiar familiarity of places from childhood: a green door, a brass knocker shaped like a hand, a window above the entrance where a plant had gone brown. He climbed four flights of stairs and found the flat waiting for him. The key turned easily. Inside, every room was empty.

Sometimes there was a table in the kitchen, with one chair pulled out. Sometimes rain tapped at a window that wasn’t there the last time. Once, he found a pair of shoes in the hall—Rachel’s shoes, he thought, although he couldn’t remember what shoes she wore. But the flat itself never changed. It was always waiting. It was always unused.

Daniel had been having the dream for months. It came in clusters, three or four nights in a row, then disappeared just long enough for him to stop mentioning it. When it returned, he woke before dawn with the unpleasant sensation that he’d forgotten an appointment.

He told Isabel about it on a Sunday morning while she made coffee. They’d been together nearly three years, and their life had acquired the reliable rhythms of a shared household: the groceries, the laundry, the small negotiations over windows and thermostat settings. It was a good life, Daniel thought. He just wasn’t sure he knew how to be fully inside it.

“Does the flat belong to you?” Isabel asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Does it belong to Rachel?”

“I don’t know that either.”

She set his mug in front of him and sat opposite. “You’ve been dreaming about your ex-wife and an empty flat for half a year. You’re allowed to have a theory.”

“Maybe I’m supposed to move in.”

“Maybe you’re supposed to stop going there.”

He smiled, grateful for the joke and a little stung by it. Isabel wrapped both hands around her mug. She had a way of listening without hurrying him toward the right answer.

“Rachel and I never had an empty flat,” he said. “We had a very full one. Too full. Books, plants, boxes we never unpacked. We could barely find the table.”

“Then what do you think it means?”

He looked past her at the kitchen window. Their neighbor was carrying a sleeping child across the courtyard, the child’s head tucked against her shoulder.

“Maybe it’s just a place I don’t recognize.”

“Or a place you want to recognize,” Isabel said.

They talked about Rachel more than they had in a long time. Not because Isabel demanded it, but because the dream seemed to have opened a door. Daniel told her how he and Rachel had met at a friend’s party, how she’d laughed at his terrible impression of a radio host, how the two of them had learned to fight with the precision of people who knew exactly where the other was tender.

“I used to think if you loved someone enough, you’d eventually understand them,” he said. “Then we spent years proving that wasn’t true.”

Isabel stirred her coffee, although she took it black. “I thought my first husband would become the person I needed if I waited patiently enough.”

“You don’t talk about him much.”

“I don’t think about him much.” She shrugged. “But I did learn something. I can make a home with almost anyone. I just can’t make a home where I’m always waiting.”

Daniel heard the difference between her words and an accusation. That did not make it easier to hear them.

“Do you ever feel like I’m waiting?” he asked.

“For what?”

He didn’t know. For an invitation, perhaps. For certainty. For the old self who had once believed a life could be planned like a trip, each stop noted in advance.

Isabel gave a small, tired laugh. “You tell me.”

That night he dreamed of the flat again. This time there was a light on in the kitchen. He stood in the doorway and could hear water running somewhere, but when he went to find the tap, the sound stopped.

The next morning he told Isabel about it over toast. She said she’d dreamed she was back in her childhood kitchen, arguing with her mother about a cracked blue bowl. Neither of them could remember what the argument was about, only that they were both determined to win.

“Maybe dreams just bring back the rooms,” she said. “We supply the furniture.”

“Very wise.”

“I have my moments.”

They kept talking after breakfast. It wasn’t a grand confession or a promise to start anew. It was smaller than that, and harder: Isabel told him she missed doing things with him that weren’t errands. Daniel admitted that he often waited for her to suggest plans, then felt hurt when she chose something without him. They talked about how they’d both learned to be careful in relationships, and how care, when overdone, could resemble distance.

“We used to go canoeing,” Daniel said, almost without meaning to.

“With Rachel?”

“Yes. Before we got married, mostly. There was a place north of the city. We rented this green canoe that leaked.”

“Did you like it?”

“I liked being out on the water. I liked not having anything else to do.”

“Why did you stop?”

He thought about it. “We got busy. Then we got bad at being together. By the end, even choosing a restaurant felt like a statement.”

Isabel smiled faintly. “I’m glad we haven’t reached the restaurant stage.”

“I’ll try to keep us away from it.”

“Don’t make a promise. Make a plan.”

He did not make one that day. For a while, the dream stopped, and he assumed he had finally explained it to himself. Work grew busy. They bought a new lamp for the living room and argued amicably over where to put it. Isabel’s sister came to stay. The strange flat receded, like a house seen through a moving train window.

Then, one Thursday evening, Daniel found the keys.

He was looking for a receipt in the bottom drawer of the hall table, where old cables and takeout menus gathered in a loose archaeology. Beneath a folded map of the county, he found a small brass ring with three keys attached. One was a flat silver key with a square head. Another was long and blackened at the teeth. The third had a faded blue plastic tag.

He recognized the blue tag. His name was written on it in Rachel’s neat handwriting.

For a moment he stood perfectly still, holding the keys in the open drawer. The sight of them brought back a summer afternoon from nearly fifteen years earlier. He and Rachel had borrowed a canoe from the rental hut and carried it down a slope to the lake. The day was so hot they could feel the sun through their clothes. She had tucked the keys into the pocket of his shorts so they wouldn’t sink if she tipped the canoe.

“You’re assuming I’ll be the one to tip us over,” she’d said.

“I’m making a statistical prediction.”

“You don’t know statistics.”

“I know you.”

She had shoved him with her paddle. A wave rocked the canoe. They both yelped, then laughed so hard that the boat turned sideways and drifted into the reeds.

It was a clear memory, bright as the water had been. And unlike the dream, it did not ask him to go back. It simply reminded him that there had been a time when he and Rachel could make a mess and laugh about it.

Daniel sat down on the hall bench. From the living room came the muffled sound of Isabel on the phone, speaking to her sister. He turned the keys over in his hand. The flat key could have belonged to any number of locks; he had no idea why he’d kept it. The long black one might be for the storage locker they’d rented after the divorce. Or an old bicycle. The key ring, unlike his dream, offered no explanation.

Still, it had brought him somewhere. Not to the empty flat, but to the lake.

When Isabel ended her call, Daniel held out the keys.

“Do you remember these?”

She examined the blue tag. “Your ex-wife’s handwriting?”

“Yes.”

“Are you about to tell me they open the mysterious flat?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Good. I’d hate to have to compete with a haunted apartment.”

He laughed. Then, before he could talk himself out of it, he said, “Would you go canoeing with me?”

“Canoeing?”

“There’s a place up north. I used to go. I thought it might be nice.”

“Is it a date?”

“If you want it to be.”

“I do.” She turned the keys in her fingers. “But I’m not carrying them. You’re responsible for not tipping us over.”

On Saturday, they drove out of the city beneath a low sky. The rental place was still there, though the wooden hut had been painted yellow and the owner was younger than the one Daniel remembered. They chose a red canoe that did not leak, which Isabel considered an unfair advantage.

At the water’s edge, Daniel felt the old uncertainty rise in him. He was afraid of doing the wrong thing—of making the day about Rachel, or making it seem he had brought Isabel to fill an absence. But Isabel stepped into the canoe without hesitation and held out a hand.

He took it.

They pushed off. At first their paddles struck the water at different times, sending them in a slow circle. Isabel laughed. Daniel laughed too. Then they found a rhythm: dip, draw, lift; dip, draw, lift. The shore slipped away. Reeds shivered along the banks. A pair of ducks glided ahead of them and vanished around a bend.

They talked about nothing important. Isabel told him about a man in her office who put his lunch in the communal fridge and labeled it with his full name, including his middle initial. Daniel told her about a childhood teacher who’d insisted the moon followed his family’s car. Their stories drifted between them as easily as the canoe.

After a while, Isabel rested her paddle across her knees.

“What did the keys make you remember?”

“A day with Rachel. We nearly tipped over.”

“Were you happy?”

“For that afternoon, yes.”

She nodded. She didn’t look away from the water, and she didn’t ask him to make the memory smaller.

“I don’t want to erase anything,” he said.

“I wouldn’t ask you to.”

“I want to be here, though.”

“Then be here.”

It was not a command. It was an opening.

Daniel put his paddle back in the water. Isabel followed. Their strokes fell into rhythm again, and the canoe moved steadily toward the far bank.

That night, he dreamed of the flat. The green door was there, and the brass hand on it, and the familiar staircase. He stood outside the flat with the old keys in his palm. But when he tried them, none of them fit.

For the first time, he did not feel that he was late.

He turned away from the door and began walking down the stairs. At the bottom, the street was bright with afternoon sun. Somewhere nearby, water tapped against the side of a boat. He woke before he could see who was waiting for him, but this time the unfinished part of the dream did not feel like a debt.

In the kitchen, Isabel was making coffee. Daniel went to her, kissed the top of her head, and asked whether she wanted to go canoeing again next weekend.

“Only if you let me steer,” she said.

“Absolutely not.”

She leaned back against him, laughing, and he held on.
