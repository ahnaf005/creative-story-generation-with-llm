### The Flat Above the River

In the dream, Rachel was always already gone.

Daniel would find himself in a narrow hallway with a strip of yellow light under a door. Somewhere beyond it, a tap dripped. He knew the flat was his, though he had never seen it before: the rooms were empty, the windows filmed with dust, and the walls had the pale, rubbed look of a place just vacated. He knew, too, that Rachel had been there. Not that she was hiding, exactly. She had simply left without telling him which way she’d gone.

He woke before he could open the door.

The dreams came every few weeks, then every few nights. By morning he remembered little beyond the flat’s damp smell and the strange certainty that he’d forgotten something important there. Rachel had been his wife for six years. They had been divorced for four. He hadn’t seen her in more than a year, though now and then a photograph surfaced online: Rachel outside a gallery, Rachel in a red scarf, Rachel looking as if she had just heard something funny.

There was no reason to dream about her. People appeared in dreams. That was all. The flat, however, stayed with him. It felt less like a place he had invented than somewhere he’d once been and failed to return to.

He mentioned it to Isabel over breakfast, after waking from it three times in one week.

“Do you think it means something?” he asked.

Isabel put down her mug. She was still in her dressing gown, one sleeve pushed above her elbow. “The flat?”

“The whole thing.”

“Maybe it means you’re worried about the boiler.”

He laughed, though he had been hoping she’d say something more useful. Isabel watched him over the rim of her cup.

“Does Rachel say anything?”

“No.”

“Do you?”

“Apparently not.”

“Maybe your dream is very British.”

He smiled. The joke took some weight off the morning, but not all of it. He kept seeing the door, the thin bar of light beneath it.

That night, he dreamed the hallway again. This time there was a brass number on the door: 4B. He woke with his hand curled around the sheet, as if he’d been about to turn a key.

At dinner, a few days later, he tried again.

“I think I’m more bothered by the flat than by Rachel,” he said. “She’s just… there. Like a name on a letter that’s been forwarded.”

“Do you miss her?”

“No.” He answered quickly. Too quickly, perhaps. “I miss being married, sometimes. Not to her, exactly. The certainty of it. Having a person who knew the whole history.”

Isabel leaned back in her chair. Outside, rain threaded the dark window. “I know some of your history.”

“Not all of it.”

“Neither do you know all of mine.”

That was true. They had been together three years, but they were still discovering each other in odd patches: a former holiday, an old resentment, a particular song Isabel couldn’t listen to anymore. A shared life didn’t arrive all at once. It collected, like dust in the corners.

“What about you?” he said. “Do you dream about Mark?”

Isabel’s mouth tightened slightly. Mark had been her partner for eight years, a fact Daniel had learned early and then tried not to treat as a measurement. “Not usually.”

“Usually?”

“Sometimes I dream I’m trying to get him to read something. He’s on the other side of a room, and I keep saying his name, but he can’t hear me.”

“Was that how it was?”

“Near the end.” She traced a ring of water on the table with one finger. “He wasn’t a bad person. It’s just that we got very good at not asking each other anything.”

Daniel considered the empty flat: the bare rooms, the tap. “That sounds familiar.”

“Which part?”

He didn’t answer straight away. With Rachel, he had mistaken silence for peace. He had believed that if a thing was important, it would announce itself; he hadn’t understood that people could stop saying important things because they’d said them too many times.

Isabel reached across the table and touched his hand. “You can tell me about the dream without it being a confession.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He looked at their joined fingers. “I’m trying.”

They spoke about their previous relationships then, not in a single grand revelation but in smaller turns. Isabel told him how Mark used to plan every holiday down to the minute, and how she had once loved being carried along by someone else’s certainty. Daniel told her that Rachel had been the first person he’d met who could make a room feel full just by entering it. He didn’t say that later, sometimes, her presence had filled it so completely that he hadn’t known where to stand.

“Do you think people change?” Isabel asked.

“Some.”

“That’s not a convincing answer.”

“I think they change in pieces. Usually after they’ve spent a long time insisting they’re fine.”

She gave him a look. “And are you fine?”

“Mostly.”

“Daniel.”

“No,” he said. “Not always.”

The next morning, the dream returned. The door was open a crack. Light fell across the floor in a narrow wedge. Beyond it, something shifted—no voice, no footsteps, only the soft scrape of a chair being pulled back. Daniel woke before he could see inside.

For days afterward, he was alert to the thought of keys. He checked the hook by the door, the bowl on the hall table, his coat pockets. The apartment contained plenty of keys, of course: keys that opened things he used, keys whose purpose he’d forgotten. None of them seemed to fit the dream.

The real ones turned up in a biscuit tin in the back of a desk drawer. He had been looking for a receipt. The tin was blue, its lid dented inward, and it rattled when he lifted it. Inside lay a small crowd of keys, dulled with age, tangled together on an orange plastic float.

He knew the float before he knew the keys. It had the faded name of a canoe club printed on one side.

The memory came back in a rush: Isabel and him on the river in their first summer together, paddling the wrong way because he’d pretended to understand the map. The water had been silver under a high sky. They’d bumped into a reed bed, and Isabel had laughed so hard she’d nearly dropped her paddle. Later they’d tied the canoe to a post and eaten warm sandwiches on the bank, their knees touching in the grass.

The keys had belonged to the club locker where they stored their shoes and bags. Daniel must have taken them home by mistake, then forgotten them. For a while, he had carried the orange float in his pocket, as if it were a souvenir of the afternoon. Now the plastic had cracked along one edge.

Isabel was at the kitchen table when he came in, keys in hand.

“Look what I found.”

She picked up the float. Her face opened with recognition. “The canoe keys.”

“I wondered where those went.”

“You stole them?”

“Borrowed them indefinitely.”

“You did say you were good with maps.”

“I said I’d seen a map.”

She turned the float over, smiling. “I’d forgotten about this.”

He told her what he remembered: the reeds, the sandwiches, her laughing at him. He didn’t tell it perfectly. The afternoon had no great message in it, no sign that they’d be together three years later. It was simply theirs: bright, ordinary, still available to be remembered.

“Do you want to go again?” he asked.

“Canoeing?”

“Unless you’ve developed an objection to boats.”

“I’ve developed an objection to you navigating.”

“That seems fair.”

They went the following Saturday. The old club had moved its boats to a new shed, but the river was much the same. They rented a canoe and pushed off into a cool morning. Daniel sat in front and Isabel in back. The paddle dipped, lifted, dipped again. At first they kept knocking blades, correcting each other’s rhythm.

“You’re going too fast,” Isabel said.

“I’m trying to keep us straight.”

“We’re going in a circle.”

“I know.”

The canoe turned slowly toward the bank. Isabel rested her paddle across her knees. “You don’t have to keep us straight all the time.”

The old Daniel might have argued. He might have insisted the river was difficult, that the current was responsible, that keeping straight was the entire point of a canoe. Instead he looked at the water and let the boat drift.

It was not easy to be still with someone. Stillness left room for the things they hadn’t said. But it also left room for Isabel’s shoulder close behind his, for the little adjustment of her weight as the canoe rocked, for a heron lifting from the reeds and passing over them with a slow beat of its wings.

“Do you still dream about the flat?” she asked.

“Not last night.”

“Maybe it’s gone.”

“Maybe.”

He didn’t believe the dream would vanish on command. It might return, with its hallway and dripping tap and that door he was always almost brave enough to open. There was no reason to think the past would stop appearing just because he had found something good in the present.

But he could imagine the flat differently now: not a warning, exactly. An empty room. A place that became a prison only if he stayed there, waiting for someone who had already left.

“Isabel,” he said.

“Yes?”

“I don’t want us to get good at not asking.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she leaned forward, resting her chin briefly against his shoulder. “Then ask me things.”

“What are you thinking?”

“That you’re still not very good at paddling.”

He laughed, and she laughed too, and the canoe wobbled under them. For a moment they were both off balance, both reaching, neither quite sure which way the boat would go. Then they found their rhythm again, not straight and not certain, but moving together down the river.
