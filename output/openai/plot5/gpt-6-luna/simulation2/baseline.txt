### The Flat Above the Bakery

For three weeks, Daniel dreamed about the flat above the bakery.

In the dream, he was always looking for it. He climbed a narrow staircase that smelled of yeast and rain, past doors marked with numbers he couldn’t read. Somewhere above him, a radio played an old song. He knew the flat was his, though he had never seen it before. He knew, too, that Rachel was waiting inside.

At the top of the stairs was a green door with no handle. Daniel would put his palm against it, and the door would swing inward.

The flat was furnished but empty. A table stood by a window. There were two cups on it, both clean. Beyond the window, the city seemed to be holding its breath. Rachel was never in the room, but sometimes he heard her moving around in the next one. Once, he saw the edge of her blue sleeve disappear around a doorway.

Each time, he woke before he could follow.

He told himself the dreams were ordinary. Rachel had been his wife for eleven years; people appeared in dreams for less reason than that. They had been divorced for four. They spoke occasionally, usually about their daughter, Lucy, who was sixteen and had learned to keep her parents’ conversations practical.

Still, Daniel began waking with the flat in his mind, as if he had forgotten something there.

He didn’t tell Isabel at first. He and Isabel had been together for two years, long enough to know the small habits of each other’s mornings: her tea forgotten beside the sink, his glasses misplaced on top of his head. They had also been together long enough for Daniel to recognize when silence was starting to look like concealment.

One Sunday, while Isabel was buttering toast, he said, “I keep dreaming about Rachel.”

The knife paused.

“Is that all?” she asked.

“There’s a flat, too. I’ve never been there. I don’t know why it’s hers, or mine, or what.”

Isabel sat down. She wore one of his old sweaters, the grey one with a darned elbow. “What happens in the flat?”

“Nothing. That’s what’s odd. It’s empty, but it’s ready for someone. There are two cups on a table.”

“Do you think you miss her?”

Daniel looked at the toast, then at Isabel. “I don’t think I miss being married to her.”

“That wasn’t quite what I asked.”

He considered this. “I miss knowing what she’s thinking, sometimes. Or I miss thinking I knew.”

Isabel nodded. She didn’t make him reassure her. That was one of the things he loved about her: she could let a difficult answer sit on the table between them without trying to polish it into something easier.

“Maybe the flat is just a room in your head,” she said.

“That sounds like something a therapist would say.”

“I’m charging less.”

He laughed, relieved, and she leaned over to kiss his cheek. But later he found her standing at the kitchen window, gazing into the back garden.

“Do you ever dream about anyone?” he asked.

“People from before you?”

“Anyone.”

She turned. “I dream about my mother’s house sometimes. It was sold when I was twenty. In the dream, I’m always trying to find the bathroom, and every door opens onto another bedroom.”

“That sounds worse than my flat.”

“It is. My ex, Martin, turns up now and then, but he’s usually carrying a plant. I don’t know why. He was terrible at keeping plants alive.”

“Maybe your mind is giving him one last chance.”

“Maybe.” Isabel came to stand beside him. “I used to think remembering someone meant I was being disloyal to whoever came after. It took me a long time to understand that memory doesn’t work like that.”

Daniel watched the rain stitch the garden back together. “I don’t know what the flat means.”

“You don’t have to know tonight.”

They talked about their former lives in pieces after that. Not as a grand confession, but in the loose, unplanned way people speak when washing dishes or walking home.

Isabel told him that Martin had wanted to move to the country, where the air was “better for the soul,” but had never once opened a window. Daniel told her that he and Rachel had bought a house because they thought a house was what people bought when they were certain about each other.

“We were happy for a while,” he said. “I don’t want to rewrite that.”

“Good,” Isabel said. “People do enough rewriting of the present.”

He asked if she’d ever been afraid that he was comparing her to Rachel.

“I was,” she said. “At the beginning.”

“And now?”

“Now I think you compare yourself to the person you were with her. That’s different.”

He looked at her, surprised. She shrugged.

“You get quiet when Lucy calls. You try to solve everything before anyone asks. You’re afraid of being the reason a family breaks apart, even though the family already changed shape years ago.”

“I didn’t know I did that.”

“Most habits are more visible from the outside.”

He wanted to tell her she was wrong, but there was no useful way to do it. Instead, he took her hand. She held his for a moment, then squeezed.

The dreams continued. In one, the flat’s window opened onto the sea. In another, Rachel stood behind the green door and said, “You left the kettle on.” Daniel woke with his heart beating hard, then lay still beside Isabel, listening to her breathe.

He didn’t feel that he was betraying anyone. He did feel that some old part of him was asking to be noticed.

On Saturday, he went to the cupboard beneath the stairs to find a tape measure. It was crammed with things they had meant to sort out: a broken lamp, winter scarves, a box of photographs, a canvas bag full of screws. While moving the bag, he heard a small metallic clatter.

A ring of keys had slipped behind the box.

They were old brass keys, tied together with a fraying red ribbon. Daniel stared at them, then picked them up. The largest had a square bow, rubbed smooth at the edges. For a moment, the present room seemed to tilt.

The key belonged to the boathouse.

He hadn’t thought about that place in years. It had been a rented shed on the river, nothing more than a low wooden structure with a warped door and a shelf for paddles. He and Rachel had rented it when Lucy was small. On Sundays they would take her out in a canoe, all three of them crowded together, Rachel at the back steering, Daniel in front pretending to know what he was doing.

Lucy had been terrified of the ducks. Rachel had made up a song about them, full of nonsense rhymes, and by the end of the summer Lucy was singing it louder than either of them.

The last time they went, the river was high and brown. They’d argued about a bill before leaving the house and continued arguing in whispers while Lucy sat between them. At the boathouse, Rachel had handed Daniel the keys. He’d dropped them in the grass. Lucy found them and made a solemn ceremony of returning them, placing them in his palm one by one.

“Don’t lose these,” she’d said.

He hadn’t lost them. He had simply forgotten what they opened.

Isabel came into the hallway holding a laundry basket. “What did you find?”

Daniel opened his hand.

“Old keys.”

“Do you know what they’re for?”

“The boathouse we rented when Lucy was little. I think it’s gone now.”

He rolled the keys between his fingers. The red ribbon had once been bright. “I forgot this whole summer.”

“No, you didn’t,” Isabel said gently. “You just hadn’t been there in a while.”

That evening, he called Lucy. She was at Rachel’s house, finishing a school project. When he mentioned the keys, she laughed.

“The canoe place? I remember the ducks.”

“You were scared of them.”

“They were big.”

“They were ducks.”

“They had intentions.”

He laughed, and heard Rachel in the background asking what was funny. Lucy explained. There was a pause, then Rachel’s voice came through, warm with surprise.

“I remember that ribbon,” she said.

Daniel sat at the kitchen table, the keys laid in front of him. “I found it under the stairs.”

“You kept those?”

“Apparently.”

Another pause. They spoke for a few minutes about the old river and Lucy’s duck song, which neither of them could remember properly. Then Rachel had to go. The call ended without becoming anything more than a pleasant conversation.

The flat did not appear in Daniel’s dream that night. He dreamed of the river instead. He was in the canoe, and though he couldn’t see who sat behind him, the boat moved smoothly through the water. When he woke, he felt neither relieved nor disappointed.

At breakfast, he told Isabel about the dream.

“Do you want to go canoeing?” she asked.

He looked up. “With you?”

“Unless you’re planning to take your ex-wife and your teenage daughter on a secret nostalgia tour.”

“I wasn’t.”

“Good. Because I’m an excellent paddler and a poor secret.”

They found a rental place on a stretch of river an hour from the city. The morning they went, the sky was pale and the air smelled of wet leaves. Daniel had expected to feel foolish climbing into the canoe, but the boat steadied under them. Isabel sat at the front with her paddle across her knees.

“You’re in charge,” she said.

“That seems unwise.”

“I’ve seen you steer a shopping trolley. I’m willing to risk it.”

He pushed them away from the bank. The canoe slid out into the slow current.

For the first few minutes, they paddled in the wrong rhythm. Their blades knocked the gunwales. Isabel splashed him. He complained, and she said she was testing his commitment. Then they found a pace together: her stroke, his stroke, the small hush of water falling from the paddles.

The river bent between willow trees. A duck glided past, leaving a clean line in its wake.

“Look,” Isabel said.

Daniel did. The duck dipped its head, then vanished below the surface.

“Do you think it has intentions?” he asked.

“Obviously.”

He laughed. Isabel did too. Their canoe wobbled but stayed upright.

They drifted beneath a low branch. For an instant, the leaves brushed Daniel’s shoulder, and the water turned the color of old glass. He thought of the empty flat, the two clean cups, the green door. Perhaps it was not a room he had left behind. Perhaps it was simply a space his mind had kept ready for the things he hadn’t managed to carry forward.

Isabel reached back, touching his wrist. “You’re thinking.”

“I am.”

“About the flat?”

“A little. About how there’s no door here.”

She glanced around at the river, the trees, the bright strip of sky. “There’s the bank.”

“Good point.”

They paddled on. The current carried them around the bend, out of sight of where they had started. Isabel began humming a tune Daniel didn’t know, and he tried to follow it, missing the notes but keeping the rhythm.

Behind them, the river closed over their passage. Ahead, it opened, not to anywhere they recognized, but to more water.
