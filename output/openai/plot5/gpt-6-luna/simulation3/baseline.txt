**The Flat Above the Bakery**

In the dream, Daniel was always looking for the light switch.

The flat had a narrow hallway, a kitchen with blue tiles, and a bedroom whose windows overlooked a railway line. It smelled of damp plaster and something sweet, like bread cooling downstairs. The rooms were empty except for a folded newspaper on the floor. Daniel knew he had never lived there, but in the dream he also knew he had been paying rent for years.

Rachel stood at the far end of the hall.

“Did you remember the keys?” she asked.

He never did. Then a train went by, rattling the windows, and he woke.

The dream returned every few weeks, sometimes twice in one week. Rachel had been his wife for eleven years and his ex-wife for six. They had not spoken in months, though there was no particular reason for the silence. Their separation had been orderly: signatures, boxes, one difficult Sunday over the last of the books. They had not been cruel to each other. They had simply come to the end of a life they had once imagined would go on.

Still, when Daniel woke from the dream, he felt as if he had been caught neglecting something.

He didn’t mention it to Isabel. At first, this was because it seemed too small to mention. Then, because it had happened so often that describing it would make it sound important.

Isabel had a gift for noticing the things he tried to pass over. One evening, while they were washing dishes, she turned from the sink and said, “You’ve been waking up tired.”

“I’m fine.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

He dried a plate and put it away in the wrong cupboard. Isabel waited.

“I keep having a dream about Rachel,” he said.

The dish towel paused in his hands.

“Is it a bad dream?”

“No. Not exactly. We’re in this flat I’ve never seen before. It’s empty. She asks me if I remembered the keys.”

Isabel leaned against the counter. “Did you?”

“No.”

“Maybe that’s why you keep dreaming it.”

He smiled, relieved by the lightness in her voice. “Maybe.”

“What do you think the flat means?”

“I don’t know. Something left over. A place I’m supposed to go, but don’t.”

Isabel went back to the dishes. “I used to dream about Mark,” she said. “Usually I was trying to get him to answer a question, but every time I opened my mouth, I was underwater.”

“Did you ever work out what the question was?”

“No. I think I liked having an excuse not to ask it.”

They laughed, but gently. They had both learned that laughter could make a door easier to open, not necessarily close it.

Later, in bed, they talked about the people they had loved before each other. Isabel told him how Mark had once planned a trip to Italy and then spent the whole holiday complaining about the heat. Daniel told her how he and Rachel had bought a set of pale-green mugs because they thought their future kitchen would be pale green. The kitchen had never been painted. The mugs had chipped one by one.

“What happened to the last one?” Isabel asked.

“I don’t know. Maybe Rachel kept it.”

“Or maybe it broke.”

“That’s less poetic.”

“Life often is.”

The conversation drifted, as such conversations did, from old partners to old selves. Isabel had been twenty-six when she moved in with Mark, certain that love meant becoming easy to live with. Daniel had been thirty when he married Rachel, certain that love meant never changing the plans they had made together.

“And now?” Isabel asked in the dark.

“Now I’m not certain what love means.”

“Good,” she said. “Certainty can be exhausting.”

For a while, Daniel’s dream disappeared. He took this as a sign that talking had helped, though he didn’t say so. Then one Saturday, while clearing the cupboard under the stairs, he found an envelope tucked behind a box of old cables. It held three keys on a brass ring and a small green plastic tag, cracked down the middle.

He knew the keys before he knew why.

He sat back on his heels. The brass ring had once belonged to his father. Daniel had carried it for years, first on his own key chain, then in the kitchen drawer after the divorce. He had assumed it had been thrown away. The green tag had the number 14 written on it in fading marker.

He rubbed his thumb over the number.

The flat above the bakery.

The memory came in pieces. He and Rachel had looked at the place after their wedding, when they were thinking of buying somewhere larger than their rented house. It was small, gloomy, and absurdly expensive. A bakery occupied the ground floor. Rachel had liked the blue tiles. Daniel had liked the railway view, though he’d pretended to find it noisy. They had imagined renovating the place and renting it out, a sensible investment for a future they believed would keep expanding.

They had paid a deposit. Then Rachel’s job moved to another city. Daniel’s father became ill. They had backed out of the purchase, lost some money, and tried not to think about it again. The keys had been left with Daniel by the estate agent, who had forgotten to collect them. He’d carried them around for months, then set them aside.

In the dream, the flat was not a place he had failed to visit. It was a place he had once tried to make real.

That evening, Daniel showed Isabel the keys.

She turned them over in her palm. “So the flat actually existed.”

“It did. Though we never lived there.”

“And Rachel asks you about the keys.”

“Apparently.”

“What do you think you’re supposed to do with them?”

“I have no idea.”

“Could find out if the building’s still there.”

He looked at her. “That’s all?”

“What else should you do? Write a poem to the keys?”

He laughed. “I might.”

“I’d read it.”

The next morning, he called the estate agent’s old number, which belonged to a woman who had never heard of the building. By lunchtime, he had found its address online. The bakery was still there, though it had changed owners. The flats above it had been renovated and sold years ago.

The news was a relief. There was no hidden door to open, no secret Rachel had left behind. The keys belonged to a lock that had long since been replaced.

Daniel put them back in the envelope.

He considered calling Rachel. He even opened their old email thread, where the last message was a note about a tax document. But what would he say? I found the keys to the flat we never bought? He imagined Rachel receiving the message, then sitting quietly with it. He imagined the answer: That’s funny. I’d forgotten about that.

He didn’t send anything.

Instead, he told Isabel what he’d found, and together they searched for the old building on a map. She studied the street view and declared the bakery’s new sign “a little too pleased with itself.” They made a joke of it, then let the conversation settle. The past could be looked at without being reopened, Daniel thought. It could be a room with a light on.

A week later, Isabel came home with a pair of secondhand paddles under one arm.

“You bought paddles?” Daniel asked.

“I borrowed them from my brother. He’s selling the canoe, but I thought we might take it out first.”

He looked at the paddles, then at her. “When did we last go canoeing?”

“Never.”

“Right.”

“I know you used to go with your dad,” she said. “You told me once.”

Daniel had. His father had taken him out on a lake when he was twelve, letting him steer badly and insisting that a canoe was not a boat so much as an argument between two people and the water. Daniel had forgotten the story almost as soon as he told it.

They loaded the canoe onto Isabel’s car the following Sunday and drove to a reservoir north of the city. The morning was cool and bright. At the water’s edge, Isabel held the canoe steady while Daniel climbed in. They wobbled, swore, and nearly tipped before finding their balance.

“An argument, then?” Isabel called, climbing in behind him.

“Between two people and the water.”

“Good. I was worried it might be us.”

The first stretch was clumsy. Daniel paddled too hard on one side, and the canoe turned in slow circles. Isabel laughed until she was breathless. He laughed too, despite himself. When they finally moved straight, the shore slipped away from them, and the noise of the road fell quiet.

They paddled past reeds and a half-submerged branch. Sunlight shifted over the water. Daniel felt his shoulders loosen. He told Isabel about his father’s old canoe, the dent in its side, and the way he used to sit at the bow pretending he knew where they were going.

“You did know,” she said.

“I was twelve.”

“Still.”

He looked back at her. She was squinting into the sun, a few strands of hair escaping her cap. He wanted to tell her that he was glad she had brought them here. He wanted to say that he sometimes feared their life together might become a plan he followed without noticing, a hallway leading to rooms he hadn’t chosen. Instead, he said, “I don’t always know how to stay in the present.”

Isabel rested her paddle across the canoe. “You don’t have to do it perfectly.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He thought about it. “I’m learning.”

She smiled, not as if she had won something, but as if she were glad to be beside him while he learned.

They turned back toward shore. For a moment, the canoe drifted, held in place by a faint current. Daniel could hear a train somewhere beyond the trees, though the sound was distant and soft. He thought of blue tiles, an empty flat, and the keys in their envelope at home.

Then Isabel dipped her paddle into the water.

Daniel followed her stroke, and the canoe moved on.
