### The Unused Flat

In the dream, the flat was always on the third floor.

Daniel knew this because he climbed the same narrow staircase every time, past the same green-painted doors and the same patch of damp spreading across the ceiling. He never remembered the street outside, or how he had come to have the key. But he knew the flat was his. He knew it was empty. And he knew Rachel was waiting inside.

The door opened without a sound. Beyond it lay a long room with bare floorboards and tall windows. No furniture, no curtains. Dust softened the light. Rachel stood near the far window, looking out at something Daniel couldn’t see.

“You found it,” she said.

“Found what?”

She turned, but her face was already fading. Then Daniel woke, with the question still in his mouth and the empty room lingering behind his eyes.

The dream had visited him six times in two months. It arrived without warning, though sometimes a staircase or a green door in the daytime would set him thinking of it. Each time, he woke with the peculiar sense that he had forgotten to do something important.

He didn’t tell Isabel at first. Dreams were private, weren’t they? Or at least, they were embarrassing. Better to let them dissolve over breakfast.

But the morning he finally mentioned it, Isabel was trying to rescue a burnt piece of toast.

“You’re walking up the stairs again,” she said, scraping at the black edge with a knife. “You’ve got that face.”

“What face?”

“The one you get when you’re trying to remember whether you shut a window.”

Daniel poured coffee. “I had the dream again.”

Isabel set down the knife. “The flat?”

He glanced at her. He’d told her about it once, in a half-joking way, after waking abruptly beside her. “I suppose it’s strange that you remember.”

“I remember most things you tell me. Even the strange ones.” She leaned against the counter. “What happens this time?”

“Same as always. I go in. Rachel’s there. She says, ‘You found it.’ Then I ask what, and I wake up.”

“Do you think it’s about Rachel?”

The obvious answer was yes. The irritating answer was that he didn’t know. Rachel had been his wife for eleven years. She had been gone for six. There were whole stretches of his life she still occupied in memory, like a room in a house he no longer lived in. That didn’t mean she was the point of the dream. Did it?

“I think it’s about the flat,” he said.

“Was there ever a flat?”

“Not that I remember.”

Isabel nodded, not as if she believed him, but as if she understood why he needed to say it. She put the ruined toast in the bin and made another slice.

“Maybe it’s somewhere you didn’t go,” she said.

That afternoon, Daniel thought about what she’d said. Somewhere he didn’t go. The phrase followed him through a meeting, then into the supermarket, where he stood staring at a shelf of tinned tomatoes as though it might provide an answer.

That evening, he and Isabel sat at the kitchen table, eating pasta and listening to rain tick against the window. He told her more about the dreams: the green doors, the stairs, the blank room. He described Rachel’s voice.

“Did you ever have plans for a place together?” Isabel asked.

“We looked at houses. There was one place outside Lewes, but it was falling down.”

“That sounds promising.”

“It had a tree growing through the kitchen floor.”

“Even more promising.”

He smiled. “We didn’t buy it. We didn’t buy anything. We rented wherever we could afford, then bought the house we lived in for the last eight years.”

“Was that a bad house?”

“No. It was a good house.” He twirled pasta around his fork. “A bit small. Too much damp. Rachel wanted to move, but we never quite did.”

“Maybe the flat is the life you didn’t have.”

He looked up. “That’s a bit grand for a dream about an empty room.”

“Dreams aren’t known for their modesty.”

Isabel told him then about her first marriage. She’d been twenty-four, and she and Mark had bought a house because it was what grown-ups did, she said. They’d painted the spare room yellow, then repainted it white when they couldn’t agree on what it was for. They’d stayed together long enough to fill it with boxes.

“I used to think the room was the problem,” she said. “As if if we’d found the right use for it, we’d have been all right.”

“And now?”

“Now I think we were two people who didn’t know how to ask each other what we wanted.”

They sat with that awhile. Daniel was careful not to say that he and Rachel had been different. They had been different, of course. Still, he remembered how often he had mistaken silence for agreement. How easily a plan could become a life without either of them choosing it.

“Were you happy?” Isabel asked.

“With Rachel?”

“With the life you had.”

He set his fork down. “Some of it.”

“That’s an honest answer.”

“Was Mark?”

“I imagine so, sometimes. He had a way of making things seem settled. I liked that, until I didn’t.” She reached across the table and touched his wrist. “I’m not asking you to compare.”

“I know.”

But some part of him did compare them anyway, in the way people compare weather: not to decide which was better, but to know what to wear. Isabel was less afraid of unfinished things than he was. She could leave a question on the table and come back to it later. Daniel tended to tidy questions away, even when they weren’t finished.

The next weekend, he decided to clear out the drawer in the hall.

It was a drawer of small refusals: batteries that might still work, a screwdriver with no matching bits, instruction booklets for appliances they no longer owned. Beneath a tangle of charging cables, his fingers found a ring of old keys.

There were five of them. Two brass, one silver, one small and blackened with age, and a flat steel key with a blue plastic tag. He turned them over in his palm. The metal was cold.

The blue tag bore the faded name of a place: *Mere Cottage*.

A memory opened.

It was early spring, eight years ago. He and Isabel had only been together a few months. They’d rented a little cottage beside a lake, though it had rained for most of their stay. The key had stuck in the lock, and Isabel had laughed while he wrestled with it, saying that the cottage was testing whether they deserved to come in.

On the second day, the rain had stopped. They’d borrowed a battered canoe from the owner and pushed it out from the muddy bank. Isabel had insisted on sitting in front because she wanted to steer. She had no idea how to steer. They’d turned in slow, undignified circles until Daniel, laughing too hard to be useful, took the paddle from her.

At last they’d moved out across the water. The hills were dark with rain, the lake quiet except for the drip from their paddles. Isabel had trailed her fingers in the water. Daniel remembered looking at the back of her head and thinking, with a suddenness that startled him, that he wanted to keep finding out what she would do next.

The memory was so clear he could almost smell the wet wool of his jumper. He could see the key in Isabel’s hand when they locked the cottage, her thumb resting on the blue tag. He hadn’t thought about that trip in years.

He carried the keys into the kitchen.

“Look what I found,” he said.

Isabel took the ring and turned it in her hands. “Mere Cottage.”

“You remember?”

“I remember the canoe that tried to kill us.”

“It was a perfectly safe canoe.”

“We went round in circles for twenty minutes.”

“That was your steering.”

She laughed, and he felt the old scene come alive between them—not as a picture of something lost, but as a place they could both still enter.

“Do you remember what you said?” he asked.

“Several regrettable things.”

“You said you wanted to steer.”

“I did. I was wrong.”

He leaned against the counter. “I’ve been thinking about the dream.”

“Have you solved it?”

“Not exactly. But maybe that empty flat is just an empty room. Maybe I keep trying to make it mean I missed a life I was supposed to have.”

“And?”

“And I don’t know. I don’t want to spend all my time looking for the door I didn’t open.”

Isabel considered him. “That sounds like progress.”

“It sounds like something I heard in a podcast.”

“Progress often does.”

He took the keys back. “Would you go canoeing with me?”

“Now?”

“Not now. The lake won’t be in the kitchen.”

She smiled. “Are you asking me on a date?”

“I’m asking you to sit in the front and steer.”

“Then yes.”

They went the following Saturday, to a lake an hour away. The sky was clear and cold. They rented a blue canoe from a man in a wool hat and pushed off from the bank, scraping the hull over stones.

Isabel took the front seat.

“Don’t let me steer,” she said.

“I wouldn’t dream of it.”

They moved out onto the water, paddling unevenly. The canoe wobbled, then steadied. A breeze pushed them toward the reeds. Daniel started to correct their course, then stopped. Isabel was already adjusting her paddle.

“There,” she said. “See?”

“I see.”

The lake opened around them, bright with the reflection of the sky. Daniel watched the small wake widening behind the canoe. For once, he didn’t feel he had to know where the water led before he could keep moving.

When he looked back, the shore was already a little farther away.

Isabel dipped her paddle. He dipped his.

Together, they went on.
