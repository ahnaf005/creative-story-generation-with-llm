### The Empty Flat

In the dream, Daniel was always looking for the same flat.

It stood on the fourth floor of a narrow brick building, though the staircase had more landings than it should. He knew the place was empty before he reached it. He knew, too, that Rachel was somewhere inside.

At the top of the stairs, a door waited beneath a brass number four. Daniel would find a key in his pocket, but it never fit. Sometimes he heard Rachel moving around on the other side. Once, he heard her laugh. In the latest dream, she called his name—not urgently, not sadly, just as if she expected him to come in.

Then he woke beside Isabel, with the taste of dust in his mouth.

The dreams had been coming for weeks. They left him with an odd, low restlessness that lasted through breakfast and followed him into the office. He never told Isabel. He told himself there was nothing to tell: dreams were only the mind sorting its clutter.

But one rainy Thursday, Isabel found him standing in the kitchen with the kettle boiled dry.

“You’ve gone somewhere,” she said.

“I’m here.”

“I know. That’s not what I mean.”

She was leaning against the counter, sleeves pushed to her elbows. They had been together for three years, long enough to know each other’s silences, and lately they had accumulated too many of them. Their conversations had narrowed to work, groceries and whose turn it was to call the plumber. Daniel had started to miss the way they used to talk—not any particular subject, just the feeling that a door could open between them.

He looked at the empty kettle. “I keep dreaming about Rachel.”

Isabel’s expression changed, but not in the way he feared. She didn’t stiffen or ask whether he still loved his ex-wife. She only nodded, waiting.

“And there’s a flat,” he added. “A place I’ve never been. It’s always empty. I think she’s there, but I never get inside.”

“Do you know why you’re looking for her?”

“No.”

“Do you want to?”

He considered this. “I don’t think so. I just want to know what’s in the flat.”

Isabel took the kettle from him and filled it. “That’s different.”

They sat at the kitchen table while rain stitched the windows. Daniel told her what he could remember: the wrong number of stairs, the stubborn key, the sound of Rachel behind the door. He tried to describe how the dream felt less like longing than like being late for something.

“Maybe your brain is making a room for something you haven’t dealt with,” Isabel said.

“Very therapist of you.”

“I dated a therapist for six months. I picked up the vocabulary, not the wisdom.”

He smiled. “What did you deal with after him?”

“Mostly his certainty that he knew what I was feeling.” She looked into her tea. “Before that, there was Martin. He wanted a life that looked settled. A house, a dog, a photograph in the right frame. I liked him. I even wanted some of those things. But I couldn’t breathe inside the version he’d planned.”

Daniel thought of Rachel, and of the life they had once planned with equal confidence. They had wanted a house too, though they’d argued about every other detail: the city, the furniture, whether a kitchen should have a table. “Rachel and I were good at making plans,” he said. “We were less good at noticing when they stopped fitting.”

“Did you know it was over before it was over?”

“I knew we were tired. I thought that was the same thing as temporary.”

Isabel gave a small, sympathetic nod. “I stayed with Martin too long because I thought leaving meant I’d failed to make it work.”

“Do you still think that?”

“No. But I still sometimes catch myself trying to make a relationship prove I’m sensible.” She reached across the table and touched his hand. “I don’t want us to become a project, Daniel.”

“Neither do I.”

“Then maybe we should talk before we’re only discussing the plumber.”

He squeezed her fingers. It was not an answer, exactly, but it was a beginning.

After that, the conversations came in pieces. On a walk through the park, Isabel told him how she had once left a suitcase outside Martin’s apartment and then gone back for it because it contained her grandmother’s blue scarf. Daniel told her about the last months of his marriage, when he and Rachel could spend an entire evening discussing what to watch and not say that they were lonely.

They didn’t make these stories into lessons. They let them be what they were: accounts of two people who had tried, and been changed by trying. Sometimes they laughed. Sometimes they sat quietly. The flat stayed in Daniel’s dreams.

One Saturday morning, he opened the hall cupboard looking for a screwdriver. The cupboard was a museum of postponed decisions: dead batteries, old envelopes, a wool hat no one wore. Behind a shoebox, his fingers closed around a small metal tin.

Inside lay four keys on a faded blue cord.

He knew them at once. One was long and blackened, one had a chipped green plastic head. He turned them over in his palm, and suddenly he was standing beside a river in late summer, seventeen years old, with his father’s canoe bumping softly against the bank.

His father had given him the keys when Daniel was eighteen. They belonged to a boathouse and a padlocked shed at the old rowing club, where the family kept a battered red canoe. Daniel had spent whole afternoons on the water, learning how to steer without fighting the current. After his father died, the club had changed hands and the canoe had been sold. The keys had come home in a drawer and disappeared into the cupboard.

The memory arrived whole: sunlight on the river, his father’s hand laid over his on the paddle, the instruction to stop trying to force the boat straight. “Feel what the water’s doing,” he had said. “Then decide.”

Daniel had not thought of that afternoon in years.

When Isabel came into the hall, he was still holding the keys.

“Treasure?” she asked.

“More like evidence.” He told her about the canoe, his father, and the river. “I used to take Rachel out sometimes, after my dad was gone. It was the only thing we did where neither of us had to decide where we were going.”

“Did she like it?”

“She was terrible at steering.”

“I’m sure she’d be pleased to hear you say that.”

“She knew. She used to say the canoe had a personal grudge against her.”

Isabel picked up the chipped key and examined it. “Do you miss the river?”

The question seemed simple. The answer wasn’t.

“I miss being on it,” he said at last. “Not who I was there with. Just… that feeling.”

“Then let’s go.”

They found a canoe rental place online and booked for the following Sunday. On the drive, Daniel worried that the idea was too earnest, or too much like a symbolic gesture in a film. Isabel turned the radio down and asked him to tell her more about his father. So he did.

The river was broad and brown beneath a pale sky. Their rented canoe was green and scratched. Isabel climbed in first, holding the sides with both hands.

“This feels extremely stable,” she said.

“It’s not. But it looks stable.”

“That’s not what you want to hear from your guide.”

“I’m not a guide.”

“Then I’m glad we’ve clarified that.”

They pushed off. For the first few minutes they paddled in opposite rhythms, turning the canoe in a slow, undignified circle. Isabel laughed, and Daniel laughed too. He showed her how to dip the paddle and draw it back without chopping at the water. She tried, missed, and splashed his sleeve.

“Personal grudge,” she said.

“Exactly.”

They found their rhythm eventually—not perfectly, but enough to move forward. The river carried them beneath willow branches and past gardens where washing stirred on the lines. Daniel felt the old habit of wanting to control the boat, to correct every small drift. Then he remembered his father’s hand over his own.

Feel what the water’s doing. Then decide.

He let the canoe turn slightly with the current. Isabel adjusted her stroke to match his.

For a while neither of them spoke. The silence was no longer the kind that closed a room. It was simply space around them: water, sky, the quiet dip of paddles.

On the way home, Isabel rested her hand on the console between them. Daniel took it.

That night he dreamed again. He climbed the crooked stairs, past the extra landings, to the door marked four. This time there was no key in his pocket. The door stood open.

Inside was not an apartment, but a narrow room full of afternoon light. No furniture, no Rachel, no sign that anyone had ever lived there. Daniel stood in the doorway, waiting to feel what he was supposed to feel.

Then he heard water moving somewhere beyond the walls.

He woke before dawn. Isabel slept beside him, one hand curled near his shoulder. He did not wake her. He lay still, listening to the ordinary sounds of the building, and understood that the flat did not need to contain an answer. It had only been a place his mind made for the past: empty, because he had already carried everything worth keeping out of it.

In the morning, he told Isabel about the dream over coffee.

“Did you go in?” she asked.

“Just stood in the doorway.”

“Sounds like progress.”

“Maybe.”

She leaned against him. He put an arm around her, not to hold her in place, but to feel her there.

Outside, the day opened slowly. On the table between them lay the old keys, bright with use where his fingers had turned them. Daniel could not return to the river he had known as a boy, or to the marriage he had once imagined would last. But the water was still there, and so was Isabel, and there were plenty of mornings left in which they might choose a direction together.
