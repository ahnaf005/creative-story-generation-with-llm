### The Unused Flat

The flat had no furniture, but Daniel always knew where the kitchen was.

It lay at the end of a narrow corridor, past a room with a window that looked onto a brick wall. The kitchen had a cracked sink and a single bare bulb. Sometimes Rachel stood there, though never in the same place twice. Sometimes she was outside on the landing, turning a key in the lock. In the dream Daniel would call her name, and she would look up as if he’d interrupted her.

Then he woke, usually before dawn, with the peculiar conviction that he had forgotten something important.

The flat had been appearing for months. Rachel, his ex-wife, had been in some of the dreams, though they had divorced seven years ago and last spoken about anything personal nearly as long. The flat itself was new. Daniel had never rented one like it, never visited one, never seen that long dim corridor. And yet he knew, in the way a dream insists you know, that it belonged to him.

He tried not to tell Isabel. Dreams were private, weren’t they? Not because they meant anything—he didn’t believe that—but because their logic was so flimsy. You could spend a whole morning explaining a dream and end up sounding like a man with a secret.

But Isabel noticed the mornings.

“You’re awake before the alarm again,” she said one Thursday. She was pulling on her socks at the edge of the bed, her hair sticking up on one side. “Is something wrong?”

“Nothing.”

“Daniel.”

He should have been able to leave it there. Instead he watched the grey light gather at the window and said, “I keep dreaming about Rachel.”

Isabel paused. Not dramatically. Just long enough for him to hear the quiet house around them.

“Okay,” she said. “What happens?”

The ease of the question unsettled him more than suspicion might have. “There’s a flat. I’m in it, but it’s empty. Sometimes Rachel’s there. Sometimes she’s just at the door.”

“Do you know the flat?”

“No.”

“Do you want to be there?”

He almost said, Obviously not. But the word stuck. He did want to know what was in it. He wanted to see the whole place, every room, even the ones the dream kept hiding.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Isabel nodded, accepting the answer without pretending it was complete. “Maybe your brain’s filing old things in a new folder.”

“Sounds efficient.”

“Mine’s more of a laundry basket.”

He smiled, relieved to have been given something ordinary. Isabel had been with him for four years. She knew his silences, though not all their causes. He had thought this was one of the things they were good at: not prying, not turning each other into a problem to solve. But lately their evenings had become careful. Work, dinner, the odd programme they half-watched. A life held together by pleasant habits, like a room nobody quite used.

That night, after he told her more—the bare bulb, the brick wall, Rachel’s expression—Isabel sat cross-legged on the sofa and let the silence sit between them.

“Rachel and I used to rent a flat above a bakery,” he said. “Before we got the house.”

“I know.”

“Nothing like this one. This one has no smell at all. That’s the strange part. Dreams usually have some sort of smell, don’t they?”

“Maybe you’re underselling the bakery.”

It was true: the old flat had smelled of scorched sugar by seven in the morning, and of damp plaster whenever it rained. There had been a window in the bedroom that stuck halfway open. Rachel used to wedge a folded postcard into the gap. He remembered her there in the pale morning light, saying they should buy a plant, though she was hopeless with plants. He remembered carrying the plant home and watching it die by inches.

A marriage could be made of small, nearly invisible things. A window that wouldn’t shut. A plant watered too often. A long list of ways to get something wrong without ever intending to.

“Do you dream about your ex?” he asked.

“Occasionally.” Isabel turned the mug in her hands. “Not in a romantic way. In a ‘why did I let him choose the paint colour’ way.”

“What colour?”

“Orange. Not a good orange.”

Daniel laughed, and she smiled. Then her face shifted into thought.

“I used to be frightened that if I talked about him, you’d think I still wanted him,” she said. “I don’t. But I did want parts of that time. The travelling, the feeling that everything was starting. Not him, exactly. Just… me, then.”

Daniel looked down at his hands. So that was allowed, then. To miss a version of yourself without wanting your old life back. He had never made that distinction. He had treated every memory of Rachel like evidence in a trial.

“You miss who you were,” he said.

“Sometimes. Do you?”

He wanted to say no. It came so quickly that he knew it wasn’t the whole answer. “I miss being able to tell what came next.”

Isabel reached out and touched his wrist. “That sounds exhausting.”

It was kind of her to say so. It was also, somehow, a little sad. They were sitting together, but he had the sense that they were each facing a different window.

The dreams went on. The flat was sometimes larger than he remembered, with rooms opening off the corridor. Once there was a narrow balcony. Once Rachel stood in the kitchen with her hand on the bare counter and said, “You’ve left it too long.” He woke before he could ask what she meant.

He did not tell Isabel about that one.

By April, the evenings had stretched. On a Sunday, Isabel announced that she was sorting the hall cupboard, a task she had been threatening for over a year. Daniel was given the top shelf and a box of unsorted things from the back.

“You’re supposed to decide what we still need,” she said.

“Define need.”

“If you haven’t looked at it in five years, it may not be a vital household object.”

He found a phone charger for a phone neither of them owned, a cracked plastic compass and a bundle of takeout menus. At the bottom of a canvas bag lay a ring of old keys. They were brass, gone dull at the edges, with a blue plastic tag. On it, in his own handwriting, were the words: OAKMERE BOATS.

He turned the keys over.

For a moment, the years fell away. A bright afternoon on the river. Isabel younger, with her hair tied up and a stripe of sunscreen across one cheek. He had taken her canoeing on their third date because he’d wanted to seem adventurous. In truth he had been nervous of the water and had paddled too hard on one side. They’d spun in slow, ridiculous circles while Isabel laughed so much she could hardly hold her paddle.

They had stopped beneath the willows and let the current carry them. She’d dipped her hand in the river. Her fingers trailed in the water, and sunlight broke around them in bright scales. They had talked about nothing urgent, nothing to settle: her favourite places, his childhood dog, the weirdest jobs they’d ever had. At some point, her bare foot bumped his beneath the seat. Neither of them moved it away.

He’d forgotten how easy it had been to be with her before they’d built a life that needed managing.

“Found something?” Isabel called from the other end of the cupboard.

“Keys to the canoe shed.”

“You kept those?”

“Apparently.”

“Do they still work?”

“No idea.”

He almost put them back in the box. The old keys were only keys, after all. But the memory had made the present feel less sealed up. It was still there, underneath the dust and old charging cables. Not a different life. This one, when it had first begun.

“Want to find out?” he asked.

Isabel appeared in the doorway, holding a stack of folded blankets. “You mean go canoeing?”

“Unless you have a stronger attachment to the blankets.”

“I do, actually. But they can wait.”

The following Saturday, they drove to Oakmere. The boat club was smaller than Daniel remembered, its shed leaning towards the river. A woman at the desk said the locks had been changed twice since the old keys were issued, but she had a key for the public racks and could let them borrow a canoe.

“Worth a try,” she said, with the same practical optimism Isabel had used.

The sky was bright and cold. Daniel carried the canoe down to the bank, trying not to remember too much about the first time. He didn’t need to perform the past. It was gone. It could stay gone.

They settled into the boat. Isabel pushed off with one hand, and the shore began to slip away.

For the first few minutes they paddled in silence. They had to work out the rhythm again: his stroke on the left, hers on the right, the boat nudging off course whenever one of them hurried. Daniel felt an old embarrassment stir in him.

“We’re doing the circle thing,” he said.

“We are,” Isabel agreed. “Perhaps it’s a tradition.”

The boat swung slowly around.

He grinned. She grinned back, and then they were laughing, just as they had years ago—not because they had recovered some lost version of themselves, but because they were here, in this ridiculous circling boat, together.

They found the right rhythm eventually. The river carried them beneath a line of willows, their new leaves trembling in the wind. Isabel dipped her fingers into the water.

“Cold,” she said.

“Sounds like the river hasn’t changed much.”

“Maybe it’s filing things in a new folder.”

He looked at her, and she turned to meet his eyes. There was nothing to confess, nothing to promise. Just the green flecks of the river in her gaze, and the small, familiar crease that appeared between her eyebrows when she was trying not to laugh.

On the way back, Daniel thought of the flat. He could not summon its corridor clearly now. The dream might return; it might not. Perhaps the place was nothing more than an unused room in his mind, waiting for him to stop treating every closed door as a warning.

The keys lay in the car, useless to the boat shed and good for nothing at all. He was glad he had kept them. Glad, too, that he had asked Isabel to come.

When they reached shore, she held out her hand. He took it, and together they pulled the canoe onto the bank. Neither of them hurried. The water went on moving behind them, carrying the afternoon forward.
