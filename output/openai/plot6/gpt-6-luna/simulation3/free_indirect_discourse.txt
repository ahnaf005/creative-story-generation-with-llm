### The Rooms Between Us

By October, Mara had learned that the student union had invisible borders. The long tables by the windows belonged to the rowing team; the sofas near the vending machines to people who looked as if they had invented the band everyone else was listening to. The student newspaper occupied a table in the middle, as though it had been granted authority over the whole room.

Carl sat there most afternoons, leaning back in his chair as if the place had been built around him. He could make a person feel chosen merely by remembering their coffee order. Mara found this both irritating and difficult to resist.

“You should write for us,” he said one Tuesday, sliding a paper cup across the table. “You notice things.”

She had been trying not to notice him noticing her. “What things?”

“Who’s pretending not to be lonely, for starters.”

That sounded like a joke until she saw he meant it. She laughed anyway. The newspaper needed writers, he explained, and they were planning an issue about belonging. The subject was broad enough to be safe. It was also broad enough to swallow her whole.

Lizzy, her roommate, was delighted when Mara got an assignment. “Finally,” she said, sitting cross-legged on their kitchen counter. “Something for your opinions besides me.”

“I have opinions about you.”

“Most of them flattering, I hope.”

Mara tried writing about the campus’s grand, expensive buildings and the students who slept in the library because they couldn’t afford the rent. She wrote a paragraph about the student rooms where she’d spent her first year: the damp patch above her bed, the radiator that clanked through the night, the way everyone hung photographs over peeling paint. Then, without deciding to, she wrote about her mother’s flat after the landlord sold it. How her mother had packed the kitchen into cardboard boxes while Mara kept insisting they could find somewhere better. How, at nineteen, Mara had gone away to university and called that leaving home a triumph.

She stopped there. The page felt too much like a door she’d opened by mistake.

Lizzy found the draft on their shared desk. Mara had left it under a stack of lecture notes, which was not the same as hiding it. Still, she wished Lizzy hadn’t read it.

“It’s beautiful,” Lizzy said that night. She stood by Mara’s bed, holding the pages carefully, as if they were something hot. “You should let Carl see this.”

“It isn’t for the paper.”

“Why not?”

“Because I wrote it for me.”

Lizzy nodded, too quickly. “Right. Of course.”

The next morning Carl asked her to come by the newspaper office. His office was a converted storeroom with a window overlooking a brick wall. On the desk lay her pages, smoothed flat.

Mara stared at them. “Lizzy gave you those?”

“She thought they were too good to leave in a drawer.” He spoke gently, as if gentleness could make the decision belong to both of them. “We’d run it in the belonging issue. With your name.”

“I said it wasn’t for the paper.”

“I know. I’m sorry. But I think you’d be glad it was out there.”

He looked at her while he said it. She wanted to be the sort of person who could answer at once. Instead, she stood very still and tried to identify the person he expected her to become: brave, grateful, a little embarrassed by her own privacy.

“Can I think about it?”

“Of course.” He smiled. “We’re lucky to have you.”

She left without taking the pages. It was easier to pretend that she had not yet chosen.

Johan was waiting outside the newspaper office with a camera strap looped around his wrist. He took photographs for the arts section and seemed to know how to stand at the edge of a crowd without looking excluded.

“You look like you’ve been handed a bill,” he said.

Mara managed a smile. “Maybe I have.”

He didn’t ask what it was for. That was what she liked about him, at first. He left space around questions. They started walking to the library together, then to dinner, then to a small bar where he told her about his father’s collection of broken radios. Their closeness gathered almost invisibly. One day she noticed that when something happened—a ridiculous lecture, a spectacularly bad cup of coffee—she was saving it to tell him.

Carl, meanwhile, had become attentive. He brought her tea and asked how she was feeling about the essay. He stood close enough in the newspaper office that she could smell the orange soap on his hands. Nothing had ever happened between them, not quite. A kiss at the end of a party, a hand on her shoulder left there too long; small things she could dismiss separately and add up whenever she felt lonely.

She still hadn’t told him no.

On Thursday, the belonging issue appeared in the newspaper racks. Her essay was on the front page.

It had been shortened. The title was now “The Art of Leaving.” The last paragraph, about going to university and calling it a triumph, had become its headline point: *I left home and learned to be grateful for the distance.* She hadn’t written that sentence. She would never have written that sentence. Her mother had called her the previous night to say she’d found somewhere new to live. Mara could still hear the strain in her voice, underneath the cheerful list of rooms and windows.

The essay was signed with Mara’s full name.

People stopped her on the stairs to tell her it was moving. One girl said she understood exactly what Mara meant about leaving home. A boy in her seminar said he liked the honesty, as if honesty were a coat she had chosen to wear.

Mara found Carl in the newsroom. “You published it.”

His smile faded. “I thought you’d decided.”

“I didn’t.”

“You didn’t tell me not to.”

She almost laughed. There it was, the neat little gap where he had stored her silence and called it permission.

“You had the draft before I’d agreed.”

“Lizzy thought—”

“Don’t put this on Lizzy.”

But Lizzy was already in the doorway behind him. She looked stricken, which somehow made Mara angrier. She had expected the satisfaction of being betrayed to look clean and bright. Instead, it felt like indigestion.

“I thought you wanted people to read it,” Lizzy said.

“You knew I didn’t.”

“I thought you were scared. I thought if Carl—”

“If Carl decided for me, I’d be grateful?”

Lizzy’s face tightened. “I was trying to help.”

Mara knew she had believed it. That was the worst part. Lizzy had always been good at helping people become the version of themselves she admired.

Behind the desk, Martin, the copy editor, kept his eyes on his hands. He had checked the proofs. He must have seen the sentence Carl had added.

“Did you know?” Mara asked him.

He looked up, then down again. “I knew it was going to print.”

The room seemed to shrink around them. “And?”

“I thought you’d said yes.”

“You thought that because it was easier than asking.”

Martin’s silence answered for him.

Outside, rain had slicked the paths and made the buildings shine. Mara walked without an umbrella. She felt exposed, but not in the way the essay had exposed her. This was more like being angry in public, an embarrassing and useful thing.

Johan found her sitting beneath the library overhang. He had an umbrella, though he held it closed.

“I read it,” he said.

“Everyone has.”

“I’m sorry.”

She watched the rain run off the stone step. “It isn’t even all wrong. That’s what’s bothering me.”

He waited.

“I did leave. I did want the distance. But they made it sound like leaving was a clean thing. Like my mother was a place I escaped from.”

“Do you want to change the piece?”

“I want to stop having to explain the piece.”

“Fair.”

He sat beside her. Not too close. “You could tell them that’s not what you meant. You could also tell them nothing.”

She wanted him to say that she was right, that Carl was awful, that Lizzy had always been selfish. A more decisive sort of comfort. But Johan was looking at her as if her choice was still hers, which was maddening. Also kind.

The next morning, Mara asked the newspaper to print a correction and a new version of the essay. Carl told her there wasn’t room in the next issue.

“Then make room in the online edition,” she said. Her voice wavered, but the words arrived intact. “And put my note before it. I want readers to know I didn’t approve the first version.”

Carl rubbed his forehead. “You’re making this sound like I stole it.”

“You published something I told you wasn’t yours.”

For once, he didn’t have a ready answer. He looked tired, or perhaps only inconvenienced. Mara felt the old pull to soften the moment, to laugh it off before anyone could be angry with her. She let it pass. It went by without taking her with it.

Martin agreed to add the correction. Lizzy apologized over dinner that night, then cried. Mara listened, though she didn’t reach across the table to comfort her. The apology mattered; so did the space before she could accept it. A friendship, she was learning, could survive the knowledge that its other person had wanted to be right more than they had wanted to ask.

Johan walked her back to their building afterward. On the steps he said, “I’m glad you said something.”

“So am I.”

He touched her hand. She let him. Then she turned her palm upward and took his.

It wasn’t a grand declaration. No one applauded. The campus carried on around them, students hurrying through the rain with their bags held over their heads, each person going somewhere they had chosen or somewhere they had learned to call inevitable.

Later, Mara rewrote the essay alone in the library. She wrote about the flat her mother had found, with its narrow windows and the small square of sunlight that crossed the kitchen floor at noon. She wrote that leaving could be a victory and a grief, and sometimes an ordinary thing you did because the bus was coming. She wrote that distance did not make love easier to measure.

At the bottom, she put her name.

This time, it fit.
