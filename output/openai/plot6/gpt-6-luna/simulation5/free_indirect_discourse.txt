By October, Mara had learned that the student union café had territories.

The table beneath the cracked clock belonged to the theater people, though they rarely discussed theater. The long bench by the window belonged to Carl and whichever person he was currently persuading to join something. Martin occupied the corner near the radiator, where he could listen to everyone and be mistaken for reading. Lizzy claimed the whole room, in the way she claimed borrowed sweaters and the last word in an argument.

Mara had no territory. She moved between them, carrying her coffee from one table to another, telling herself this was flexibility and not fear.

Carl called it being good with people. He liked her that way—easy to introduce, easy to include, easy to kiss at a party and then leave talking to someone else. She liked him for the same reasons, at first. He could make a crowded room feel like it had opened just for her. Naturally, she had thought that meant something.

“Come on,” he said one afternoon, dropping into the chair beside her. “The Lantern’s doing a live reading next week. We need something honest.”

Mara glanced up from her notebook. “You need something people will clap for.”

“Those can be the same thing.”

He smiled. He was good at smiling as though a disagreement were a game he’d already decided they were both enjoying.

She had been writing a piece about the first month of university: the noise in the halls, the unfamiliar confidence of everyone else, the homesickness she pretended was just tiredness. She hadn’t meant to show it to anybody. She certainly hadn’t meant for Carl, editor of the student magazine and her boyfriend since September, to call it “a proper essay.”

“You should read it,” he said. “At the event.”

“No.”

“Not the whole thing, then. Just the bit about your dad.”

Her pen stopped. She had mentioned her father once, late at night, while they lay tangled in her narrow bed. He had called after midnight to ask if she was eating properly. Mara had felt embarrassed by the tenderness in his voice, and by Carl hearing it.

“You remember that?”

“I remember everything you tell me.”

How flattering. How dangerous, apparently.

Across the room, Martin looked over his book. He had been Mara’s friend since orientation, when both of them had gotten lost looking for the lecture hall and pretended it was on purpose. He raised an eyebrow at Carl. Careful, it said. Or perhaps it said, Don’t let him have it. With Martin, the trouble was that his expressions were easy to interpret whichever way you wanted.

Lizzy arrived carrying three plates of chips. “No one asked me, but I think live readings are where private feelings go to die.”

Carl laughed. “You’re welcome to read.”

“Absolutely not. I have standards.”

“You have a grudge,” said Martin.

“I can have two things.”

Mara laughed with them, grateful for the escape, and then felt ridiculous for being grateful. There. The whole café was a map of small loyalties. She was forever checking where she stood.

That night, she sent Carl the essay.

He had asked to help edit it. He was her boyfriend. It was only a piece of writing. What else was love, if not letting someone see the unpolished parts?

The next morning, a message appeared in the magazine’s group chat: **Mara’s reading is going to be the heart of the event.** Carl had attached a paragraph from her essay. Not the paragraph about her father, but one about sitting alone in the dining hall and wondering whether everyone else had been given instructions she’d missed.

The words looked different on a screen. Too neat. Too exposed.

She messaged him privately. *I said you could read it, not share it.*

His reply came at once. *I know. Sorry. I was excited. It’s good, Mara.*

Good. As if quality settled the question.

She considered telling him she was angry, then imagined the conversation: Carl explaining, Mara softening, both of them deciding she had made a fuss over nothing. She closed the chat instead.

At the event, the lecture room filled early. Lizzy sat in the front row, arms folded, looking prepared to defend Mara from the entire university. Martin stood at the back by the door, a paper cup in one hand. Johan, who took photographs for the magazine, crouched near the stage adjusting his camera.

He had a quiet way of looking at people. Not the way Carl looked, as if he were already imagining what they could become. Johan looked as though the person in front of him had arrived complete.

“You nervous?” he asked, checking the camera.

“Not yet.”

“That’s lucky. It usually comes afterward.”

She smiled. “Good to know.”

When Carl introduced her, he called the essay “brave” and “beautifully vulnerable.” Mara walked to the microphone. The word vulnerable landed on her skin like cold water.

She’d planned to read the paragraph about the dining hall. She’d even practiced it in the mirror, stopping whenever her voice sounded too much like she was asking for forgiveness. Now the paper in her hand seemed thin and foolish. Why was it so hard to say no? Why did everyone keep assuming a page of words belonged to whoever had the confidence to carry it into a room?

She set the paper down.

“I was going to read something I wrote,” she said. “But it’s not ready to be shared.”

Someone in the audience shifted. Carl’s face tightened, then smoothed into a supportive smile. She knew that smile. It said, We’ll discuss this later, when you’re less emotional.

Mara gripped the edge of the lectern. “I thought being honest meant telling people everything. I don’t think that’s true anymore. Sometimes honesty is saying, ‘This is mine.’”

There was a pause. Not a dramatic pause, though it felt long enough for everyone to judge her. Then Lizzy clapped, loudly. A few others joined in. Mara stepped away from the microphone before she could talk herself into making a joke.

Outside, the air smelled of wet leaves and the bus fumes from the main road. Carl followed her down the steps.

“You could’ve warned me,” he said.

“I didn’t know I was going to do it.”

“Right. Well. It made me look ridiculous.”

There it was. She had been hoping he would ask if she was all right. How predictable of her.

“Your feelings aren’t the only ones in the room,” she said.

He blinked. “I was trying to help you.”

“I know you think you were.”

He looked almost hurt, and for a second she wanted to take it back. This was the part she hated: the sensation of holding a boundary while someone else suffered. Maybe love was supposed to feel like opening a door. But she had been leaving every door open, and somehow she was still alone in the house.

Carl left first. He didn’t slam the door; he never did anything so simple.

Martin waited by the bike racks. “I should tell you something.”

Mara felt her stomach tighten. “That sounds promising.”

He looked down at his paper cup. “I sent Carl your draft.”

For a moment she could hear nothing but the traffic.

“Why?”

“He said he was worried you’d back out. He wanted to encourage you. I thought if he saw it, he’d understand how much it mattered to you.”

“You thought that?”

“I know. It was stupid.”

“You knew it was private.”

“Yes.”

“Did you want him to read it because you wanted to help me, or because you wanted him to mess up?”

Martin’s mouth opened. His silence answered first.

“I don’t know,” he said. “Maybe both. I hate that you let him decide what you’re allowed to do.”

“And you decided for me instead.”

He flinched. It was a small satisfaction, and she wished she hadn’t felt it.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She believed him. That didn’t make the hurt smaller. “I need you to understand that saying sorry doesn’t put it back where it was.”

“I do.”

She hoped he did. She couldn’t make herself comfort him just because he looked miserable. That, too, was new.

Lizzy came out of the building and took one look at them. “Do I need to hit someone?”

“No,” Mara said.

“Good. I wore the wrong shoes.”

She looped an arm through Mara’s and steered her away. Johan followed a little distance behind, camera hanging at his side. He didn’t ask what had happened. For once, nobody demanded that she turn the evening into a story they could understand.

The next day, Carl left a note beneath her door: *I miss you. Can we talk?* She read it twice, waiting for longing to appear in the familiar handwriting. Something appeared, but it wasn’t longing. It was grief, maybe, for the version of them she’d believed in.

She folded the note and put it in her desk.

Martin sat beside her in the library later that week, leaving a careful chair’s worth of space between them. He didn’t apologize again. He asked if she wanted company. She said yes, and they worked in quiet. Trust, Mara discovered, did not return like a lost scarf—one quick, grateful reunion. It came back in smaller pieces, if it came back at all.

Johan found her outside the art building after class. “I’m photographing the river tomorrow,” he said. “Not for the magazine. Just because it’s there. Want to come?”

“Will you take my picture?”

“Only if you ask me to.”

The answer made her smile. She had thought affection meant being noticed. Maybe it could also mean being allowed to choose how.

“Then I’ll come,” she said.

They walked toward the bus stop together, not touching. The campus was still a shifting arrangement of glances and invitations, rumors and old hurts. The café would still have its territories. Carl would still be there, and Martin, and Lizzy, and all the versions of herself she had been trying to make acceptable to them.

But she didn’t have to belong everywhere. She could leave a sentence unfinished. She could keep a story to herself until it was hers to tell.

The thought was small, and it held.
