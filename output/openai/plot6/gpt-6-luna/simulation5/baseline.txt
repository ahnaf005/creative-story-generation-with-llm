## The Names We Keep

By the second week of university, Mara had learned that every room had its own weather.

The lecture hall was cold with ambition. The student café was warm with gossip. In the theatre basement, where the pipes knocked overhead, people behaved as if a secret had already been shared and were only waiting to hear which one.

Mara was still learning what kind of person she was in each place.

Her roommate, Lizzy, seemed to know already. She wore red lipstick to morning lectures and could ask a stranger for a cigarette, a favor, or a confession without changing her tone. Carl, who acted in the student theatre, made everyone feel as if he’d been waiting for them to arrive. Martin, who organized the university’s arts festival, knew who held the keys to every room and who could persuade them to lend one. Johan ran the lights and sound. He spoke rarely, but when he did, he seemed to have listened to the whole conversation first.

Mara liked all four of them for different reasons. She liked Lizzy’s nerve, Carl’s attention, Martin’s competence, and Johan’s quiet.

She liked herself best when they were all in the same room.

At home, she had been the girl who didn’t mind being overlooked. At university, she discovered how tiring it was to pretend she didn’t mind.

Carl first kissed her after a late rehearsal, beneath the blue EXIT sign at the back of the theatre. They had spent the evening making fun of Martin’s color-coded festival schedule. Carl had laughed at Mara’s jokes as if they were better than anyone else’s, and later, when the others had gone, he asked whether she always hid the good stories until the end.

She told herself that was why she kissed him back.

For a few weeks, they moved through campus like a pair of people who had agreed not to name what they were. Carl held her hand beneath the table in the café, then let go when his friends arrived. He texted her after midnight and forgot to answer her in the daylight. When she asked if he wanted to come to dinner with her, he said, “Let’s see what the week does to us.”

She understood what he meant. She disliked herself for accepting it.

“You don’t have to audition for people who already like you,” Lizzy told her one night, while brushing mascara from her eyelashes.

“I’m not auditioning.”

Lizzy looked at her in the mirror. “That’s what makes it convincing.”

Mara laughed, but the words stayed with her.

Martin was preparing an event for the festival called *Voices from Here*: short performances made from students’ writing and stories. He asked Mara to contribute. She said she didn’t have anything.

“You’re always writing,” Lizzy said.

She was right. Mara filled notebooks with fragments: overheard remarks, descriptions of strangers, sentences she couldn’t say aloud. One piece was about her mother, who cleaned lecture rooms at a college before Mara left for university. It described the way her mother used to wipe the board after the students had gone, leaving the faint chalk ghosts of arguments behind.

The piece was not only about her mother. It was about Mara’s fear that she had brought the wrong life with her—that other students could see, in the way she ate or dressed or spoke, the place she came from and the fact that she wanted to leave it.

She showed the piece to Lizzy on a wet Sunday afternoon.

“Don’t show anyone,” Mara said.

“I won’t.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

A few days later, Martin stopped Mara outside the theatre.

“Lizzy said you had something for the festival,” he said. “It’s good. We could do it anonymously, if you want.”

Mara felt a sudden, sour heat in her face. “She gave it to you?”

“She said she thought you might be too nervous to submit it yourself.” Martin paused. “I can take it out of the programme.”

He looked sincerely sorry. That made it harder to be angry.

Mara should have said yes. Instead, she pictured the piece disappearing back into her notebook, unseen and safe. She heard her own old excuses lining up: not ready, not good enough, not the right time.

“Keep it anonymous,” she said. “And let me approve whoever reads it.”

Martin nodded. “Of course.”

Carl was cast to perform it. He told Mara after rehearsal, grinning as though he’d received a present.

“I think I can make this one work,” he said.

“You know it’s mine?”

His grin faltered. “I didn’t, at first. Martin told me it was anonymous. I thought it was powerful.”

“And now?”

“Now I think it’s still powerful.”

There was something honest in his answer, and something that made her feel small. She wanted him to ask whether she was comfortable. She wanted him to already know.

“Don’t change it,” she said.

“I wouldn’t.”

The night of the performance, the theatre was crowded. Students sat on the floor and leaned against the walls. Mara sat beside Lizzy, whose lipstick had left a red mark on her water glass. Martin paced at the back, checking the running order. Johan sat behind the sound desk, one hand resting on the sliders.

Carl walked onto the stage.

He began with the piece’s opening line, about a woman wiping a blackboard clean. But he had changed the rhythm. He added a pause where Mara had written a comma, and softened a line she had meant to be sharp. It sounded, suddenly, more beautiful than it had on the page.

Then he reached the part about the woman folding her cleaning cloth and placing it in the pocket of her blue coat.

Mara’s mother had worn that coat for years. The detail was small, but it belonged to her. Mara could feel the room noticing the truth of it—the way an audience leans forward when it senses the writer has stepped too close.

Carl looked up. His eyes found Mara’s.

The applause came before she was ready. People turned to see her. Martin was smiling, relieved. Lizzy touched Mara’s arm.

Mara stood and left.

Outside, the rain had stopped. She walked until the theatre’s light was gone behind her, then sat on a low stone wall by the closed library. She was angry with Carl for changing the piece, with Martin for not asking, with Lizzy for sending it, and with herself for agreeing. She was also angry that some part of her had been pleased by the applause.

Johan found her there a few minutes later. He didn’t ask if she was all right.

“Do you want company or do you want me to go?” he asked.

Mara wiped her cheeks with the heel of her hand. “Company. But don’t tell me it was brave.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

He sat beside her without touching her. After a while, she said, “I thought if they saw it, they’d see me.”

“Did they?”

“I don’t know. They saw the part of me I gave them.”

Johan nodded. “That’s not the same thing.”

When she returned to the theatre, Carl was waiting in the corridor.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I thought you’d want people to hear it.”

“You thought I’d want them to hear the version you made.”

“I didn’t change much.”

“That’s not the point.”

He looked at her, ready with another answer, then seemed to reconsider. Mara loved him, she realized—not completely, and not wisely, but enough that being disappointed in him felt like discovering a bruise. She had loved how bright she felt beside him. She had mistaken that brightness for being known.

“I should have asked,” he said.

“Yes.”

He reached for her hand. She let him take it for a moment, then pulled away.

Lizzy waited in their room. She had taken off her lipstick and looked younger without it.

“I thought you were hiding it because you were scared,” Lizzy said. “I wanted them to hear it.”

“You decided for me.”

“I know.”

“You told me you wouldn’t.”

Lizzy looked down. “I’m sorry.”

Mara wanted the apology to erase the hurt. It didn’t. She wanted to tell Lizzy that this was the end of their friendship. She couldn’t. Lizzy was still the person who knew which tea Mara drank when she had a headache, and how she went quiet when she was trying not to cry. Both things could be true: Lizzy loved her, and Lizzy had crossed a line.

The next morning, Mara met Martin in the festival office.

“I want the piece withdrawn,” she said.

Martin nodded. “I can do that.”

“I also want to read a new version at the closing event. Under my name.”

He raised his eyebrows. “Are you sure?”

“No.” Mara took a breath. “But I want the choice to be mine.”

She called her mother that evening. She expected her to be hurt that Mara had written about her, or proud that the story had been performed. Instead, her mother asked what the story said.

“It says you cleaned the lecture rooms,” Mara replied. “It says you wore your blue coat.”

“I did wear that coat.”

“I made it sound like you were lonely.”

“Was I?”

Mara thought. She could picture her mother under the white lights of the lecture hall, sweeping up biscuit crumbs and listening to the pipes knock. She could picture the same woman at home, singing along to the radio while she cooked.

“Sometimes,” Mara said. “But not always.”

“Good,” her mother said. “Don’t make me into a lesson.”

Mara revised the piece. She left in the blackboard and the coat, but she added herself: the daughter who had wanted to be ashamed of the cleaning cloth, the daughter who had resented her mother’s quiet, the daughter who now understood that leaving home had not made her the only one with a complicated life.

On the night of the closing event, she stood backstage with her pages trembling in her hands. Martin gave her a brief nod from the wings. Lizzy waited near the front row, without lipstick. Carl stood at the back, his expression unreadable. Johan was at the sound desk, eyes on her.

Mara stepped into the light.

She said her name first.

Then she read.

Her voice shook at the beginning. It steadied when she stopped trying to sound like someone who wasn’t afraid. The audience was silent, but she no longer mistook silence for judgment. It was only silence: a room giving her space to continue.

When she finished, people clapped. Some looked at her. Some looked away. She let them.

Outside afterward, Lizzy asked if they could talk.

“Not tonight,” Mara said. Then, because it was true, she added, “But soon.”

Lizzy nodded. “Soon.”

Carl approached, hands in his pockets. “It was yours,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry I made it mine for a moment.”

Mara believed that he was sorry. She also understood that forgiveness was not the same as returning to where they had been. She squeezed his fingers once, gently, and let go.

Johan was waiting by the steps. He didn’t ask what she wanted the evening to mean. They walked toward the dorms together, shoulders almost touching.

For the first time since arriving at university, Mara wasn’t trying to discover which version of herself would be most welcome. She was tired, embarrassed, still in love with someone she could not trust in the same way, and unsure whether her closest friend had earned her forgiveness.

She was also, at last, the person who had chosen what to say—and when.
