### The Quiet Between Headlines

By October, Mara knew that every student at Bellweather University belonged to at least three different versions of themselves.

There was the version who sat in lecture, the version who queued for coffee, and the version who appeared in the campus newspaper beside a photograph someone had taken without asking. Mara had come to Bellweather hoping to become a person who knew what she thought. Instead, she became a person who knew where everyone else was supposed to be.

Carl was usually in the newsroom, one foot on a desk, talking as if the next brilliant idea had just occurred to him. He was editor of *The Bell*, quick-witted and handsome in the careless way of someone who never checked his reflection because he expected it to approve. He was also Mara’s boyfriend, though neither of them had used that word lately.

Lizzy was her roommate, closest friend, and chief supplier of borrowed sweaters. She could make even a bad day feel like a story they would laugh about later. Martin, who edited copy at the newspaper, had been kind to Mara since the first week, when she’d accidentally sent a draft full of highlighted notes to the entire staff. He corrected her commas without making her feel foolish.

And Johan was the person Mara kept meaning to interview properly.

He was a third-year student who organized protests against the university’s plan to convert the cheapest dormitory into “premium independent living.” He had a calm, watchful face, and he listened as if he didn’t need to prepare his reply while someone was still speaking. The first time Mara met him, he was taping a hand-lettered sign to a campus door.

“Are you going to ask me about the housing campaign,” he said, “or just take a picture of me looking tragic?”

“I’m here to ask about the housing campaign.”

“Good. I’m terrible at looking tragic.”

Mara laughed. She liked him before she had decided whether she was allowed to.

Her article began with a student named Priya, who had been told to leave her dorm room at the end of the term. Priya’s scholarship covered tuition but not the new housing fees. She spoke softly, as if the walls might report her. Johan explained the proposed changes and the student union’s demands. Mara spent a week checking figures, attending meetings, and taking notes until her fingers cramped.

One night, after a long interview, she told Lizzy how much the story had unsettled her. Mara’s mother had been working extra shifts to help cover her own rent back home. Mara had never told anyone at Bellweather how close she’d come to turning down her place because she couldn’t afford the deposit.

“I’m scared that if I write it honestly, people will decide I don’t belong here,” she said.

Lizzy put down her mug. “You belong anywhere you can make it through a Tuesday.”

Mara laughed, but she was grateful. She trusted Lizzy with the thought because Lizzy made it feel safe to have one.

The next day Carl read the first draft and told Mara it was strong, but not ready.

“We need the finance office to confirm the numbers,” he said. “And Johan’s claim about the planned rent increase needs another source. Give me a day.”

Mara was relieved. She wanted the story to be fair. She also wanted Carl to tell her she had done well. He kissed her forehead as he left the newsroom, and she pretended not to notice that he hadn’t kissed her properly in weeks.

Two days later, the new issue of *The Bell* appeared across campus.

The lead story was about the housing changes. It opened with Priya’s situation, described in the same careful detail Mara had gathered. It quoted Johan. It used a metaphor Mara had written in her draft about “a door that closed quietly because the person outside was expected not to make a fuss.”

The byline was Lizzy’s.

Mara read it once, standing by the library entrance. Then again, sitting on the cold steps. On the third reading, the words blurred.

When she reached the dorm, Lizzy was at their desk, painting her nails.

“You wrote it,” Mara said.

Lizzy looked up. “Mara—”

“You used my interviews.”

“I didn’t think Carl was going to run it under my name. He said you’d stepped back.”

“I told him it needed checking.”

“I thought you didn’t want to be involved anymore.”

“You thought that?” Mara could hear her voice getting louder. She hated that. “You knew I was working on it. You knew how much I cared.”

Lizzy’s expression tightened. “I did the final reporting. Carl said it was an opportunity. I’m sorry.”

The apology landed badly, like a coat someone had tossed at her in the rain. Mara couldn’t tell whether Lizzy was sorry for what she had done or only for the fact that Mara knew.

Carl found her in the newsroom that evening. He said Lizzy had written a fresh version and that Mara’s draft had been “more of a starting point.” He said the paper couldn’t miss its deadline. He said the story was good for the students it was trying to help.

“You told me it wasn’t ready,” Mara said.

“I was trying to protect the paper.”

“From what?”

He rubbed his forehead. “From putting something out that could be challenged.”

“So you put it out under someone else’s name?”

“Mara, don’t make this personal.”

For a moment she could only stare at him. Then she understood that he had said exactly what he meant. The story was personal to her; the decision wasn’t.

Martin appeared in the doorway after Carl left. He didn’t say he’d warned her. He didn’t say he knew Carl could be like this. He just held out a printed copy of the article, folded open to the second page.

“There’s a mistake in the rent figures,” he said. “The university’s old proposal is being quoted as the new one.”

Mara sat down. “Did you know he was going to run it?”

“I knew Carl had put Lizzy on it. I thought you’d agreed.” Martin hesitated. “I should have asked you.”

The three people she had counted on had each made a different choice without her. What unsettled her most was how easily she had given them the authority to do it. She had spent months trying to be agreeable, helpful, undemanding. She had thought that if she became easy to work with, people would take her seriously. Now she wondered if she had taught them that her work could be taken.

The next morning, she found Johan outside the student union, handing out flyers. He glanced at the paper in her hand.

“You wrote this,” he said.

“I reported it. Lizzy wrote the final article.”

“Did you give her my interview?”

“No. I thought it was still being checked.”

He folded a flyer in half. “The article says we accused the university of lying about the rent increase.”

“We didn’t. The paper got the figures wrong.”

“Then correct it.”

“I will.”

He studied her. “Are you going to correct it because it’s wrong, or because you’re angry?”

Mara flinched. It was the sort of question she wished he would stop asking, and the sort she needed him to ask.

“Both,” she said.

“Good. At least that’s honest.”

They walked along the path behind the union building, where wind lifted the corners of the flyers. Johan told her the campaign had been going badly even before the article. Students were tired. Some residents were afraid that complaining would affect their housing applications. He didn’t ask her to rescue the campaign, and he didn’t tell her she was a good person underneath the mistake.

That, Mara found, was why she wanted to stay beside him.

At the newsroom, she wrote a correction. Then she wrote a note about the article’s reporting process and her own part in it: she had gathered the interviews and notes; she had not approved the final version; the rent figures were wrong. She asked Carl to publish it.

He read the draft and sighed. “This makes everyone look bad.”

“It is bad.”

“You could have talked to me first.”

“I did.”

Carl looked at her then, really looked, and for a second Mara saw the boy who had shared his scarf with her during orientation week. Then the moment passed.

“I don’t know how you expect this to work,” he said.

“I don’t know either,” she replied. “But I can’t keep pretending it already does.”

The correction ran the following day, smaller than the original article but large enough to be seen. Carl didn’t speak to her for the rest of the week. Lizzy apologized again, more quietly this time. She admitted she had been flattered when Carl offered her the byline, and ashamed to refuse it once she realized what it meant. Mara heard her out. She didn’t say everything was fine.

Martin took her to the dining hall and let her complain for twenty minutes before confessing that he had always liked her. He said it gently, with no expectation attached. Mara was grateful, and sorry, and not in love with him.

“I think you liked the idea of being the person I needed,” she said.

Martin considered this. “Maybe I did.”

It was the kindest thing he could have said.

A week later, Mara met Johan outside the library. The housing campaign had won a delay in the university vote, not a victory. He had a paper cup of coffee in each hand and gave her the warmer one.

“You’re not going to write about the campaign again?” he asked.

“I am. But this time I’m going to tell the people I’m quoting what’s in it before it runs.”

“That sounds slower.”

“It is.”

He smiled. “You always wanted to be faster.”

“I wanted everyone to think I was good.”

“And now?”

Mara looked at the cup between her hands. She thought of Priya’s lowered voice, Lizzy’s painted nails, Carl’s careful smile, Martin’s honest hesitation. She thought of all the ways she had mistaken approval for affection, and silence for patience.

“Now I want to know when I’m telling the truth,” she said.

Johan reached for her hand. She let him take it, not because he had rescued her, but because she wanted to be there. Love, she was beginning to understand, was not the prize at the end of making herself useful. It was something that asked her to arrive as she was—and let the other person do the same.

On Monday, Mara walked into the newsroom and opened a blank document. She wrote her name at the top before she wrote the headline.
