### The Map of Almost

By October, Mara had learned that the university had two maps: the one printed in the welcome brochure, with its neat paths between lecture halls, and the one everyone else carried in their heads.

That second map was harder to read. It showed which cafeteria tables belonged to which friend groups, which professors liked to be interrupted, and how long you could stand at a party holding a cup before someone noticed you had no one to talk to. Mara was still learning its symbols.

Her roommate, Lizzy, seemed to have arrived knowing them all. She could turn a stranger into a friend between the drinks table and the stairs. She also had a talent for making Mara feel included without ever quite slowing down.

“Come on,” she’d say, tugging Mara into a circle. “You’ll like everyone.”

Sometimes Mara did. Sometimes she spent the evening smiling until her cheeks hurt, then went back to their room and listened to Lizzy describe the party as if Mara hadn’t been there.

Carl was easy to like. He had a warm, attentive way of looking at people, as though what they said mattered and he had nowhere else to be. He was running for student union president and talked about the campus as if it were a problem he could solve with a good meeting and a better poster.

“You should make something about the places people don’t feel they belong,” he told Mara one afternoon. “That’s your project, right?”

Mara was studying sound and design. Her project, *The Map of Almost*, was a collection of short recordings from students describing places where they had nearly felt at home. A stairwell where someone ate dinner to avoid the crowded dining hall. A library window where a homesick student called her mother. The small, unremarkable locations where people went to be alone, or pretended not to be.

She had met Johan in the media lab. He was studying photography and spoke so little that at first Mara thought he disliked her. Then she discovered he was simply careful with words. He listened to each recording before they edited it, sometimes twice, and noticed things she missed: the click of a radiator, the quick breath before a laugh.

“It’s not just a map of places,” he told her once. “It’s a map of what people don’t say.”

Mara wrote that down, though she pretended she hadn’t.

Martin, who edited the student newspaper, had been her first friend on campus. They’d met during orientation, both stranded outside a locked lecture hall while everyone else seemed to know where they were going. But as Mara found other circles, she answered his messages less often. Martin never complained. He merely became harder to find.

When Carl asked to see the project, Mara brought him to the media lab. He listened closely, his expression softening at the recordings. Afterward, he asked if he could use one in a speech.

“Anonymously,” Mara said. “And only the section I’ve already cleared for the exhibition.”

“Of course,” Carl said. He looked directly at her. “Your work stays yours.”

Mara believed him. Believing him felt like being welcomed.

A week later, she and Johan were hanging prints in the student center when they heard Carl’s voice echoing from the lecture hall next door. He was giving a campaign speech. The doors stood open, and a knot of students had gathered in the corridor.

“We talk about community,” Carl said, “but some people feel so alone they eat dinner in a stairwell, hoping no one will see them.”

Then he added, “That’s what Mara, a first-year student, told us.”

For a second, Mara thought she had misheard. Then the room laughed gently—not at her, she knew, but at the image. A sympathetic laugh. The kind that made a private thing sound harmless.

Her hands went cold.

Johan took one step toward the doorway, then stopped. Mara stared at his back. He had heard it too. He had been there when she recorded that line, though she’d used a different name in the file. He had helped her cut the audio.

Carl glanced toward the door. When he saw her, his smile faltered.

Mara left before the speech ended.

Lizzy was waiting in their room that night, sitting cross-legged on Mara’s bed.

“Before you say anything,” Lizzy began, “I thought he had your permission.”

“You gave him the recording?”

“He asked for an approved clip. I thought you’d said yes.”

“I said he could use the cleared section. That wasn’t it.”

Lizzy looked down at her hands. “I’m sorry.”

Mara wanted to believe the apology. She also wanted to ask why Lizzy had not checked. Instead, she said, “You could have asked me.”

“I know.” Lizzy’s voice tightened. “I thought you’d be happy. Carl was going to make your project part of the campaign. I thought it meant people would finally see it.”

There it was: not cruelty, exactly. Something messier. Lizzy had wanted to help, and she had wanted to be close to the people who made things happen. Mara knew that feeling. She had wanted it too.

“Did you listen to the clip?” Mara asked.

Lizzy shook her head. “No.”

Mara turned away, ashamed of how much the answer hurt.

The next day Carl found her outside the library. He apologized quickly, then explained that he’d thought the story was already public, that he’d only wanted to show the need for better communal spaces.

“You used my name.”

“I know. I should have checked.”

“You did check,” Mara said. “You asked me. I said no to that recording.”

His expression changed. For a moment he looked less like a candidate and more like a person who had been caught doing something he had already decided was reasonable.

“I thought you meant the project generally.”

“You heard what you wanted to hear.”

Carl glanced toward the library doors, where students were coming and going. “I can clarify at the debate tonight. Say the quote was used without your—”

“Don’t turn my life into a correction on your campaign.”

He swallowed. “Then what do you want?”

Mara hadn’t known until he asked. She had expected to want an apology big enough to undo the humiliation, or a promise that he would never use her work again. But what she wanted, suddenly, was to stop being spoken for.

“I want you not to use it again,” she said. “And I want the recording back.”

“I can do that.”

She didn’t thank him.

That evening Martin came to the media lab. He asked if he could write about Carl’s use of Mara’s project in the paper.

“I don’t want to be a headline,” she said.

“Then you don’t have to be.” Martin leaned against the table, hands in his coat pockets. “I can write about consent and student campaigns without naming you.”

“Why do you care?”

He gave a small shrug. “Because it was wrong. And because I remember you telling me, in September, that you didn’t want to be the kind of person who let people decide what she was allowed to say.”

Mara stared at him. “I told you that?”

“You were sitting on the floor outside the lecture hall. You said it while we waited for the door to open.”

She had forgotten. Or had decided that the version of herself who said brave things in September didn’t count.

“Write it,” she said. “But send it to me before it runs.”

Martin nodded. He didn’t ask why she’d stopped answering his messages. She wished he would, and was grateful when he didn’t.

The debate was held in the old gym, under banners that had been hung too high to read. Mara sat at the back beside Johan. Carl was onstage, composed and bright beneath the lights. Lizzy sat several rows ahead, her shoulders stiff.

During the question period, a student asked about the campaign’s use of the audio project. Carl began to answer. Mara stood before he could finish.

The room quieted. She had not planned what to say. She had imagined a speech in the shower, another on the walk over, and lost both of them when she stood up.

“I recorded that story,” she said. Her voice sounded unfamiliar through the microphone. “I agreed to share it without my name. I didn’t agree to have my name attached. I made the project because I wanted people to talk about loneliness without having to become an example of it.”

She looked at Carl. He was watching her, but she didn’t wait for him to speak.

“I also want to say that I let people make decisions for me because I thought being easy to get along with was the same as being liked. It isn’t. And I’m going to stop pretending I don’t mind when I do.”

Her hands shook. She put them flat on the podium until they steadied.

No one applauded at first. Then someone did, and a few more joined in. The sound was modest, uncertain. Mara preferred it that way.

Afterward, Lizzy found her outside the gym.

“I should have asked,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I wanted Carl to think I was useful.” Lizzy’s eyes shone, but she didn’t look away. “And I wanted you to be proud of me for helping. I’m sorry I didn’t care enough to check whether it helped you.”

Mara thought of all the times she had made Lizzy guess what she needed, then resented her for guessing wrong.

“I should have told you how important that clip was,” she said. “But it was still yours to check.”

“I know.”

They stood in the cold, neither reaching for the other. It was not forgiveness yet. It was, perhaps, the first honest conversation they had had in weeks.

Johan waited by the bike racks, camera strap crossed over his chest.

“I should have said something in the hall,” he said.

“Yes,” Mara replied.

“I didn’t. I was afraid Carl would pull the funding for the exhibition.”

“Were you?”

“Partly.” He looked down at the pavement. “Mostly I was afraid of making a scene.”

Mara understood the temptation. She had built a life out of not making scenes. “Next time, make one.”

“I will.”

She believed he meant it. That wasn’t the same as knowing what he would do. It was enough for that night.

They walked toward the media lab together. Their shoulders brushed once, then again. Neither of them moved away. Mara felt the old habit of turning a small tenderness into a promise rise in her, and let it pass without naming it.

The next afternoon, she and Johan reopened the exhibition folder. They removed the recording Carl had used and replaced it with a new one: Mara’s own voice, recorded in the empty library.

“I used to think that if I could find the right table, the right group, the right version of myself, I would stop feeling like a visitor,” she said into the microphone. “Now I think belonging might begin when you stop auditioning.”

She listened to the playback. Her voice trembled slightly on the word *visitor*. She kept it.

When the exhibition opened, the room filled slowly. Students stopped at the listening stations, headphones covering their ears. Some smiled. Some stood very still. Lizzy came and stayed until closing. Martin published his piece without Mara’s name. Carl sent a message saying he hoped, one day, she might forgive him. She didn’t answer that night.

Near the exit, Johan was photographing a patch of sunlight on the floor. Mara stood beside him, watching the bright shape shift as clouds passed over the windows.

“Do you think this counts as a map?” he asked.

“It counts as a beginning.”

Outside, the campus paths divided in every direction. Mara still didn’t know where all of them led. For once, she didn’t feel she had to pretend otherwise.
