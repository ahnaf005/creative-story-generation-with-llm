**The Weather Inside Glass Hall**

Maya first noticed Carl because he laughed too loudly in the library.

It was the third week of autumn term, the rain had begun its daily argument with the tall windows of Glass Hall, and everyone had already settled into their roles. The law students occupied the far tables with color-coded folders. The theatre students draped themselves across chairs as if posing for invisible audiences. The philosophy students gathered near the vending machines, always mid-debate, always frowning at the snacks as though crisps were a moral failure.

Maya sat alone by the window with her laptop open and a cold coffee she had forgotten to drink. She was supposed to be writing a paper on memory and identity, but mostly she was watching rain bead and slide down the glass.

Then Carl laughed.

It was warm, reckless, and completely inappropriate for a library. Three people turned to glare. Carl only grinned wider, pushed a lock of blond hair out of his eyes, and leaned closer to the girl beside him—Lizzy Vale, who could make even a grey sweater look deliberate.

Maya knew Lizzy from their first-year seminar. Everyone did. Lizzy had the gift of arriving in a room as if the room had been waiting for her. She spoke easily, dressed effortlessly, and collected people without seeming to try.

Carl noticed Maya looking and raised his eyebrows as if they shared a secret.

She looked back at her essay, cheeks warm.

“Don’t encourage him,” said a voice from the next table.

Maya turned. Martin was sitting behind a wall of books, his dark hair falling into his eyes. He was in her literary theory class, though she had never heard him speak unless called upon. He always gave the impression of someone who had just returned from a long walk in the rain, even when dry.

“Encourage who?” Maya asked.

“Carl. He survives on attention. Like ivy. Or mould.”

From across the room, Carl whispered loudly, “I heard that.”

Martin did not look up. “Good.”

Maya smiled despite herself. It was the first time all week she had done so without thinking about it.

That afternoon became the beginning of something, though at the time she could not have said what. Carl soon wandered over under the excuse of borrowing a pen. Lizzy followed, carrying two coffees and an air of amusement. Martin pretended to keep reading, but when Carl dragged a chair around and sat backward on it, Martin sighed and closed his book.

“You’re Maya,” Lizzy said. “You made Professor Halden change his mind about Plath last week.”

“I didn’t make him.”

“You did,” said Carl. “He looked wounded.”

“He always looks wounded,” Martin murmured.

Maya laughed again, and just like that, she belonged to a table.

By October, the four of them had become a routine. Carl studied politics but cared more about student elections than actual theory. He had plans for everything: parties, campaigns, midnight trips to the river, petitions against cafeteria prices. Lizzy studied visual culture and photographed everyone with a small silver camera, catching them mid-sentence, mid-yawn, mid-lie. Martin studied literature and wrote poems he claimed were only “notes.” Maya, who studied psychology, became the one they all came to when their essays collapsed, their families called, or their hearts made trouble.

There was also Johan.

Johan did not so much join the group as orbit it. He was a final-year music student who worked evenings at the campus café, playing piano when the manager was in a generous mood. He was quiet, broad-shouldered, and careful with words. Maya met him properly one night when she stayed too late in the café, hiding from a storm and from herself.

“Closing in ten,” he said, wiping down the counter.

“Oh. Sorry.” She began gathering her notes.

“You don’t have to run. I meant it as information, not a threat.”

He brought her a tea she hadn’t ordered. Chamomile, with honey.

“You looked like you were about to declare war on that notebook,” he said.

Maya glanced at the page. She had written only one sentence: *Why do I keep becoming who people need?*

“It started it,” she said.

Johan smiled softly. “Then it deserves what it gets.”

After that, she began to notice him everywhere: in the practice rooms, at open mic nights, under the sycamores with headphones around his neck. He never demanded attention like Carl or bent a room around himself like Lizzy. But when he looked at Maya, he seemed to see her without requiring performance.

It unsettled her.

Carl was easier. Carl sent messages at midnight: *Are you awake? I’ve had a brilliant idea.* Carl pulled her into debates and introduced her to people as “Maya, the only one here with a functioning conscience.” Carl made her feel bright and chosen, especially when he leaned close and lowered his voice just for her.

“You understand me,” he told her one evening after a student council meeting, as they walked beneath the yellow campus lamps. “Everyone thinks I’m just loud.”

“You are loud.”

“But with layers.”

“Very loud layers.”

He laughed, bumping his shoulder against hers. For a moment, she thought he might take her hand.

He didn’t.

Instead he said, “Lizzy thinks I should run for union president.”

Maya ignored the small sinking feeling in her chest. “You’d probably win.”

“Would you help?”

“Of course.”

It was the sort of answer she gave too quickly.

The campaign swallowed November whole. Carl’s posters appeared in corridors and bathrooms, always showing him smiling as if caught in the act of saving the university. Lizzy designed them. Maya wrote his speeches, edited his statements, calmed him before debates, and reassured him afterward. Martin watched from a distance with increasing irritation.

“He’s using you,” Martin said one night in the library.

Maya did not look up from Carl’s manifesto. “He’s my friend.”

“Those aren’t opposites.”

“You don’t like him.”

“I like him when he’s asleep.”

“You’ve never seen him asleep.”

“I’m optimistic.”

She tried to smile, but exhaustion pressed behind her eyes. “Why are you being like this?”

Martin’s expression changed. His sarcasm fell away, leaving something bare and awkward. “Because you disappear when you’re around him.”

“That’s not true.”

“It is. You laugh at jokes you don’t find funny. You agree to things before you know if you want them. You make yourself smaller so he can feel enormous.”

Maya shut the laptop. “You don’t know me well enough to say that.”

Martin looked wounded then, genuinely. “Maybe not.”

The next few days were brittle. Martin stopped coming to their table. Lizzy noticed, of course.

“What happened with our resident storm cloud?” she asked while touching up a poster with a black marker.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing is everyone’s favorite lie.”

Maya hesitated. “He thinks Carl takes advantage of me.”

Lizzy capped the marker. “Martin thinks anyone who enjoys applause is morally corrupt.”

“Do you think Carl does?”

Lizzy’s gaze flickered toward Carl, who was across the room charming two first-years into handing out flyers.

“I think Carl wants what he wants,” she said at last. “And sometimes he forgets people aren’t ladders.”

Maya waited for more, but Lizzy only lifted her camera and took a picture of Carl mid-gesture.

Election night arrived cold and bright. Glass Hall filled with students, music, cheap wine, and nervous electricity. Carl won by a margin large enough to make him unbearable. He hugged everyone, lifted Lizzy off the ground, kissed the cheek of a stunned campaign volunteer, and finally turned to Maya.

“We did it,” he said, hands gripping her shoulders. “I couldn’t have done this without you.”

For one suspended second, she believed he meant it in the way she wanted.

Then he was pulled away by a cheering crowd.

Maya stood with a plastic cup in her hand, smiling at nothing. Across the hall, she saw Lizzy and Carl near the stairwell. Lizzy touched his tie, laughing. Carl bent his head toward her, intimate and familiar. Then they kissed.

It was quick. It was not their first.

Maya felt something inside her go silent.

She left without her coat.

Outside, the cold struck like water. She walked blindly past the library, past the chapel, toward the music building where light spilled from a basement window. Piano notes drifted into the night, slow and searching.

Johan found her sitting on the steps with her arms wrapped around herself.

“You’re freezing,” he said.

“I’m fine.”

“That’s a brave lie.”

He disappeared and returned with his coat. It smelled faintly of cedar and coffee. She put it on because refusing would have required strength she did not have.

They sat side by side while the party noise thudded faintly behind them.

“Do you want to talk?” he asked.

“No.”

“All right.”

His silence was not empty. It made room.

Eventually she said, “Have you ever mistaken being needed for being loved?”

Johan looked out across the wet pavement. “Yes.”

“What happened?”

“I became very useful. And very lonely.”

Maya closed her eyes. Tears slipped out, hot against the cold. She hated crying in front of people, but Johan did not rush to comfort her or ask for details. He only sat close enough that she did not feel abandoned.

The next morning, campus was full of Carl’s victory. His face smiled from discarded flyers trampled into puddles. Maya skipped her first lecture and went to the café, where Johan made her tea without comment.

By afternoon, Carl had sent seven messages.

*Where did you go?*  
*You okay?*  
*You vanished.*  
*Did I do something?*  
*Maya?*  
*Please don’t be weird.*  
*Can we talk?*

She answered none of them.

Lizzy found her in the photography darkroom, though Maya had no reason to be there except that it was dim and quiet. Red light washed Lizzy’s face, making her look like a beautiful ghost.

“I owe you an apology,” Lizzy said.

Maya leaned against the counter. “For kissing him?”

“For not telling you.”

“How long?”

Lizzy looked down. “Since the summer workshop. Before you and I really became friends.”

The word *really* stung.

“Did everyone know?”

“No. Martin guessed. Johan maybe. Carl didn’t want it public during the campaign. He thought it would complicate things.”

Maya laughed once, sharply. “For him.”

“Yes,” Lizzy said. “For him.”

It would have been easier if Lizzy had been cruel. Maya wanted arrogance, excuses, some polished line she could hate. But Lizzy stood there with wet eyes and chemicals staining her fingers, looking younger than usual.

“You knew I liked him,” Maya said.

“I suspected.”

“And you let me help him. You both did.”

Lizzy swallowed. “I told myself it wasn’t my secret to tell.”

“That’s convenient.”

“It was cowardly.”

The honesty left Maya nowhere to put her anger. It moved through her anyway, scorching and necessary.

“I don’t know how to be your friend right now,” she said.

Lizzy nodded. “I understand.”

Maya almost said, *No, you don’t.* But perhaps Lizzy did. Perhaps everyone understood betrayal from one side or another eventually.

The days after were strange. Without Carl’s campaign filling every hour, Maya discovered how tired she was. She slept through alarms. She stopped answering group messages. She ate toast for dinner and stared at her reflection in dark windows.

Martin appeared beside her after class a week later.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She tightened her scarf. “For being right?”

“For being smug about it before it happened.”

“You weren’t smug.”

“I was internally smug. It counts.”

She smiled faintly.

They walked across campus together, leaves dissolving underfoot.

“I should have listened,” Maya said.

Martin shook his head. “No. You should have been able to trust your friends.”

The kindness of that nearly undid her.

At the library, he gave her a folded piece of paper. “Don’t read it if it’s weird.”

“That’s an alarming introduction.”

“It’s a poem. So yes.”

She read it later in her room. It was about a girl made of windows who learned that transparency was not the same as emptiness. It was clumsy in places, beautiful in others, and devastatingly gentle. Maya pressed it between the pages of her notebook.

She began to write again—not essays, but private questions.

*What do I want when no one is watching?*  
*Who am I when I am not useful?*  
*Why does attention feel like proof?*  
*Can I forgive without returning?*

Johan became part of those weeks too, though quietly. They walked after his café shifts, hands tucked in pockets, breath clouding ahead of them. He told her about growing up above his mother’s bakery, about choosing music against his father’s wishes, about how lonely practice rooms could be. She told him things she had never told Carl: how her parents’ divorce had trained her to keep peace, how she feared being difficult, how admiration could feel like warmth even when it burned.

One night, as snow threatened but did not fall, Johan asked, “Do you miss him?”

Maya considered lying. “Yes. Not all of him. Mostly who I was hoping he’d be.”

Johan nodded. “That can be the hardest person to lose.”

She looked at him then, at his calm eyes and careful mouth, and felt a different kind of pull. Not lightning. Not applause. Something steadier, like a lamp left on.

“Johan,” she said, “I like you. And I’m afraid I’ll turn that into another way to disappear.”

He slipped his hands deeper into his coat pockets, as if resisting the urge to reach for her. “Then don’t disappear. Stay exactly where you are. I can meet you there.”

No one had ever offered her that before.

In December, Carl cornered her outside the seminar rooms.

He looked less golden than usual. Tired. Human. “Can we talk?”

Maya almost said no. But growth, she was learning, was not always escape. Sometimes it was standing still without surrendering.

They sat on a bench under the bare trees.

“I messed up,” Carl said.

“Yes.”

“I should have told you about Lizzy.”

“Yes.”

“And I shouldn’t have leaned on you so much during the campaign.”

She watched a crow hop along the path. “Why did you?”

He rubbed his face. “Because you were good at it. Because you made everything easier. Because I liked how you saw me.”

“I didn’t see you,” Maya said softly. “I edited you.”

Carl flinched.

“I made you kinder in my head. Braver. More honest. That part is mine. But you let me.”

“I’m sorry.”

She believed he was, in that moment. But apology was not a bridge by itself.

“I forgive you,” she said, surprising herself. “But I don’t want to be close to you right now.”

His jaw tightened. He nodded. “Okay.”

When he left, Maya felt grief, but also space.

The term ended beneath a pale winter sun. On the last evening before break, Lizzy hosted a small exhibition in the arts building. Maya almost didn’t go, but Martin insisted he needed “moral support against people who say things like ‘liminal’ near cheese cubes,” and Johan promised to meet them there.

The walls were lined with Lizzy’s photographs: blurred dancers, empty lecture halls, rain on bicycle seats, students laughing with mouths open and eyes closed. Near the back, Maya found a picture of herself taken weeks earlier in the library. She was looking out the window, unaware of the camera. Her expression was not sad exactly, but searching.

The title beneath it read: **Becoming Visible**.

Lizzy approached carefully. “I can take it down if you want.”

Maya studied the girl in the photo. She had spent so long wanting others to look at her in a way that would explain her to herself. But the photo did not explain. It witnessed.

“No,” Maya said. “Leave it.”

Lizzy’s shoulders eased. “Thank you.”

“I’m still angry.”

“I know.”

“But not only angry.”

Lizzy’s eyes shone. “That’s more than I deserve.”

“Probably.”

They both laughed, just a little.

Across the room, Carl was talking to a cluster of students, his hands moving dramatically. For once, Maya did not wonder whether he would look over. Martin stood near the cheese cubes, visibly suffering through someone’s interpretation of “urban melancholy.” Johan waited by the doorway, smiling when he saw her.

Maya walked to him slowly, not because she was uncertain, but because she was paying attention.

Outside, the first snow of the season began to fall over campus, softening the sharp roofs and stone paths. Glass Hall glowed in the distance, every window lit from within.

Johan held out his hand.

Maya looked at it, then at him. She thought of Carl’s brightness, Lizzy’s beauty, Martin’s fierce and awkward loyalty. She thought of every version of herself she had offered up to be liked, needed, chosen. She thought of the sentence in her notebook, the question that had started everything.

*Why do I keep becoming who people need?*

The answer was not simple. Maybe it would take years. But for the first time, she felt no hurry to solve herself for anyone else’s comfort.

She took Johan’s hand.

Not to be rescued. Not to be completed. Only to walk beside someone, into the snow, as herself.
