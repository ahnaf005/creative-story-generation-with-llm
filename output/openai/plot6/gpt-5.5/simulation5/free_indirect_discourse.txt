## The Shape of the Semester

By October, the campus had learned to turn itself theatrical. The plane trees along the main walk shed leaves in perfect rust-colored drifts, as if someone in Facilities had arranged them overnight for atmosphere. The old brick seminar building, with its green copper gutters and narrow windows, looked less like a place where students misquoted Foucault and more like a place where people ruined one another in letters.

Mara noticed these things because she was trying not to notice Carl.

He stood beneath the archway outside the student café, laughing with his head tipped back, one hand pressed to his chest in that self-mocking way of his. As if even his own delight embarrassed him. As if he were not, at all times, aware of the effect. A blue scarf hung loose around his neck, ridiculous for the mild weather, but of course on Carl it looked deliberate, continental, like he had just stepped out of a film in which no one ever checked their bank account.

Mara slowed. She had promised herself she would not slow.

Across from him, Lizzy was saying something animatedly, her curls trembling around her face. Lizzy’s hands moved when she spoke, slicing the air into bright certainties. That was one of the things Mara had loved first about her: the sense that Lizzy entered rooms already believing she deserved space there. Mara, who had spent her first year apologizing to chairs when she bumped into them, had admired it with an almost private reverence.

Carl saw Mara before Lizzy did. His face changed by a degree too small for anyone else to notice. But Mara noticed; noticing was the whole disease. His smile folded inward, became personal.

“Mara,” he called. “We were just discussing whether sincerity is still possible after nineteen.”

Lizzy turned then, grinning. “Carl thinks irony is a moral injury.”

“I think laziness is a moral injury,” Carl said. “Irony is just laziness wearing eyeliner.”

Mara should have laughed immediately. That was what clever people did: they received cleverness and sent it back polished. Instead she adjusted the strap of her bag and said, “Maybe sincerity after nineteen is just bad style.”

Carl’s eyes lit. “See? Mara understands.”

Lizzy looped her arm through Mara’s. “Mara understands because Mara has a soul, unlike the rest of us.”

A soul. How generous of Lizzy to give her one. Mara smiled, because she did love Lizzy—had loved her through late-night instant noodles, through mutual panic before exams, through the winter when Lizzy cried every Thursday over a boy from architecture who wore ugly rings and treated her like weather. But lately loving Lizzy had become more complicated, threaded with little burrs of resentment. Lizzy borrowed Mara’s books and returned them swollen with bathwater. Lizzy told stories that were not hers to tell, but told them beautifully, so that everyone forgave the theft. Lizzy sat too close to Carl.

They went inside. The café smelled of burnt espresso and wet wool. At the long table near the radiator, Martin was already reading, or pretending to read, a paperback held close to his face. His hair, pale and fine, fell across his forehead in a way that made him look perpetually interrupted. Mara liked Martin because he did not demand performance. Silence around him was not a failure.

He glanced up. “You survived Bergman.”

“Barely,” Mara said, dropping into the chair beside him. Their morning lecture had involved Professor Bergman drawing a triangle on the board and labeling its points Desire, Power, and Narrative, then becoming visibly irritated when no one achieved enlightenment.

Martin closed his book around his finger. “I think he’s been teaching the same triangle since 1987.”

“Maybe it’s a very deep triangle.”

“Maybe we’re shallow.”

Carl sat opposite Mara. Lizzy took the chair beside him, and Mara hated herself for noticing the small choreography of it, hated even more that Carl noticed her noticing. Johan arrived five minutes later, damp from the rain that had begun without anyone’s permission. He was broad-shouldered, soft-spoken, and always looked faintly sunburned, as if he had come from some healthier country where people built docks and said what they meant.

“Mara,” he said, smiling. Not to the table. To her.

She felt the warmth of it, inconvenient and undeserved. Johan had been in her statistics tutorial the previous year. He had once walked across campus in sleet to bring her notes when she had the flu, and he had not mentioned this act afterward, which made it impossible to dismiss as flirtation and equally impossible not to remember.

“Hi, Johan,” she said.

Carl leaned back. “We’re debating sincerity.”

Johan removed his wet coat. “Sounds exhausting.”

“Thank you,” Martin murmured.

“Johan is sincere,” Lizzy said, tapping ash from an unlit cigarette she had forgotten she wasn’t allowed to smoke indoors. “That’s why we keep him. He reminds us of prelapsarian humanity.”

Johan looked confused but kind. “I’m from Malmö.”

They laughed. Mara laughed too, and for a moment the afternoon arranged itself pleasantly around them: the radiator hissing, Martin’s dry comments, Lizzy shining, Carl watching, Johan steady as a post in a storm. She could have been happy. Wasn’t that the absurd part? The ingredients were all there, like in a recipe. Friendship. Desire. Intelligence. Youth. Add coffee. Stir until meaningful.

But the semester had its own plans.

It began, or seemed to begin afterward, with the department revue. Every November the literature students staged a chaotic evening of sketches and songs mocking professors, theory, politics, themselves. Lizzy was directing, naturally. Carl was writing half the material, or claiming to. Martin had been drafted to play Professor Bergman because he could make his voice go both nasal and tragic. Johan built sets, though no one had asked how he had acquired the ability. Mara joined the script committee because Lizzy said, “You have to, you’re the only one who can make Carl less insufferable,” and Carl said, “Impossible, but let her try.”

For three weeks, they lived in the black-box theater beneath the union building. There were pizza boxes stacked like architectural models, extension cords everywhere, actors lying dramatically across the floor. Mara wrote at a folding table with Carl, their laptops open, their shoulders almost touching. At first they bickered over jokes. Then they finished each other’s lines. Then, one rainy Wednesday after midnight, Carl read something she had written and fell silent.

“What?” she said, defensive already.

He shook his head. “Nothing. It’s good.”

“Don’t sound so surprised.”

“I’m not.” He looked at her then, really looked, without the usual flourish. “You hide how sharp you are.”

The words entered her foolishly, like light through a crack. She knew better. Compliments from Carl were not coins; they were hooks. Still, she carried that sentence home under her coat as if it might keep her warm.

Lizzy noticed. Of course Lizzy noticed.

“You and Carl seem close,” she said one evening, while they were in Mara’s room getting ready for a cast party. Lizzy sat cross-legged on the bed, applying eyeliner in a compact mirror. Mara stood at the wardrobe, pretending to choose between two identical black sweaters.

“We’re writing together.”

“Mmm.”

“What does ‘mmm’ mean?”

“It means mmm.” Lizzy snapped the compact shut. “Just be careful.”

Mara turned. “Why?”

Lizzy’s face took on the tender, irritating expression she used when preparing to be wise. “Carl likes attention. He likes being reflected in people. Especially clever girls who think they’re immune.”

Clever girls. Was Mara meant to be flattered or warned? And why did Lizzy sound as if she had already been there, as if Carl were a country she had visited and found disappointing?

“I don’t think I’m immune,” Mara said.

“Good,” Lizzy replied. “Because nobody is.”

At the party, Carl kissed Mara in the narrow hallway outside the kitchen, between a stack of empty beer crates and a poster advertising a Marxist reading group. It happened quickly, or perhaps slowly; memory later would refuse to decide. He had been telling her about a novel he wanted to write, something about a family trapped in a hotel during a storm. She had said, “That’s just The Shining with footnotes,” and he had laughed, and then there was the pause, the foolish cinematic pause. His hand brushed her sleeve. Her heart behaved like a ridiculous freshman.

Afterward he rested his forehead against hers. “I’ve wanted to do that for weeks.”

Weeks. Such a dangerous measurement. Long enough to mean something, short enough to deny.

They did not tell Lizzy. This was Mara’s first betrayal, though at the time she wrapped it in tissue paper and called it privacy. There was no need to make an announcement. People were allowed to have experiences not immediately fed into the group like scraps. Besides, what exactly was there to tell? A kiss. Another kiss two nights later in the theater wings. Carl’s hand on the small of her back. His late messages: Are you awake? I can’t stop thinking about the second act. About you, obviously, but also the second act.

She became a person who checked her phone beneath seminar tables. A person who hummed while brushing her teeth. A person who reread three-word messages until they became scripture. She watched herself becoming this person with scholarly horror. So this was desire: not grand, not liberating, but administrative. Waiting, interpreting, filing evidence.

Martin knew before she told him. They were sitting on the library steps, sharing a chocolate bar from the vending machine, when he said, “Carl?”

Mara almost choked. “What?”

“Oh, come on.”

“Am I that obvious?”

“You smile at your phone like it’s been elected pope.”

She buried her face in her hands. “Don’t.”

“I’m not judging.”

“You are a little.”

“Only because I care about narrative structure.” Martin broke off a square of chocolate. “Carl creates bad third acts.”

She should have listened. But advice from friends always arrived in the wrong tense, too early to believe or too late to use.

The revue opened to a packed house and more laughter than it deserved. Martin’s Bergman impression brought down the room. Lizzy took her curtain call flushed and triumphant, gripping Mara’s hand on one side and Carl’s on the other. For one suspended second, Mara felt part of something gleaming and intact. Then she saw Carl glance across Lizzy at her, private and amused, and the intact thing cracked.

After the show, everyone spilled into the student bar. Music thudded. Someone bought cheap sparkling wine. Johan found Mara near the coat rack and handed her a glass of water.

“You looked pale,” he said.

“I’m fine.”

“You always say that when you’re not.”

It annoyed her, this accuracy without permission. “Maybe I’m just tired.”

“Maybe.”

His gentleness made her feel cruel. She wanted to tell him that he was good, and that goodness was not the same as simplicity, and that she was sorry for wanting someone who turned every room into a mirror maze. Instead she drank the water.

Later, looking for Lizzy, Mara pushed open the door to the backstage corridor and heard voices.

Carl’s first. Low, familiar. “Don’t do this here.”

Then Lizzy’s, sharp with hurt. “Where would you prefer? Your room? Hers?”

Mara froze. The corridor smelled of dust and old paint. Through the half-open dressing room door, she could see their reflections in the spotted mirror: Lizzy standing rigid, Carl with a hand over his eyes.

“You said it was over,” Lizzy whispered.

“It was.”

“When? Yesterday? Before or after you kissed her?”

Mara’s body understood before her mind did. There had been a country, then. Lizzy had visited. No—Lizzy had lived there, and Mara had walked in smiling with her suitcase.

Carl said something she could not hear. Lizzy laughed once, a broken little sound. “You’re unbelievable. And she’s my friend.”

Mara stepped back, knocking into a prop chair. The sound cracked down the hallway.

Lizzy turned. Their eyes met in the mirror.

There were moments, Mara would think later, that did not pass but opened. You fell into them and kept falling. Lizzy’s face changed from anger to recognition to something worse: calculation. Not cruel calculation. Wounded calculation. How much had Mara heard? How much had she done? How much could be forgiven?

Carl came to the doorway. “Mara—”

She left before he could finish. Very dramatic, some remote part of her noted. Excellent blocking. She pushed through the bar, past Martin calling her name, past Johan rising from a table, out into the cold. Rain struck her face with bureaucratic persistence.

By morning, the story had moved faster than truth. Not publicly, not loudly—worse, intimately. A few people knew; therefore everyone knew in the dense, silent way student communities know. Lizzy did not answer Mara’s messages. Carl sent seven, then stopped. Martin arrived at her room with coffee and sat on the floor without speaking.

“I didn’t know,” Mara said finally.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Martin looked at her carefully. “I know you didn’t know enough.”

It was the kindest possible condemnation.

For days Mara moved through campus like a ghost haunting her own mistakes. She replayed every conversation with Lizzy. Be careful. Nobody is. Had Lizzy tried to warn her? Or had she been protecting territory? And why did Mara want that distinction so badly? If she could prove Lizzy had been obscure, possessive, unfair, then perhaps her own betrayal would shrink to a misunderstanding. But at night, the truth lay beside her, breathing steadily: she had hidden things because hiding had been convenient. She had enjoyed being chosen in secret. She had allowed not knowing to become innocence.

Carl found her after a lecture the following week. He looked tired, which gave her no satisfaction. Or gave her some, which she disliked.

“Can we talk?”

They walked to the river because neutral ground was necessary, apparently, for people who had made a mess of bedrooms and theaters. The water was high from rain, brown and muscular beneath the bridge.

“I handled it badly,” Carl said.

Mara almost laughed. “That’s one phrase for it.”

“I didn’t plan for this to happen.”

How comforting, to be unplanned. “With me or with Lizzy?”

He flinched. Good. No, not good. There was no good here.

“With either. With both.”

“Were you with her when you kissed me?”

“No. Not exactly.”

There it was: the homeland of cowards, Not Exactly. Mara watched a leaf spin in an eddy below. She had thought confrontation would make her powerful, but instead she felt tired, older by a semester.

“You should leave me alone,” she said.

“Mara—”

“No. You don’t get to make your confusion the weather everyone else has to live under.”

The sentence surprised her. It sounded like something Lizzy might say, bright and clean as a blade. Carl stared at her, and for once had no clever answer.

Repair with Lizzy took longer, and was less elegant. There was no single tearful reunion, no swelling music. At first there was only the awkward diplomacy of shared spaces: a nod in seminar, a seat left empty, a mutual refusal to make friends choose tables. Then, in December, Lizzy knocked on Mara’s door carrying a paper bag.

“I brought those almond things you like,” she said. “From the bakery.”

Mara stood aside.

They sat on the rug because the desk chair was piled with laundry. For a while they ate pastries that flaked sugar onto their sleeves.

“I should have told you about Carl,” Lizzy said.

Mara looked up.

“I wanted to seem above it,” Lizzy continued. “Like I didn’t care. Like warning you would make me pathetic.”

“I should have told you when he kissed me.”

“Yes,” Lizzy said. Not cruelly. Just yes.

Mara nodded. “I liked being chosen.”

Lizzy’s mouth twisted. “He’s good at making chosen feel like loved.”

There it was, the small room inside the larger room, where they could both sit without knives. Mara felt tears rising and hated their timing. Tears always tried to purchase forgiveness at a discount.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

Lizzy looked at her for a long moment. “Me too.”

They did not hug. Not then. Trust, Mara was learning, did not return because summoned. It had to grow back like skin, sensitive and imperfect.

The semester ended under snow. Campus emptied in stages: suitcases over cobblestones, bicycles abandoned to frost, windows going dark one by one. Mara stayed an extra week to finish an essay she had avoided with the dedication of a monk avoiding sin. Martin kept her company in the library. Lizzy sent occasional messages, cautious but real. Carl became a figure glimpsed at a distance, blue scarf replaced by a gray one, already acquiring the unreality of someone else’s bad decision.

On the last Thursday, Johan asked if she wanted to walk before he took the train home. She almost said no out of habit, the habit of refusing uncomplicated kindness because it made no demands and therefore seemed suspect. But the afternoon was clear, and the snow along the river had hardened into bright crusts, and she was tired of arranging her life around avoidance.

They walked past the boathouse, their breath visible. Johan told her about his younger sister, who had dyed the family dog pink by accident. Mara laughed, really laughed, and the sound startled her. It had been waiting somewhere, apparently.

After a while Johan said, “I liked you last year.”

Mara’s heart gave a small, guilty movement. “Johan—”

“I know,” he said quickly. “I’m not saying it to ask for anything. I just thought you should know. I think we all spend too much time pretending not to want what we want.”

She looked at him. Snowlight softened his face. He was not simple. No one was simple. She had made him simple because simplicity was easier to set aside.

“I don’t know what I want,” she said.

“That’s something.”

“Yes.” She smiled faintly. “A very annoying something.”

They stopped on the bridge. Below, the river moved black between shelves of ice. Mara thought of triangles on chalkboards, of desire and power and narrative. Professor Bergman had not been entirely wrong, which was irritating. For months she had believed the story was happening to her: Carl’s attention, Lizzy’s anger, Martin’s warnings, Johan’s patience. But perhaps that was another vanity, the softest one. She had been choosing all along—choosing silence, choosing drama, choosing the version of herself reflected in other people’s faces.

And now? Now there was no grand revelation. She was still vain, still afraid of being ordinary, still capable of hurting people she loved. But she could see these things without turning away. That had to count for something. Not redemption. Not yet. But a beginning shaped like honesty.

Johan leaned on the railing beside her. “You’re thinking very hard.”

“I’m trying not to.”

“Any luck?”

“None.”

He laughed, and she did too.

When they walked back, he did not take her hand. She was grateful. She was also, unexpectedly, disappointed. Both feelings could exist; apparently the heart was not a courtroom where only one truth was allowed to testify.

At the station, Johan hugged her goodbye. A careful hug, warm through layers of wool. “See you next term, Mara.”

“See you.”

She watched his train pull away, red lights shrinking into the early dark. Then she turned toward campus. Snow began to fall again, lightly now, not enough to hide anything. The path ahead was marked by footprints going in several directions, crossing and recrossing, some already filling in.

Mara put her hands in her pockets and walked slowly, not because she was waiting for anyone, not because she wanted to be seen, but because for once there was no need to hurry past herself.
