Mara learned, during her second week at Northbridge University, that every cafeteria had a weather system.

The one in Calder Hall was stormy: crowded tables, sudden laughter, rumors moving like gusts, the clatter of trays like rain on glass. The café beneath the library was foggy and subdued, full of people whispering over annotated books, eyes red from late-night reading. But the student union café—where Mara spent most afternoons because the coffee was cheap and the chairs were soft—had the unpredictable brightness of spring. Friendships bloomed there in minutes and withered just as quickly. People appeared at your table and became important before you understood why.

That was where she first met Carl.

He sat down across from her without asking, carrying two paper cups and wearing a scarf too elegant for the rainy campus outside. “You’re in Professor Lind’s modern drama seminar,” he said.

Mara looked up from her notebook. “I am?”

He grinned. “You are. You sit by the window and underline everything twice.”

She closed the notebook halfway, suddenly self-conscious. “And you’re the one who argues with Professor Lind.”

“Only when he’s wrong.”

“Which is always?”

“Which is educational.”

He pushed one of the cups toward her. “Black coffee. You look like someone who drinks black coffee.”

“I look tired,” Mara said, but took it.

Carl had a way of speaking as though the world were a stage built for him to cross. He was handsome in an effortless, careless way, with dark curls and sharp eyes that seemed always amused. Within days, he had folded Mara into his circle—students from literature, politics, theater, and philosophy who gathered in corners and debated as if grades, careers, and history depended on whether a poem was sincere.

Mara liked them immediately and feared them almost as quickly.

Before Northbridge, she had lived in a small coastal town where people knew what her mother planted in the garden and what her father ordered at the bakery. She had been clever there, quiet there, dependable there. At university, cleverness was not rare. Quietness was mistaken for depth by some and dullness by others. Dependability seemed almost embarrassing.

Carl’s group shimmered with intensity. They used words like alienation and praxis and bourgeois without irony. They talked about love as if it were a political position. Mara listened, laughed when she understood the joke, and went back to her dorm each night feeling both larger and less visible.

The first person who made her feel seen was Lizzy.

Lizzy lived two doors down in the residence hall and owned three pairs of boots, all red. She was studying biology but knew everyone in the theater department, most of the music students, and at least two people who claimed to be starting an underground newspaper.

“You’re Mara,” Lizzy said one evening, leaning against Mara’s doorframe with a bowl of instant noodles in one hand. “You went out with Carl and his disciples.”

“They’re not disciples.”

“They are absolutely disciples. Did they make you discuss death?”

“Only briefly.”

Lizzy rolled her eyes and stepped inside without waiting for an invitation. “Then you got off easy.”

Lizzy was warm, funny, and disarmingly direct. She could walk into a room and find its center without trying. Unlike Carl, who made Mara feel chosen, Lizzy made her feel included. There was a difference, and Mara began to understand it.

Soon Lizzy was borrowing Mara’s sweaters, reading over her essays, appearing at breakfast with gossip and toast. Mara told her things she had not planned to tell anyone: that her parents were separating but hadn’t said the word divorce; that she had chosen literature because stories made more sense than people; that sometimes she felt like she was performing the role of herself without having read the script.

Lizzy listened carefully, her bright face softening. “Everyone’s pretending,” she said. “Some people just have better costumes.”

Then there was Martin.

Mara met him in the library basement, where the air smelled of dust and old paper. He was sitting on the floor between shelves, surrounded by books on medieval history, scribbling notes in a black-bound journal.

“Sorry,” she said, stepping over a pile of photocopies. “I’m trying to find Scandinavian Drama.”

“You’re one aisle over,” he said without looking up. “But if you want tragedy, stay here. I’ve been translating tax records for two hours.”

She laughed. He looked up then, and his expression changed—slowly, as if surprise took time to reach him.

Martin was not dazzling like Carl or radiant like Lizzy. He was steady. Tall, slightly stooped, with pale hair that fell into his eyes, he spoke carefully and only when he had something to say. In seminars, he listened before responding. At parties, he stood near windows. When he smiled, it felt like a secret given freely.

They began studying together on Tuesdays. At first it was accidental: same table, same time, a shared complaint about the library heating. Then it became habit. Martin brought apples and always offered one. Mara brought chocolate and pretended it was for herself.

With Martin, silence did not demand filling. This relieved her so deeply that she mistook it for something ordinary.

“You’re calmer with him,” Lizzy observed one night as Mara brushed her teeth.

“With who?” Mara said through foam.

“Martin.”

Mara rinsed. “I’m calm generally.”

Lizzy laughed so hard she almost dropped her toothbrush. “You? You fold your socks when anxious.”

“That’s organization.”

“That’s a cry for help.”

Mara threw a towel at her, but afterward, alone in bed, she thought of Martin’s hands turning pages, his quiet “Take your time” whenever she struggled to explain an idea, the way he never watched her like she was a puzzle to solve.

By late October, the campus had turned gold and wet. Leaves stuck to the sidewalks. Rain streaked the windows of lecture halls. Mara’s life divided itself among Carl’s electric gatherings, Lizzy’s crowded dorm-room confidences, and Martin’s library quiet. She moved between them like someone crossing borders, changing language without noticing.

Johan entered when everything was already complicated.

He was Lizzy’s friend from secondary school, studying architecture at the technical college across town. He arrived one Friday evening with a roll of drawings under his arm and the weary charm of someone who had slept badly for weeks.

“This is Johan,” Lizzy announced. “He designs buildings that look like moral judgments.”

“They’re efficient,” Johan said.

“They’re grim.”

“They have integrity.”

Mara liked him instantly. Johan had a dry humor and a watchful kindness. He did not compete for attention but earned it by noticing what others missed: an empty glass, a shiver, a silence that had lasted too long. At a party later that night, when Carl launched into a theatrical critique of student activism, Johan leaned toward Mara and murmured, “Does he always speak in chapters?”

Mara nearly choked on her drink.

Carl noticed.

After that, something shifted. Carl began seeking Mara more insistently. He saved seats for her, asked her opinions in groups, touched her elbow lightly when making a point. It should have flattered her—and it did—but beneath the pleasure was unease.

One night after a poetry reading, he walked her back through the rain. The campus lamps blurred in the mist. His shoulder brushed hers under the narrow shelter of his umbrella.

“You’ve become mysterious,” Carl said.

“I didn’t know I was expected to remain transparent.”

He smiled. “You know what I mean. You drift away.”

“I have classes. Friends.”

“Martin?” he asked, lightly.

The name felt suddenly exposed. “Martin is a friend.”

“And Johan?”

“Also a friend.”

Carl stopped walking. “And me?”

Mara looked at him. Rain tapped on the umbrella above them. He seemed less amused than usual, almost uncertain, and that uncertainty moved her.

“You’re Carl,” she said.

His face softened. “That sounds like either a compliment or a diagnosis.”

“Maybe both.”

He kissed her then, quickly at first, as if testing whether the moment would hold, then more urgently. Mara kissed him back because she had imagined it, because he was beautiful, because being wanted by him felt like stepping into a spotlight. But when she returned to her room, Lizzy asleep across the hall and rain whispering against the glass, Mara sat on her bed and felt not joy but confusion.

The next week, Carl behaved as though an agreement had been signed. He waited after lectures, draped his scarf around her shoulders, referred to “us” with alarming ease. People noticed. Lizzy raised her eyebrows but said nothing until Mara finally collapsed onto her bed and groaned.

“Do you like him?” Lizzy asked.

“Yes. I think so. I don’t know.”

“That is a deeply unhelpful answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Lizzy sat beside her. “Do you feel like yourself with him?”

Mara stared at the ceiling. “I feel like someone he finds interesting.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

Mara knew. She hated that Lizzy knew too.

Martin said nothing about Carl, though Mara suspected he had heard. Their Tuesday study sessions became slightly more formal, their silences less easy. Once, reaching for the same book, their fingers touched; Martin withdrew his hand so quickly that Mara felt the absence like a door closing.

“Martin,” she said.

He looked at her.

But she did not know what to ask. Do you care? Do I? Would it matter? Instead she said, “Did you finish the reading?”

“Yes,” he said, and opened his notebook.

The betrayal came in November, though afterward Mara understood that betrayal rarely arrives suddenly. It gathers itself from small cowardices.

Carl had persuaded her to show him an essay she was writing on Henrik Ibsen and self-deception. “I won’t steal your brilliance,” he promised. “I’ll only admire it.”

She sent it to him late one night, embarrassed by how much she wanted his praise.

A week later, in seminar, Professor Lind asked Carl to summarize his presentation topic. Carl leaned back in his chair and began speaking about Ibsen, performance, and the lies people mistake for duty. At first Mara listened with interest. Then her stomach tightened.

The phrasing was hers.

Not all of it. Carl was too clever for that. He had rearranged sentences, sharpened arguments, replaced some examples. But the central idea—the one she had stayed up three nights shaping, the one she had written in the margin of her notebook with a small exclamation mark—came from her essay.

She looked at him across the room. He did not look back.

After class, she followed him into the corridor, heart pounding.

“That was my argument,” she said.

Carl adjusted the strap of his bag. “What?”

“Your presentation. You used my essay.”

His expression shifted from surprise to annoyance so quickly she almost missed the guilt between them. “Mara, we discussed ideas. That’s what people do.”

“I sent you a draft.”

“And I responded to it. You don’t own a theme.”

“It wasn’t a theme. It was my work.”

Students passed around them, pretending not to listen. Carl lowered his voice. “Don’t be dramatic.”

The words struck harder than the theft.

Mara stepped back. “I trusted you.”

Something like regret moved in his face, but pride smothered it. “If your idea can’t survive being shared, maybe it wasn’t strong enough.”

She walked away before she could cry.

By evening, everyone seemed to know. Northbridge’s social weather turned vicious. Some people said Carl had always borrowed too freely. Others suggested Mara was overreacting. A few asked her for details with eyes bright from the pleasure of scandal. Mara stopped going to the union café. She avoided seminars until Professor Lind emailed, asking if she was unwell.

Lizzy was furious on her behalf. “I’ll kill him,” she said, pacing Mara’s room in red boots.

“Please don’t. Prison would disrupt your exams.”

“I’ll ruin him academically but in a way that leaves no evidence.”

Despite herself, Mara laughed.

But even Lizzy became part of the hurt. Two nights later, Mara overheard voices in the hall—Lizzy and Johan, speaking softly.

“She’s taking it badly,” Lizzy said.

“Of course she is,” Johan replied.

“I warned her about him.”

Mara froze behind her door.

“You did?” Johan asked.

“Well, not directly. But everyone knows what Carl is like. She wanted to be special to him.”

There was no malice in Lizzy’s voice, only frustration. That made it worse.

Mara opened the door.

Lizzy’s face changed. “Mara—”

“You think I wanted this?”

“No. I didn’t mean—”

“You think I’m stupid.”

“I think you’re hurt.”

“You think I should have known better.”

Lizzy was silent.

Mara closed the door gently because slamming it would have been too easy.

For two days, she spoke to no one. She went to classes, ate mechanically, wrote nothing. Her mother called, and Mara let it ring. Her father sent a photo of the sea, gray and restless, with the message: Thought you’d miss this. She did, suddenly and painfully.

On the third day, Martin found her in the library basement.

He sat on the floor beside her without asking. For a while they listened to the hum of the lights.

“I read your essay,” he said at last.

She looked at him sharply. “What?”

“Professor Lind asked if I had seen you. He mentioned the situation. I told him I hadn’t. Then he said your draft was excellent.” Martin hesitated. “He wanted you to know he recognized the difference between your work and Carl’s performance.”

Mara blinked hard.

“I’m sorry,” Martin said.

The sympathy undid her. “I feel ridiculous.”

“You’re not.”

“I liked being admired by him. I liked that he noticed me. Maybe Lizzy’s right.”

“What did Lizzy say?”

“That I wanted to be special.”

Martin considered this. “Most people do.”

Mara wiped her eyes angrily. “That doesn’t make it noble.”

“No. But it makes it human.”

They sat quietly. Somewhere above them, footsteps crossed the library floor.

“I was jealous,” Martin said.

Mara turned to him.

He looked straight ahead, ears slightly red. “Of Carl. Of how easily he took up space in your life. I didn’t say anything because it seemed unfair. You were happy, or I thought you were.”

“I wasn’t,” Mara said. Then, more honestly, “Not exactly.”

Martin nodded. “You don’t have to explain.”

But Mara wanted to. She wanted, suddenly, to stop being a series of reactions to other people’s expectations.

“With Carl, I felt brighter,” she said. “But also smaller. Like I had to keep earning the attention. With Lizzy, I felt safe until I heard what she really thought. With Johan…” She smiled faintly. “With Johan I feel like he sees everything and judges nothing.”

“And with me?” Martin asked softly.

Mara’s heart beat once, hard.

“With you,” she said, “I forget to perform.”

He looked at her then, and the sadness in his face was tender, not demanding.

“That sounds peaceful,” he said.

“It scares me.”

“Peace often does, if you’re used to weather.”

Mara laughed through the last of her tears.

Repair took time.

She spoke to Lizzy first. They sat on the laundry room floor while dryers thundered around them. Lizzy cried before Mara did.

“I was careless,” Lizzy said. “I made your pain into proof that I was right. I hate when people do that to me, and then I did it to you.”

“You did warn me,” Mara admitted.

“No. I hinted. That’s different. Hinting lets you feel wise without being brave.”

Mara looked at her, surprised by the precision of it.

Lizzy sniffed. “I’ve been thinking.”

“Dangerous.”

“Shut up.”

They laughed, and something loosened.

Johan apologized too, though Mara told him he had done nothing wrong. “Listening can be a kind of participation,” he said.

“You architecture students make everything structural.”

“Everything is structural.”

He invited her to see an exhibition of student models across town. Mara went, and they spent an afternoon examining tiny buildings made of cardboard and light. Johan spoke about foundations, load-bearing walls, the honesty of materials. Mara found herself thinking about people: what they displayed, what held them up, what cracks they plastered over.

“You’re different,” Johan said as they walked back toward the tram.

“Because I cried in public and survived?”

“Partly.” He smiled. “You seem less interested in being interpreted.”

Mara carried that sentence with her for days.

Carl’s apology arrived late and poorly.

He found her after seminar in early December. Snow had begun falling, softening the edges of the campus. He looked tired. For once, his scarf was crooked.

“I handled it badly,” he said.

Mara waited.

“I shouldn’t have used your ideas without credit. I told Lind. He’s letting me redo the presentation on a different topic.”

“Good.”

Carl winced. “I’m sorry, Mara.”

She studied him. She had imagined this moment many times, imagined anger blazing through her, imagined delivering some perfect sentence that would make him understand the exact shape of what he had broken. But standing there, she felt only a quiet distance.

“You didn’t just use my work,” she said. “You made me doubt my right to be angry.”

His face tightened. “I know.”

“I don’t think you do. But maybe you will.”

He nodded, looking younger than she had ever seen him.

As he walked away, Mara realized she no longer needed him to be villainous in order for her hurt to be real. That felt like freedom.

At the end of term, Professor Lind returned their final essays. Mara’s was marked with a high distinction and a note: Your argument has become sharper because you trust your own perception. Continue.

She read it three times.

That evening, Lizzy organized a small celebration in her room. Johan brought pastries from a bakery near the tram stop. Martin brought cider. Lizzy wore silver earrings shaped like stars and insisted they toast “academic survival and emotional literacy.”

“To not dating men with scarves,” Johan added.

Martin glanced down at his own wool scarf in alarm.

“Present company excluded,” Lizzy said.

They laughed, and Mara laughed too, fully this time.

Later, she and Martin walked across campus. Snow covered the lawns, and their footsteps made soft marks side by side. The buildings glowed with exam-week exhaustion: lit windows, shadowed figures, the blue flicker of computer screens.

“I’m going home tomorrow,” Martin said.

“For the holiday?”

He nodded. “You?”

“The day after. My parents want me for Christmas. Separately, somehow.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“It will be.” She breathed in the cold air. “But I think I can be difficult too, if necessary.”

Martin smiled. “Good.”

They stopped near the library steps. For a moment, neither spoke.

“I don’t want to rush toward something just because it’s gentle,” Mara said.

Martin’s eyes met hers. “All right.”

“But I don’t want to pretend I feel nothing, either.”

His smile changed—small, luminous. “All right.”

She took his hand. It was warm despite the cold.

They stood like that beneath the falling snow, not kissing, not declaring, not turning the moment into a promise too heavy to carry. It was enough to feel the truth of her own hand choosing his.

Mara thought of the cafeteria weather, of storms and fog and spring brightness. She thought of how desperately she had wanted to be understood, and how often she had confused being watched with being known. Carl had taught her about hunger and pride. Lizzy, about friendship’s clumsy repairs. Johan, about structures and quiet judgment withheld. Martin, about stillness.

But the discovery that mattered most had not come from any of them.

It came from the long nights after betrayal, when she sat alone with her anger and did not explain it away. It came from writing again, slowly at first, then fiercely. It came from hearing her own thoughts and recognizing them as worthy before anyone else admired them.

At Northbridge, Mara was no longer the girl from a coastal town who folded socks when anxious and waited to be chosen. Or perhaps she was still that girl, but not only her.

She was becoming someone who could enter a room without needing to become its weather.

And as snow gathered silently on the library steps, Mara held Martin’s hand and felt, for the first time in months, not certain exactly—but real.
