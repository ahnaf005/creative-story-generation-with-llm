The road had vanished beneath a skin of gravel. Elias walked on, leaning into the wind, with the mountains closing around him like a thought he couldn’t finish.

Then came the car, nose-down in a stand of pines, one wheel still turning slowly. He hurried to it. The woman inside was motionless. He touched her wrist, though he already knew. No pulse. The mountain air smelled of crushed needles and gasoline.

He called emergency services. The signal flickered, then held. They were coming, they said. How long? They couldn’t say.

So he sat beside the car and waited.

“You wouldn’t believe the trouble I got into in Marseille,” he told the woman. His voice sounded small in the open air. “I was twenty. Thought I could live on coffee and charm.”

He told her about the room above the bakery, the landlady who sang opera while sweeping, the girl whose name he had forgotten but whose laugh he could still hear. Funny, the things that stayed. A smell of bread, a laugh in a stairwell. Not the important things. Never the important things.

The woman’s coat was red. He wondered if she’d chosen it that morning, or grabbed the first thing by the door. There was no way to know. A life could be reduced, in a moment, to a coat on a roadside. It seemed unfair. Then again, fairness was a human word, and the mountains had no use for it.

He told her about his brother, who had drowned in a lake so shallow Elias had once waded across it. Their mother had kept the boy’s shoes by the door for years. As if he might come back for them. As if a thing could be kept in place by being remembered.

The wind moved through the pines. Far above, a hawk circled without effort. Elias felt the wilderness watching—or perhaps it was only that nothing out here looked away.

He pulled his coat from his shoulders and laid it over the woman. There. It was something to do, and something was better than nothing.

When the distant sirens finally rose from the valley, he kept talking. He told her about the first time he saw snow, and how he’d tried to catch it on his tongue. It vanished before he could taste it.

Of course it did. Everything did. That was the trick of it. Not that things ended, but that they had been there at all.
