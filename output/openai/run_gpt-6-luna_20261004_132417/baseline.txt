The road had narrowed to a ribbon of gravel between the mountain and the drop.

Elias heard the crash before he saw it: a metallic shout, then a long silence. He hurried around the bend and found a blue car crumpled against a stand of pines. One wheel turned slowly in the air.

A woman lay beside the open door. He knelt, touched her wrist, and felt nothing. For a moment he listened anyway, as if the mountain might answer for her.

His phone showed one bar. He called emergency services, gave them the road marker and his best guess at their distance, then stayed on the line until the signal failed. They were coming, the dispatcher had said.

Elias sat on a flat stone nearby. He had been walking alone for three days, carrying a pack and the habit of speaking only when necessary. Now he spoke to the woman.

“I once missed a train in Lyon,” he told her. “Spent the night in the station. I thought it was the worst thing that could happen. By morning, I’d met a baker who gave me coffee and told me his whole life.”

He told her about his brother, with whom he’d argued over something neither of them could now remember. About the house he had sold. About the river near his childhood home, which had changed its course and left the old footbridge stranded in a field.

The stories came slowly, then without effort. He did not know whether he was speaking for her or for himself.

Above them, clouds moved across the peaks. Wind combed the pines, and a few yellow leaves spiraled down onto the road. The world went on without haste, taking and rearranging everything: water, stone, names.

When sirens finally climbed the pass, Elias stood. He watched the lights flicker between the trees, then turned his face toward the sound.

The mountain kept its silence. The leaves kept falling. Somewhere below, the river went on changing course.
