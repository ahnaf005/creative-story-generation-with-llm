### The Last Tide

On the last morning of the residency, the sea was the color of a spoon.

Mara stood at the kitchen window of the old schoolhouse, watching rain gather on the glass, while Theo searched the refrigerator for the sandwiches he’d promised to make. The other residents had already begun packing. A cello case leaned beside the front door. Someone was taping a cardboard box labeled FRAGILE—GLASS to itself.

“You said the beach,” Mara said.

“I said we could still go to the beach.”

“You said it would be sunny.”

“I said I hoped it would be sunny.”

“You said you’d checked.”

Theo looked up from the refrigerator. “I checked yesterday.”

“That’s not checking.”

He held up a limp bunch of parsley as if it proved something. “I’m doing my best under difficult conditions.”

They had been at the residency for six weeks, long enough to learn the schoolhouse’s habits: the upstairs radiator clanged at midnight; the bathroom window stuck unless lifted at the left corner; the coffee machine worked only if someone stood beside it and threatened to replace it. They had also learned each other’s habits, though neither of them had admitted to studying them.

Theo drummed his fingers against his mug when he was waiting for an answer. Mara turned every question into a smaller one. They had slept together twice, or three times, depending on what counted. They had never discussed it in daylight.

Now their suitcases stood open in their rooms. Tomorrow Theo was taking the early train north. Mara was flying west at noon. In the afternoon, the schoolhouse would belong to the next group of artists, who would discover the loose stair and the unreliable coffee machine and make their own temporary arrangements.

Theo shut the refrigerator. “The bus still goes.”

“In this weather?”

“Buses are famously indifferent to weather.”

“And the beach?”

“Also indifferent.”

Mara smiled despite herself. “Fine. But I’m not carrying anything.”

They carried everything.

The bus dropped them at the wrong end of town, a mile from the harbor, because the driver said the road to the beach was flooded. They walked past shuttered shops and a bakery where a woman was stacking chairs on tables. Theo carried the food in a canvas tote. Mara carried a striped umbrella that turned inside out three times before she gave up and tucked it beneath her arm.

At the harbor, the rain stopped. Beyond the breakwater, the clouds were lifting in long gray strips. They bought tickets from a booth with a cracked blue window. The ferry was leaving in twelve minutes.

“We made it,” Theo said.

“We got wet and lost.”

“Those aren’t opposites.”

The ferry carried them to the far side of the bay. It was nearly empty, except for an old man with a terrier and a family of four who seemed to be having a quiet argument about a missing shoe. Mara sat outside. The wind dried the rain on her cheeks. Theo came to stand beside her, holding two paper cups of coffee.

“Peace offering,” he said.

“I didn’t know there was a conflict.”

“There’s always a conflict.”

“Not always.”

He leaned against the rail. “That’s what the conflict wants you to think.”

She took the coffee. It was watery and too hot. Across the water, the beach appeared in sections between the clouds: pale sand, dark rocks, a line of bent grass.

They had gone there once before, three weeks earlier, on a day so bright they’d both squinted through lunch. Theo had drawn the dunes in the margins of his notebook. Mara had fallen asleep with her head on his shoulder. When she woke, he was watching the water instead of her, and for the rest of the walk back they’d talked about work.

Today, when they reached the beach, the sand was empty except for a small red bucket stranded near the tide line. Theo set down the tote. Mara spread a blanket, weighting the corners with shoes.

“This is bleak,” she said.

“It’s beautiful.”

“You’re not looking at the sky.”

“I’m trying not to.”

The sea was rough, its surface broken into pewter folds. Wind swept low over the water and carried the smell of salt and wet stone. Mara took off her shoes and socks. The sand was cold enough to make her toes curl.

Theo unpacked the lunch. The sandwiches had been pressed flat by the coffee thermos. One had opened. A slice of tomato lay loose in the bottom of the bag.

“Lunch,” he announced.

“Is that the parsley?”

“It was meant to be.”

They sat on the blanket, knees almost touching. The first sandwich was mostly bread. The second held an impressive quantity of mustard. A gull watched them from a few feet away with the attention of a creditor.

“Do you think the new residents will find the missing kettle?” Mara asked.

“They’ll find the kettle.”

“I mean the one in the basement.”

“I didn’t know there was a kettle in the basement.”

“Exactly.”

Theo threw a crust toward the gull. It snatched the piece and moved closer.

“Don’t encourage it,” Mara said.

“I’m trying to make a friend.”

“You can’t just feed something and call it friendship.”

“Why not?”

“Because then it only comes back when it’s hungry.”

He glanced at her. She was looking out at the water.

A gust lifted the blanket. Theo put a hand on one corner and Mara on the other. The middle rose like a sail. For a few seconds they crouched, gripping the fabric, laughing as it tried to carry their lunch away. Then the wind dropped, leaving them with a blanket full of sand.

“Good,” Mara said. “Now it’s seasoned.”

They ate the rest of the sandwiches on a rock. The gull stayed nearby, patient as a landlord.

After lunch, Theo found the red bucket. He picked it up and examined the faded cartoon fish on its side.

“Treasure,” he said.

“Someone’s toddler is going to be devastated.”

“There are no toddlers.”

“There are definitely toddlers somewhere.”

He turned the bucket upside down. A single shell fell out. Mara put it in her coat pocket.

They walked east along the water. The tide was going out, uncovering a ledge of slick black rock. Mara stepped onto it and nearly slid. Theo caught her elbow.

“Careful.”

“I am.”

“You’re walking on algae.”

“I know what I’m doing.”

He didn’t let go immediately. His hand remained around her sleeve, the warmth of his fingers traveling through the damp fabric. She could have turned toward him. The space between them was a small, easy distance.

Instead she said, “We should get a picture.”

He let go. “Of what?”

“The beach.”

He took her phone. She stood with the water behind her, looking more serious than she felt. He lowered the phone.

“You have to press the button,” she said.

“I was waiting for the wind to stop.”

“It won’t.”

He took the picture. When she looked at it, her hair covered half her face.

“Excellent,” she said.

“I can take another.”

“No.”

She put the phone away. The wind combed the water into narrow, bright lines.

They kept walking until the land curved and the beach narrowed against a wall of rock. There was a shallow cave there, no more than a dark notch, where the previous week Theo had claimed he’d heard a seal.

“You said it was a cave,” Mara reminded him.

“It is.”

“You said there was a seal.”

“I said I thought there was a seal.”

“You said, ‘I heard a seal.’”

“I may have been speaking poetically.”

“Did you hear one?”

“No.”

“I knew it.”

He stepped into the cave and turned around. “You can hear things without hearing them.”

“That’s poetry, not evidence.”

“Maybe they’re the same.”

She watched him standing there, framed by the stone. His hair was blowing across his forehead. In another place, another life, she might have asked him to come closer. Or she might have let him. The trouble was not that she didn’t know what she wanted. It was that she knew wanting could be a way of making promises she had no intention of keeping.

“Do you ever wish,” she began, then stopped.

Theo waited.

“Do you ever wish we’d had more time to work?”

He smiled slightly. “That’s not what you were going to ask.”

“No?”

“No.”

She looked down at the shell in her palm. It was white and chipped along one edge.

He didn’t ask what she had meant. She was grateful to him for that. She was also, briefly, disappointed.

The return ferry was due at four. They had plenty of time, Theo said, so they sat in the lee of the rock and watched the clouds break apart. A strip of blue opened over the horizon. The sun came through, turning the water briefly silver.

Mara rested her head against the stone. Theo sat beside her, close enough that their coats touched.

“Are you glad you came?” he asked.

“To the residency?”

“To the beach.”

“Yes.”

He picked at a loose thread on his cuff. “Me too.”

She wanted to say that she would miss him. But the words seemed too large for the afternoon, too much like a request. She wanted to ask whether he ever thought of staying somewhere after the work was done, but she already knew what his answer would be. His life was packed into a suitcase; hers had been packed before she arrived.

A bell sounded from the harbor. Theo looked at his watch.

“We should go.”

They started back across the sand. Near the dunes, Mara discovered she had lost one glove. They retraced their steps, found it tangled in the blanket, and then realized they’d left the tote behind on the rock.

By the time they retrieved it, they were running.

The ferry was pulling away.

Mara stood at the end of the pier, one hand raised, as if the gesture might bring it back. Theo leaned over the railings, breathing hard.

“Next one?” she said.

“An hour.”

“You said we had plenty of time.”

“I was wrong.”

The gull from the beach stood on a post beside them, watching.

Mara laughed. She couldn’t help it. Theo laughed too, and for a moment the whole day seemed to loosen: the missed bus, the wet sandwiches, the lost glove, the ferry carrying them nowhere. The old man from the morning had returned on the departing boat, his terrier tucked under one arm.

They sat in the shelter and shared the last of the coffee. It had gone cold. The sky cleared in strips, and the late light settled over the harbor roofs.

“Tomorrow,” Theo said, “I’m leaving at six.”

“I know.”

“You have a car coming?”

“Seven-thirty.”

He nodded. “I’ll be gone before you wake up.”

“Probably.”

The shelter smelled of wet wool and old tobacco. Outside, the next ferry moved across the bay, small against the open water.

“You could leave a note,” she said.

“I could.”

“Or I could leave one.”

“You could.”

Neither of them said what the note might contain. The silence between them wasn’t empty, but it wasn’t a bridge, either. It was simply the place where two people sat when they had reached the end of what they knew how to say.

At the schoolhouse, they carried the damp blanket upstairs and hung it over the radiator. Theo went to his room. Mara stood in the corridor with the white shell in her coat pocket, listening to the radiator knock.

She heard his door close, then open again.

“Good night,” he called.

“Good night.”

In the morning, there was a note on the kitchen table, beneath her empty mug.

It said: *The kettle in the basement is broken.*

Mara smiled. She turned it over, but there was nothing on the other side.

At noon she left the shell on the windowsill, where the next residents would find it. Outside, the road was dry. The sea, hidden beyond the town, kept moving toward whatever came next.
