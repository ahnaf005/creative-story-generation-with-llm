### The Blue Room

At St. Anselm’s, everyone seemed to belong to three places at once: a committee, a group chat, and somebody else’s bed. Mara belonged to the Blue Room.

It was a low-ceilinged common room in the old student union, with lopsided sofas and a radiator that clanked like a person trying not to interrupt. She did her reading there, worked her café shifts there, and sometimes slept there when the library closed and her rented room felt too far away. The university planned to turn it into offices. Nobody was surprised. The university had a way of calling things “underused” just before taking them away.

Carl said they could stop it.

“You’re good with people,” he told Mara, leaning across the Blue Room’s battered coffee table. He had the kind of face that made even a request feel like an invitation. “Help us build the campaign. We need someone who actually uses the place.”

He was the student union’s events officer, the sort who knew the names of security guards and visiting professors, and seemed to enjoy both equally. Mara liked that he could talk to anyone. She liked, too, that when he looked at her, she felt chosen rather than merely noticed.

So she helped. She made posters. She persuaded people from the film club and the rowing team to come to meetings. At the café, Carl waited until her shift ended and walked her home, telling stories that made the cold seem like part of the evening’s charm.

“You never have to perform for me,” he said once, after she’d spent the whole walk laughing at his jokes.

She had laughed because the jokes were funny. Mostly.

Lizzy, her roommate, listened to the account from her bed, painting one thumbnail silver.

“He says that to everyone,” she said.

“Does he?”

“Not everyone. Just people he wants to like him.”

Lizzy said things like that: half warning, half joke, leaving Mara to decide whether she’d been helped or insulted. They had been friends since the first week, when Lizzy had lent Mara a coat and then announced, with no evidence at all, that they would be friends for life.

Martin thought Carl was trouble, though Martin thought most people were trouble once they’d been elected to anything. He edited the student paper and sat at meetings with his arms folded, as if somebody had asked him to judge a competition he’d already won.

“You’re making him sound like the campaign,” he said when Mara told him Carl wanted her to help with the speech.

“He asked me to write some lines.”

“Same thing.”

“You don’t like him because he got the events job.”

“I don’t like him because he likes hearing himself talk.”

Martin had been her friend since the first term. He knew when she was pretending to understand a reading and when she was pretending not to care. This should have been comforting. Instead, it sometimes felt like being known by someone who had kept all the evidence.

Johan was harder to place. He took photographs for the student paper, but rarely wrote captions longer than necessary. He spent late afternoons in the print room, developing pictures in the red light, and he seemed perfectly content to let silence finish its sentence. Mara had met him in the Blue Room when he asked if he could photograph the posters there.

“You can photograph the room,” she said. “The posters are mostly bad.”

“They’ll look better in black and white.”

“That’s what people say about bad posters.”

He smiled. He didn’t turn the smile into a performance. Mara noticed this more than she meant to.

As the campaign gathered pace, she found herself moving between circles like a careful waiter carrying a tray. Carl wanted energy; Martin wanted figures; Lizzy wanted somebody to remember that the women’s group had already been turned away from the union twice. Johan wanted to know whether the room had good light in the morning. Everyone was right, in the way people were right when they wanted you to do what they had already decided.

Mara was good at smoothing the sharp edges. She said “we should include that” and “that’s fair” and “let’s talk after the meeting.” It was easier than saying: I’m tired of being useful only when I can make you all agree.

Carl asked her to speak at the final campaign meeting. “Tell them why the room matters,” he said. “Not the official version. Yours.”

She wrote something on the back of a café receipt. The Blue Room had mattered, she wrote, because it was the first place at St. Anselm’s where she didn’t have to explain why she stayed late. At home, her mother worked evenings at the hospital and left the kitchen light on, even when she was too tired to wait up. A light could be a promise without anyone saying a word.

She read the lines to Lizzy in their room, then felt foolish for having read them aloud.

“That’s lovely,” Lizzy said.

“It’s a bit much.”

“It’s yours.”

Mara tucked the receipt into her notebook. She did not show it to Carl. She didn’t want the sentence polished or made useful. And yet, after he asked again, she told him about the kitchen light. Just the image, not the words. He listened quietly, which felt like trust. Then he kissed her, and the moment became something she wanted to keep.

On the evening of the campaign meeting, the hall was crowded. Carl stood at the front, smiling at the students who had come to hear him, and Mara sat beside Lizzy in the second row. Martin was near the back. Johan held his camera down at his side.

Carl began with the numbers: how many clubs used the room, how many hours it was open. Then he spoke about belonging, about the kind of place students needed when the rest of university felt like a test.

“My friend Mara told me something,” he said. “Her mother used to leave a light on in the kitchen when she worked nights. Not because Mara needed to be told to come home, but because the light said somebody expected her.”

The hall went still. Mara felt the sentence move through the room and leave her behind.

Carl continued. “That’s what the Blue Room does for people. It keeps a light on.”

It was a good line. It was the line she had written.

Applause came before she could decide what to feel. Carl found her face in the crowd, and his expression asked for approval. She stood, though nobody had asked her to, and walked out of the hall.

Lizzy caught up in the corridor.

“Wait.”

“How did he get the words?”

Lizzy’s face changed. So that was the answer.

“I sent him the receipt,” she said. “You’d left your notebook open, and I thought—he wanted something personal. I thought it would help.”

“You read my notebook?”

“I didn’t mean to—”

“You sent it to him.”

“I thought you’d be glad when he used it. It’s beautiful, Mara.”

It was possible to be grateful and furious at the same time. That was one of the things no one mentioned about friendship. Lizzy looked genuinely sorry; Mara couldn’t make herself turn that into innocence.

“You don’t get to decide what I’m glad about,” she said.

Then she kept walking.

Outside, the cold hit her face. She was angry with Lizzy, but the anger wouldn’t stay put. Some of it turned toward Carl, some toward herself. She had wanted him to hear her. She had offered the story and still imagined there was a line between offering and giving it away. Had she made that line clear? She had, hadn’t she? The questions circled without landing.

Johan found her by the bicycle racks.

“I’m not going back in,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to ask you to.”

They walked to the river path. After a while, he said, “He shouldn’t have used it without asking.”

“No.” She looked at the dark water. “But I keep thinking I helped him do it.”

Johan waited. He didn’t rush to rescue her from the thought.

“You told him about your mother,” he said at last. “You didn’t hand him the sentence.”

Mara breathed out. It was a small distinction, perhaps. It was enough to stand on.

The next day she went to the student paper’s office and found Martin at his desk, reading the meeting notes.

“You were right about Carl,” she said.

“I’d rather not be.”

“I’m speaking at the forum tonight. The real one. Not a speech he’s already written for me.”

Martin leaned back. “Good.”

“Don’t sound so pleased.”

“I’m not pleased. I’m relieved you’re not going to let him tell the story for you.”

There it was again: Martin, knowing her almost too well. She sat opposite him.

“You could have said that without making me feel stupid.”

“I could have said lots of things better.” He closed the notebook. “I’m sorry.”

It was the first apology he’d made that didn’t come with a lesson tucked inside. Mara accepted it, though she didn’t feel obliged to turn the moment into anything more.

At the forum, Carl was there, of course, standing with his hands in his pockets. Lizzy sat beside him, not looking at Mara. The administration had sent a dean who kept saying “flexible learning environment” as if the phrase might soothe anyone.

When Mara’s turn came, she left her notes on the chair.

“I don’t want to talk about the kitchen light,” she said. “That’s private, and it’s mine. I do want to talk about this room. It’s where students wait between shifts, where clubs meet when they can’t afford a hall, where people can stay without buying something. If the university closes it, the room won’t become less necessary. The people who need it will just have fewer places to go.”

Her voice shook on the first sentence. After that, it steadied. She spoke about the groups that had been turned away, the hours the room was locked, the practical ways it could be better used. This time she didn’t smooth anyone’s objections into agreement. She let the silences sit.

The university agreed to delay the decision for a year and form a student committee to review the space. Not a victory, exactly. A door left open, for now.

Afterward, Lizzy came to her.

“I’m sorry,” she said. No explanation this time. “I should have asked.”

“Yes,” Mara said.

Lizzy waited, wanting, perhaps, to be told they were still friends. Mara wanted to offer it. She wanted everything to be repaired because she hated the feeling of a cracked thing between them. But repair wasn’t the same as pretending there had been no crack.

“I need a little time,” she said.

Lizzy nodded. “Okay.”

Carl tried to speak to her too. He said he’d thought she wanted the story heard. She said she’d wanted him to hear it. He seemed unable to understand why those were different things. Maybe he would later. Maybe he wouldn’t. She was no longer required to wait for him to understand before believing herself.

That evening, Johan was in the Blue Room, taking pictures of the late sun on the battered sofas. The room looked almost handsome in the light.

“Still bad posters?” he asked.

“Worse in color.”

He lowered the camera. “Would you like to get coffee with me? Somewhere with no campaign.”

Mara smiled. She liked him. She liked that he had asked, and that the answer could be hers to give.

“Not tonight,” she said. “But yes. Soon.”

“Soon is good.”

When he left, she sat on the sofa by the radiator. The clanking began again, familiar and imperfect. She thought of the kitchen light at home, of her mother sleeping after a shift, of the sentence she had written and the one Carl had spoken. They were not the same thing. She could keep one and let the other go.

For once, she didn’t start working out what everyone in the room needed from her. She stayed where she was, with the light falling across her hands, and let the silence belong to her.
