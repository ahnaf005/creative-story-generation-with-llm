# The Missing Layer

The first sign was a brown stain above the seminar-room door, shaped like a country no one could identify.

“It’s spreading,” said Dr. Vale, standing beneath it with her hands on her hips.

“It has been spreading for three years,” said Professor Nouri. “It simply has more confidence now.”

The faculty occupied the top two floors of Wren Hall, a handsome stone building that had once housed the university’s library. Its windows were tall, its corridors dignified, and its roof, according to a report no one had read until the stain appeared, was missing a critical waterproofing layer.

The report had been commissioned after a storm sent water down the walls of the dean’s office. It had concluded that the old membrane beneath the roof tiles had “deteriorated beyond practical repair.” A second report, commissioned to review the first, concluded that the membrane had apparently never been installed in the first place.

The dean, Professor Lyle, announced the repairs at a Monday meeting.

“Work will begin during the summer recess,” he said.

“Will it be finished before term?” asked Vale.

Lyle glanced at the schedule. “That is the intention.”

He did not say whose intention.

By September, the scaffolding had swallowed the north side of Wren Hall. A plastic tunnel led from the pavement to the entrance, and the building’s usual quiet was replaced by hammering, drilling, and the scrape of metal against stone. The contractors had promised to work from eight until four. At eight, they began. At four, they stopped. The university’s timetable, however, continued as though nothing had changed.

The first week of term, Professor Nouri tried to lecture on the history of political thought while a pneumatic drill worked above him. He raised his voice. The drill raised its own.

“Perhaps,” he shouted, “we should discuss Hobbes’s account of the state of nature!”

The students laughed. He did not.

When the drill finally paused, Nouri leaned against the lectern, waiting. Somewhere overhead, a worker dropped something heavy. The ceiling gave a brief, disconcerting shiver.

The students looked up.

“Natural philosophy,” Nouri said, and carried on.

The construction notice beside the lift assured everyone that “all reasonable measures” were being taken to reduce noise and disruption. It also advised staff to use the south stairwell, which was closed for repairs to a handrail loosened, it seemed, by the scaffolding.

By October, the workers had removed the roof tiles and begun stripping away the old layers beneath them. The weather turned wet. Temporary coverings flapped above the faculty, and the smell of damp plaster settled into the upper corridors. In Dr. Vale’s office, the brown stain developed a second, darker border. She moved her books away from the wall and placed a plastic storage box beneath the leak.

The box filled overnight.

Facilities sent a caretaker with a mop and a bucket. He looked at the ceiling, then at the bucket.

“It’s not the ceiling,” he said. “It’s the roof.”

“I’m relieved to hear the distinction,” Vale replied.

A week later, a contractor arrived with a ladder and painted over the stain. The paint was still wet when the next rain came. Water emerged at the same spot, followed by a thin stream that trickled down the new white surface and collected in the bucket.

The faculty began keeping its own records. Nouri photographed every leak. Vale made a spreadsheet. The department administrator, Mina, logged complaints in a folder she labeled ROOF, though the folder soon contained messages about noise, dust, blocked doors, and a meeting that had been relocated three times in one afternoon.

The worst inconvenience was the loss of rooms. Seminar rooms were needed for contractors to store materials. Offices were emptied so workers could inspect the walls. The department’s weekly meeting was held first in the language lab, then in a borrowed classroom across campus, then in the dean’s office, where everyone sat among boxes of old university brochures.

Even the temporary arrangements became permanent in their own way. A graduate student wrote a thesis chapter in the cafeteria because the library annex smelled of wet insulation. Professor Hsu held office hours in the stairwell, where students could hear her but had to stand sideways to avoid the traffic. Nouri conducted a doctoral examination over a video call from his kitchen after a crew arrived without warning to cut into the ceiling above his desk.

The contractors said the delays were caused by unexpected conditions.

The university said it had been assured the building was safe.

The dean said the works were “progressing in a complex environment.”

No one said that the repair had become the environment.

In November, a new problem arrived in the form of dust. Fine gray particles settled along the windowsills and on desks. The cleaners wiped them away each evening, but by morning they had returned. Vale began coughing during lectures. Mina developed headaches. Hsu, who had asthma, kept an inhaler beside her keyboard and stopped coming in on days when the air seemed especially stale.

“Have they tested it?” Vale asked at a meeting.

“They’re looking into it,” said Lyle.

“Who is?”

“I’ve asked facilities.”

“And facilities?”

“They’ve asked the contractor.”

The contractor’s response, when it came, was that the dust was consistent with ordinary building work. A notice was placed in the lobby advising those with “pre-existing sensitivities” to take precautions.

Hsu read it and folded it carefully in half.

“My lungs are not a pre-existing administrative inconvenience,” she said.

She began working from home, then from her sister’s apartment, where the internet was poor but the air was clean. Her students emailed her questions. She answered them in the evening, apologizing for delays she did not owe anyone.

The university eventually brought in an environmental consultant. For one morning, everyone was asked to leave Wren Hall while measurements were taken. The consultants wore masks. The faculty watched from the pavement, beneath the scaffolding, while students hurried past with coffee and umbrellas.

The report arrived three weeks later. It found no immediate hazard, though it recommended “enhanced cleaning and improved ventilation during continuing works.” The windows, which had been sealed against the noise, could not be opened. The ventilation system had been shut off because dust from the construction might enter it.

“So,” said Nouri, “we must keep the dust out by keeping the air out.”

Lyle placed the report on the meeting table. “The important thing is that there is no immediate hazard.”

“Then why are we all coughing?” asked Vale.

No one answered. Outside, a saw whined against stone.

By winter, the faculty had grown skilled at endurance. They learned which corridor smelled least of damp and which stairwell was usually open. They carried earplugs. They moved meetings without complaint, or with complaints so familiar that they had become a kind of greeting.

“How’s your office?”

“Currently dry.”

“Ah. A good day.”

The university extended the repair schedule through February, then April. Rain found new routes as the old materials were removed. A drip appeared above the copy machine, so Mina shifted it to the opposite wall. A second drip appeared above its new position. They moved it again, to a corner near the fire door, where a contractor promptly stored a box of tiles.

The faculty grew irritable with one another. Nouri accused Vale of monopolizing the only room with a working radiator. Vale accused Nouri of leaving windows open during the rare hours when they could be opened. Hsu, returning for a single meeting, listened to them argue and said, “The building is falling apart, and we are blaming each other for the weather.”

They fell quiet.

After that, they tried to be kinder. Not always successfully. They were tired, and tiredness made small grievances feel large. A missing chair could become evidence of neglect. A delayed email could become an insult. Even the word “temporary” began to sound like a threat.

In March, Lyle called them together to explain that the university had approved additional funding. The waterproofing layer would be installed properly, he said, and the interior damage repaired. The work would take longer than expected, but it would solve the underlying problem.

“Will we be out of the building?” Vale asked.

“Some areas will be closed.”

“Which areas?”

“That has not been finalized.”

Nouri leaned back. “At least we know the roof is being fixed.”

“Eventually,” said Mina.

“Eventually,” Lyle agreed.

That evening, Vale stayed late to pack the books that were still stacked beside her office wall. The stain above the door had been painted over again, though a faint outline remained beneath the new coat. She found a postcard tucked between two volumes, sent years earlier by a former colleague who had moved to another university. On the back, in blue ink, was a sentence: *The new building leaks less, though the heating is worse.*

Vale smiled despite herself. There was always something.

A heavy rain began while she was still sorting. She heard it pattering against the plastic covering above the roof, then a hollow tapping somewhere in the corridor. She followed the sound to the seminar room. A bucket had been placed beneath the old stain, though no one could remember who had brought it there. Water fell into it at steady intervals.

Plink.

Plink.

Plink.

Vale stood watching for a moment. Then she adjusted the bucket so the drops landed in the center.

In the morning, she told Mina about it.

“I’ll add it to the spreadsheet,” Mina said.

“Don’t.”

Mina looked at her.

“Let the bucket have its privacy.”

They laughed, quietly, as though the walls might be listening.

Spring came. The scaffolding remained, but the workers began laying the new waterproof membrane. The university issued another notice. This one promised that “substantial progress” had been made. The faculty read it, shrugged, and went back to teaching.

Hsu returned for two days a week. Nouri’s seminar moved into a room with a ceiling that was still intact. Vale’s office stayed dry through one storm, then another. Each time, someone checked the ceiling. Each time, nothing happened.

They did not celebrate. They had learned not to give a dry patch too much meaning.

In June, the department held its annual gathering in the garden because the common room was still closed. The grass was uneven, the chairs borrowed, and the sky threatened rain. They brought sandwiches and paper cups of lemonade. Hsu sat in the shade. Mina took photographs of everyone before they could escape.

“What do we call this year?” a student asked.

“Character building,” said Nouri.

“Water building,” said Vale.

The student wrote both down.

They ate while a crew worked on the roof above them. Now and then came the dull knock of a hammer. The sound was distant, almost companionable. For a little while, the faculty talked about books, students, holidays, and things they had not been able to discuss over the noise.

The next year would bring more repairs. There would be invoices, closures, new stains, and arguments about where the department should be housed. The building would not become sound all at once, and neither would the people inside it. But the missing layer was finally being replaced. Water, for now, had been given a boundary.

As the first drops began to fall, everyone looked up.

Nothing came through the open sky.

Mina lifted her cup. “To the roof.”

“To the roof,” they echoed.

Then they gathered their things and went inside, not hopeful exactly, but willing to see what morning brought.
