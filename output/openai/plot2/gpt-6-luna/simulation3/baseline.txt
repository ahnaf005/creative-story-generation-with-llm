### The Space Between

On February 12, 1966, Barbara Weiss woke before dawn to the sound of snow ticking against the bedroom window.

She was eight months pregnant and had learned to measure the day by what her body would permit. Getting out of bed required a slow turn and a hand on the mattress. Pulling on her stockings was out of the question. She had given up shoes with laces and, after losing an argument with her husband, the idea that she could carry groceries.

But she was determined to go to synagogue.

“You can stay home,” David said, watching her fasten her coat.

“I can,” Barbara replied. “I don’t want to.”

He held the door while she stepped carefully onto the front walk. The snow had softened the neighborhood, muffling the traffic and laying a clean white edge along the rooftops. Across Southfield, church bells and car horns sounded far away. Barbara tucked her scarf around her neck and rested a gloved hand over the child inside her.

“Your mother is going to make you very familiar with this place,” she murmured.

The baby answered with a small, deliberate kick.

At the synagogue, people greeted her as if she had returned from a long journey rather than crossed a few icy streets. Mrs. Levin took her arm. Young Michael Stein offered to carry her prayer book. Rabbi Adler, passing through the foyer, smiled and asked after the baby.

“Still making his opinions known,” Barbara said.

“Then he will fit in here,” the rabbi replied.

He was a large, warm-voiced man, renowned beyond the congregation for his sermons and his tireless work with families who had arrived in the city with almost nothing. Barbara had heard him speak at weddings and funerals, and once, memorably, in the kitchen of a widow whose husband had died too young. He had not offered an explanation for the loss. He had sat at her table until she stopped apologizing for crying.

Now he nodded to Barbara and went inside.

She took her place near the aisle. David sat beside her, close enough to steady her if she needed to stand. The sanctuary filled slowly: damp coats, quiet greetings, the rustle of prayer books. Outside, the snow continued to fall.

For a while, the service followed its familiar path. Barbara’s back ached. She shifted in her seat and tried to focus on the music, on the rise and fall of the congregation’s voices. The baby grew still, listening—or perhaps asleep. She thought of the small room they had prepared at home, the folded blankets, the little wooden crib David had spent two Sundays assembling.

Then a young man came through the side entrance.

Barbara noticed him because he did not look toward anyone. He wore a dark coat and kept one hand in a pocket. He was perhaps twenty, perhaps younger. There was something pinched about his face, as if he had been out in the cold too long. He stood near the back for a moment, looking toward the front.

The rabbi was speaking.

The young man drew a gun.

The first sound did not fit the room. It was too sharp, too sudden, a crack that seemed to split the air rather than travel through it. Barbara saw Rabbi Adler flinch and fall. Then people were shouting, and the sanctuary broke apart.

David pulled Barbara down. Someone pushed past them. A child cried. The congregation surged toward the doors, every person trying to escape at once, though no one seemed to know where safety was. Barbara was pressed against the end of the pew. She heard another shot and then, amid the noise, a terrible silence that lasted no more than a second.

When she lifted her head, she saw the young man standing alone.

He had turned the gun on himself.

Barbara did not think. She moved.

She pushed one hand against the pew and tried to rise, the other arm wrapped around her belly. David caught her coat.

“Barbara!”

The crowd swept between them. A man stumbled backward into her; a woman seized Barbara’s sleeve, trying to pull her along. Someone shouted to get outside. Another shot rang out, or perhaps a book had fallen—Barbara would never afterward be sure. She tried to move toward the young man, toward the fallen rabbi, toward anything that made sense of what had happened.

The crush carried her the other way.

“Let me through!” she cried.

No one heard. A shoulder struck her cheek. Her feet left the floor for an instant, and she clutched at the backs of the pews until David found her again. He wrapped both arms around her and dragged her toward the side aisle, where the press of people thinned.

At the door she twisted around.

She could see only backs, coats, a scattering of prayer books. The young man had disappeared from view. Rabbi Adler was hidden by the people trying to reach him.

Barbara had wanted to go to him. She had wanted, with the unreasonable certainty of the terrified, to do something that would undo what had just happened. Instead she was outside, held upright by David, breathing snow-cold air into her lungs.

For several minutes, the congregation gathered on the steps and in the parking lot. Some people were crying. Others stood with their hands hanging at their sides, looking back at the building. A woman had lost one shoe. Michael Stein kept saying, “I saw him—I saw him,” though no one asked what he had seen.

David put his coat around Barbara’s shoulders. She shook so hard her teeth clicked.

“Is the baby—” she began.

“We’ll go to the hospital,” he said.

“I’m all right.”

“I know. We’re going anyway.”

In the car, she pressed both palms to her stomach. The child was moving. One kick, then another. She counted them until David pulled away from the curb and the synagogue disappeared behind the snow.

At the hospital, a nurse listened to the baby’s heartbeat and told Barbara that it sounded strong. The relief came so suddenly that Barbara began to sob. David sat beside her, holding her hand, and said nothing. She was grateful for his silence.

That night, the news reached their house by telephone. Rabbi Adler was dead. The young man was dead too. The authorities were asking anyone who had seen the incident to speak with them. Barbara sat at the kitchen table while David poured tea she did not drink.

She told him about the young man’s face.

“You saw his face?” he asked gently.

“Only for a moment.”

“What did it look like?”

She searched for an answer and found none. A face pinched by cold. A face young enough to be anyone’s son. A face she had never seen before and would remember for the rest of her life.

In the days that followed, people tried to make the event smaller by explaining it. They repeated what they had heard: that the shooter had been troubled, that he had been angry, that someone should have noticed. They spoke of warning signs and rumors, of things said in confidence and things said too late. Barbara listened, but none of it changed the image that returned whenever she closed her eyes: the young man standing alone at the back of the sanctuary, before the room turned into a crowd.

She went to Rabbi Adler’s funeral with David beside her. She wore a dark coat and walked slowly. People made room for her, then closed the space again. The rabbi’s wife sat in the front row, one hand resting on the hand of her daughter. Barbara wanted to tell her about the kitchen years ago, about how the rabbi had stayed until a grieving woman no longer had to apologize. She wanted to tell her that his kindness had traveled farther than anyone could know.

Instead, she sat down and listened.

When the baby came, six weeks later, it was a boy. Barbara and David named him Aaron, after David’s grandfather. She had considered other names, some suggested by family, one chosen because it sounded good with Weiss. But when she held the child and saw his small, furious expression, Aaron seemed right: a name with a history, sturdy enough to be carried.

In the quiet weeks at home, Barbara sometimes woke in the night and listened to the baby breathe. She would remember the synagogue, the gunshot, the crowd moving like a single frightened creature. She would remember the helplessness of reaching out and being pulled away.

She had once believed that kindness was something a person did by choice: a hand offered, a meal delivered, a child comforted. After February 12, she understood that there were moments when kindness could not reach the person it was meant for. The crowd could carry you away. A door could close. A life could end before anyone found the right words.

Still, she could not accept that the failure to reach someone meant the reaching had been useless.

One spring afternoon, she took Aaron to the synagogue. The snow was gone. Sunlight fell through the foyer windows, touching the polished floor. Barbara held her son beneath his blankets and stood for a moment where she had last seen Rabbi Adler.

She did not ask herself what she could have done differently. There was no answer that would restore the dead, and she had begun to understand that some questions were only another way of hurting oneself.

Instead, she adjusted the blanket around Aaron’s cheek.

The child opened his eyes, briefly, and looked up at her.

Barbara kissed his forehead and carried him inside.
