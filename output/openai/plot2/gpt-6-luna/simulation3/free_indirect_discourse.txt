### The Space Between

On the morning of February 12, Barbara woke with a stitch in her side and the solemn conviction that the baby had turned around.

She stood in the kitchen, one hand under her belly, waiting for the familiar flutter. There. A heel, perhaps. Or an elbow. The baby had been busy all night, as if impatient with the small, dim room it had been given.

“You’ll have to wait,” she told it. “There’s a service first.”

Her husband, David, had already gone to the synagogue to help with the morning preparations. Barbara dressed slowly, button by button, then sat to lace her shoes. She was seven months along and tired of people telling her she looked wonderful. She did not feel wonderful. She felt enormous, tender, and forever on the verge of needing to sit down.

Still, Saturday morning was Saturday morning. Rabbi Adler was speaking, and even the baby ought to hear him.

The synagogue stood in Southfield, just beyond the familiar streets of Detroit, its windows pale with winter light. Inside, the air smelled of wool coats, polished wood, and the coffee someone had forgotten to cover in the side room. People greeted Barbara with warm hands and careful smiles.

“Any day now?”

“Not for two months,” she said, and heard herself answer it as if two months were nothing.

Rabbi Adler was already at the front. He was a small man with silver hair and a voice that seemed to make room for everyone listening. Barbara liked the way he paused before a sentence, as though he were setting it down gently. She had been thinking of a question to ask him about the baby—about blessing a child before it was born, or whether a mother’s fear could be mistaken for wisdom. She had not yet decided how to put it.

David was across the room, speaking with the cantor. He looked up and caught her eye. His smile was the one he used when he wanted her to know he had saved her a seat.

She was halfway there when the young man came in.

At first Barbara noticed only that he was not dressed for the weather. His coat was open, and he held himself stiffly, as if he had come a long way and had not yet decided whether to stay. He was very young. Younger than David, perhaps. A narrow face, dark hair. He looked past the people who turned to see him.

Then there was a sharp sound.

For an instant the room made no sense. A book slipped from someone’s hand. The rabbi’s voice stopped in the middle of a word. Barbara thought, absurdly, that a lamp had fallen.

Then the young man raised the gun again.

The second sound split the first one open. People shouted. A chair toppled. Someone near the aisle pushed hard against Barbara’s shoulder, and she stumbled, catching herself on the back of a pew.

Rabbi Adler had fallen.

No. He had bent down. He was reaching for something. Had he dropped a page? Surely he had only—

The young man turned the gun on himself.

After that, the room became noise and movement. A cry came from the front. Another from behind her. The crowd surged toward the doors, not because anyone had called for it, but because everyone’s body seemed to know before the mind did. Barbara was pushed sideways, then backward. Her hand found the pew rail, held it, slipped.

David! She couldn’t see him. She could see a gray hat, a woman’s wide-open mouth, a child being lifted over someone’s arm. The door was ahead, and people were pressing through it so fiercely that the frame shook.

She should go with them. Of course she should. The baby was inside her, alive and kicking, and there were too many bodies, too many hands. She must get out.

But the young man was still there, on the floor, and people were moving away from him.

He was only a boy. That was the thought that came, stupid and bright as a match. A boy who had done this. A boy who might still be helped.

“Wait,” Barbara tried to say. No one heard her.

She pushed toward the front. The crowd carried her the other way. She turned sideways, one palm over her belly, the other reaching out between shoulders. Someone’s elbow struck her ribs. A man grabbed her arm and pulled.

“Come on!”

“I have to—”

“Barbara!”

David’s voice, somewhere behind her. His hand found her coat. The crowd shoved, and she was swept with it through the doorway and into the cold.

Outside, people spilled across the steps. The winter air struck Barbara’s face, clean and brutal. She gulped it in. Her knees trembled. David caught her by both shoulders.

“Are you hurt?”

The baby moved beneath her hand. A hard little nudge. No, not hurt. Not yet.

Inside, people were calling for a doctor. Someone else was saying to keep back. The doors had been left open, and the synagogue seemed to be holding its breath around the terrible sound.

Barbara leaned toward the entrance.

“Don’t,” David said. He was pale, his fingers tight around her arms. “You can’t go back in.”

The words were true, which made them worse.

She looked at his face, then past him to the doorway. She could still reach the young man. She had only to get through the door. Maybe he was breathing. Maybe he was frightened. Maybe somewhere in him, beneath the gun and the terrible act, there was a boy who wanted someone to say his name.

But the crowd had filled the entrance. Men were going in now, steady and careful. Barbara’s legs had begun to shake too badly to carry her. The baby pressed downward, heavy and insistent.

She had been swept out, and there was no way to sweep herself back.

In the hours that followed, the day became a series of rooms. The synagogue. A doctor’s office. The quiet room at home where David sat beside her without taking off his coat. People came and went. Phones rang. Nobody knew what to say, and everyone said something.

Rabbi Adler was dead.

The young man was dead too.

Barbara heard both facts repeated until they seemed to belong to the same sentence, though she could not make them belong together. The rabbi had spoken that morning. The young man had entered in an open coat. She had seen his face. A young face. That detail came back over and over, unwanted and sharp.

At night she sat on the edge of the bed, listening to the house settle. David asked if she wanted tea. She said no. He asked if she wanted him to call her sister. She said no again. The baby rolled inside her, slow and deliberate.

How could it move so calmly? How could the world go on making a child while a man lay dead in a synagogue?

She pressed both hands to her belly. The baby answered with a small thump.

All right, she thought. You’re here. That counts for something.

In the days afterward, people asked Barbara what she had seen. She tried to tell them, but what she remembered most was not the gun or the shouting. It was the moment before. Rabbi Adler pausing over a word. David smiling across the room. The young man standing in the doorway, seeming not to belong anywhere.

People wanted the story to have edges. They wanted the beginning and the end. Barbara had only the middle: the instant when she knew the rabbi had fallen, and the instant when she knew she could not reach the boy.

Weeks later, her daughter was born, small and furious, with a crease between her brows. Barbara held her against her chest and watched her breathe. She was supposed to feel only joy. There it was, yes, fierce and tender. But grief was there too, quiet as a shadow at the edge of the bed.

She named the baby Leah.

When Leah was old enough to ask why people spoke softly at the mention of Rabbi Adler, Barbara told her about the morning in the synagogue. She left out the worst of the noise. She told her about the rabbi’s voice, about the winter light, about the young man who had come through the door looking very young.

“And you went to help him?” Leah asked.

Barbara looked at her daughter’s face. At the earnestness in it. The belief that a person could reach another person if only she tried hard enough.

“I tried,” she said.

And she had. That mattered. It had to matter.

Sometimes, even years later, Barbara woke with her palm against her belly, though it was empty now, and heard the old confusion rise around her: the shout, the rush of bodies, the door giving way. She would lie still until the room returned—the familiar lamp, David’s breathing, the quiet child asleep down the hall.

The world had carried her out of the synagogue that morning. It had kept carrying her after.

She had not been able to save the rabbi, or the young man, or even herself from the memory. But there had been a child beneath her hand, insisting on life. She had felt that small, stubborn movement through the crush.

A heel, perhaps.

Or an elbow.
