### The Space Between

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara put on her blue wool coat and discovered that she could no longer fasten it.

She stood in front of the bedroom mirror, one hand holding the coat closed over her belly, the other trying the button again. It had been a hopeful gesture, really. The button had not closed in weeks. The baby was due in April, and already seemed to have claimed every inch of her.

“Leave it open,” her husband called from the hall. “It’s only a short walk from the car.”

Only a short walk. He said that about everything lately. The walk from the parking lot, the stairs at her mother’s, the aisle at the grocery store. He could say it because he did not have to carry the child inside him.

Still, she smiled. He was trying to be kind.

At the synagogue in Southfield, Detroit’s winter had followed them indoors. The entryway smelled of wet wool and floor polish. People stamped snow from their shoes and greeted one another in low, familiar voices. A little boy ran past with one mitten missing. Someone laughed. Someone else asked after Barbara’s mother. The ordinary questions were a comfort: How was she feeling? Was the baby kicking? Had they chosen a name?

Not yet, Barbara said. There was time.

There was time for everything, she thought, until there wasn’t.

She eased herself into a seat with space beside it, one hand pressed to the small of her back. The sanctuary was filling. On the bimah, Rabbi Adler arranged his papers. He was a renowned man, but not distant; his face, when he looked up, seemed to recognize each person in turn. Barbara had heard him speak at funerals and weddings, at services when her own mind was elsewhere. He made difficult things sound possible to bear. Not easy, exactly. Possible.

Her husband leaned close and whispered something about the rabbi’s sermon. Barbara nodded, though she hadn’t heard. A flutter moved beneath her ribs. The baby, perhaps. Or her own heart, working too hard.

Then the young man came in.

She noticed him because he did not look at anyone. His coat was dark, his hair untidy, his face pale in the overheated room. He stood near the back as if uncertain where to go. It was a small thing, a detail the mind could have put away. People arrived late. People came in carrying private troubles. A synagogue was full of them.

Rabbi Adler began to speak.

The young man moved.

For a moment, Barbara did not understand what she was seeing. There was a sound—not a word, not quite a crash, but something that seemed to split the air. Heads turned. Rabbi Adler’s papers slid from his hands. The room broke into motion.

Someone screamed.

A second report struck the sanctuary. Then came shouting, the scrape of chairs, the thunder of people rushing toward the doors. Barbara’s husband had her arm. She heard him say her name, but it came from far away, as though he were calling across water.

The young man held a gun.

No. That couldn’t be right. Guns belonged in newspapers and police dramas, not here, not among the prayer books and polished wood. Rabbi Adler was on the floor. The white pages around him looked almost luminous.

Barbara tried to stand. Her body was slow, a room she had to cross before she could reach the door. Someone crashed against her shoulder; someone else grabbed at the back of her coat. The crowd surged, not meanly, not on purpose. Fear had its own weight. It pressed from every side.

“Wait,” she said. “Wait—”

Her husband was somewhere behind her. Or in front. She could not see him. The aisle had become a current, and she was caught in it, pushed past the ends of the pews. Her hand reached out, searching for a railing, a shoulder, anything fixed.

Then she saw the young man again.

He had turned the gun toward himself.

There was still a space between them. Not much. A few steps, perhaps. Barbara could cross it. She could say something. What would she say? Don’t. Stop. You’re not alone. The words crowded her mouth, useless and urgent.

She pulled against the bodies carrying her toward the exit.

“Let me through!”

A man’s elbow struck her chest. She curled around her belly, protecting the baby. The crowd swept her sideways. She reached again, fingers open, but caught only the sleeve of a stranger’s coat. It tore free.

A third report.

The sanctuary’s noise collapsed into a terrible, uneven quiet. Then the shouting began again, louder, full of names. Barbara was pushed through the doors and into the entryway. Someone caught her before she fell. She was outside now, or nearly outside, where the cold air rushed in around the open doors.

Her knees shook. Her coat hung open. She pressed both hands to her belly.

The baby moved.

Thank God. Or thank whoever was listening.

A woman was crying into her scarf. A man kept saying that everyone needed to move back, but there was nowhere to move that did not feel wrong. Barbara looked toward the doors. She had to go back. Rabbi Adler was inside. The young man was inside. Her husband—

She found him beside the steps, white-faced and breathing hard. He caught her around the shoulders.

“Barbara. Are you hurt?”

“No. I have to—”

He held tighter. Not roughly. He was shaking too.

Behind him, people poured out of the synagogue. Some were crying. Some stared straight ahead as if their eyes had been left behind. The young boy who had lost his mitten stood in the snow beside his mother. The mitten was still nowhere to be seen.

Barbara tried to turn again.

“Please,” she said, though she did not know whom she was asking. “I could have reached him.”

Her husband said nothing. There was no reply that could make the sentence untrue, or make it fair.

For hours afterward, the day came back to her in pieces. The blue coat she had failed to button. Rabbi Adler’s papers floating down. The terrible pull of the crowd. The young man’s face, though she could not have said what expression it wore. She wondered whether he had looked at anyone before raising the gun. Whether he had come in wanting to be stopped. Whether she had imagined that last clear space between them.

At the hospital, a nurse listened for the baby’s heartbeat. The room was warm and smelled of antiseptic. Barbara held her breath until the nurse smiled.

“There,” she said. “Strong.”

Strong. The word seemed too simple for what it had to carry.

Her husband sat beside her, his fingers laced through hers. Neither of them spoke of Rabbi Adler’s final sermon. Neither of them knew what the young man had been called by his mother, or what name he had once wanted to give a child of his own. Barbara did not know whether it was foolish to keep wondering about him. She wondered anyway.

In the days that followed, people brought food. They called. They told her she had been brave, though she had done nothing but be carried where the crowd took her. She accepted the casseroles and the condolences. She let people say what they needed to say. At night, she heard the gunshots again, and in the instant between them she saw herself moving toward the young man—not frightened, not pregnant, not trapped, but simply a person trying to reach another.

Then the baby would shift inside her, and the room would return.

When April came, she gave birth to a daughter. Barbara held the child against her and felt the small, warm weight settle into her arms. So much of life arrived without asking permission. So much could be lost before anyone understood what was happening.

She kissed her daughter’s head.

There was time, she thought—not the time she had once imagined, laid out neatly ahead of them, but the time they had, one breath and one choice at a time. And for now, this child was here. The world had not become kind. It had not become safe. But she was here, breathing, and Barbara could hold her.
