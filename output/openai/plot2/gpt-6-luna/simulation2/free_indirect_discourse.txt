### The Space Between

On February 12, 1966, Barbara woke before the alarm and lay listening to the furnace knock itself awake. Outside, Southfield was all snow-crust and gray light, the roads still dark where the plows had passed. The baby shifted beneath her ribs, a slow, insistent turn.

“Not today,” she whispered, though she knew babies had no respect for calendars.

Sam, already dressing, laughed softly from the bathroom. “You’ve been telling him that for weeks.”

Him. They had begun calling the baby him without deciding to. Barbara pressed a palm to the taut curve of her belly. There was the little heel, perhaps, or an elbow. Something answering. She took her time with her stockings, resting once on the edge of the bed, and let Sam carry her coat to the car.

The synagogue was warm and smelled of wool, old wood, and the faint sweet dust of prayer books. Barbara had always liked that smell. She liked the order of the place, too: the familiar voices, the worn steps, the way the stained glass turned even winter light into something deliberate. Rabbi Adler was speaking when they arrived. He had a voice that filled the room without needing to rise, and people leaned toward it as if he were telling them something meant for each of them alone.

He was renowned, though Barbara had never known what to do with the word. Renowned sounded like a man carved in stone. Rabbi Adler was a man who remembered that Sam’s mother took sugar in her tea and once stayed after a service to help a boy find a lost mitten. Still, when he spoke, the room listened.

Barbara eased herself onto the bench. The baby pressed upward, making breathing a small negotiation. She folded her hands over her belly and listened as the rabbi spoke about duty—not the grand kind, he said, but the duty of noticing. A person in trouble might not ask plainly. A person might sit at the edge of a room and wait to be seen.

That was what the rabbi had said. Barbara remembered it later, though at the time she was mostly wondering whether she had worn the wrong shoes.

Then a young man stood.

For a moment, Barbara thought he meant to leave. He was near the aisle, his coat buttoned high, his face pale with cold or nerves. He looked too young to be so sure of himself. The people nearest him turned, irritated at first, then uncertain. He had something in his hand.

No, Barbara thought. Not that.

The first shot cracked through the room. The sound was much smaller than it ought to have been, almost neat. Rabbi Adler stopped speaking. He looked down, as though he had dropped something, and then he fell.

The room broke open.

People shouted. Benches scraped. Someone screamed a name. Barbara’s body tried to rise, but the baby was heavy, her knees slow, and the people around her were already moving. Sam caught her arm.

“Barbara—”

The young man still held the gun. His eyes were wide. He looked less like a monster than a boy who had reached the end of some road and found no sign there. That was impossible, of course. A boy could not do this. A boy could not stand in a synagogue with a gun and make the air turn poisonous.

But there he was.

Barbara heard the rabbi’s voice in her mind: noticing. A person in trouble might not ask plainly.

She pulled her arm from Sam’s grasp.

“Barbara, no!”

She tried to move toward the young man. It was not courage, not exactly. Courage had a clean sound to it, and this was only the conviction that something had to be done, that everyone else was running and someone ought to go the other way. She thought of the baby inside her, turning, alive and unaware. She thought of the young man’s face. He looked frightened. He looked—God help her—alone.

The crowd carried her sideways.

A man slammed into her shoulder. Someone caught at her coat. A woman grabbed Barbara’s hand and dragged her toward the doorway, crying, “Come on, come on!” Barbara tried to pull free, but her feet slipped against the floor. The baby pressed hard beneath her ribs. There was no space to plant herself, no way to turn back. All around her, bodies surged toward the exit, each one certain the next step would save them.

Then came another shot.

She knew what it meant before she knew she had heard it. The noise struck the walls and seemed to stay there, trembling. The crowd drove her through the doorway and into the corridor. Sam was beside her now, his face drained of color, one hand braced at her back.

Barbara twisted around. Through the open doors she could see only a jumble of coats and shoulders, and beyond them the stillness where the rabbi had been speaking. She had not reached the young man. She had not even come close.

Outside, people poured into the winter daylight. Some ran across the parking lot; others stopped and stared at the building as if it might explain itself. A man in a dark suit was shouting for someone to call the police. The cold slapped Barbara’s cheeks. Her breath came fast and shallow.

“Are you hurt?” Sam asked.

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

She nodded. The baby moved, a small flutter under her hand. She wanted to say that she had tried. But trying was not an answer to anything.

They sat in the car while the first sirens came. Sam kept one hand on the steering wheel and the other around hers. Barbara could feel his fingers trembling. Her own hands were cold, though the car heater had begun to blow warm air.

The synagogue doors opened and closed. People came out in groups, some weeping, some too stunned to speak. Barbara watched them and waited for a face she knew. She wanted someone to say Rabbi Adler was alive. She wanted the young man to emerge under guard, his hands empty, his eyes lowered. She wanted the world to return to the shape it had had an hour ago, when the baby was kicking and she was thinking about lunch.

But the news came in fragments. The rabbi was dead. The young man was dead, too.

Dead. The word was so small. Barbara had imagined death as a thing that announced itself, a door slammed, a great silence. Here it came in scraps of speech, passed from one frightened person to another. Rabbi Adler, who knew people’s names. The young man, whose name Barbara did not know. Two lives reduced to a sentence repeated in the parking lot.

At home, Sam helped her out of the car and up the steps. She leaned on him, though she did not need to. She needed to feel another person there. In the kitchen, he put water on for tea and forgot to light the burner. Barbara sat at the table and listened to the house: the furnace ticking, the clock over the sink, the telephone ringing once and then stopping.

She kept seeing the young man’s face. Not the gun, not the moment the room emptied of sound. His face, before it happened. Perhaps she had made him frightened because she needed him to be frightened. Perhaps he had been angry, or numb, or nothing she could have understood. She could not know. She had seen him for only a few seconds. A person could not be reached in a few seconds. A person could not be saved because someone noticed.

And still, she had wanted to try.

The baby pressed a foot—or an elbow—against her palm. Barbara held it there, waiting. The child moved again, impatient and strong. She bent over her belly.

There would be a birth. There would be a room full of voices, and pain, and people telling her what to do. There would be a day when the child would ask what happened at the synagogue, and she would have to find words that did not make the world seem simple. She could not tell him that good people always stop bad things. She could not promise that fear could be reasoned with, or that reaching out would be enough.

She could tell him that Rabbi Adler had taught people to notice one another. She could tell him that, when the room began to run, she had tried to go toward someone who seemed alone. The crowd had swept her away. That was true, too.

Barbara rested both hands over the child. Outside, the afternoon light faded early. Somewhere in the house, the telephone rang again. Sam went to answer it. She stayed at the table, listening to his voice in the other room, to the familiar sounds that had not vanished.

The baby turned beneath her hands.

“Not today,” she said again, and this time it was not a request.
