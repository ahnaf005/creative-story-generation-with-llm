### The Space Between

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara Weiss buttoned her coat over the great roundness of her belly and told her husband she could manage the walk.

“I’ve been pregnant for eight months,” she said. “I haven’t turned to glass.”

Daniel smiled, though he still held out his arm. They went together through the cold streets to the synagogue in Southfield, where the snow had been pushed into gray banks along the curb. The sky was bright and mercilessly clear. Barbara could feel the baby shifting beneath her ribs, an insistent little turn, as if it too wanted to see where they were going.

Inside, the sanctuary was warm. Coats whispered as people shrugged them off; voices rose and fell in greetings. Barbara knew many of the faces, if not all the names. She had begun attending services after she and Daniel married, and she liked the way the congregation made room for her without asking her to become someone else. There was always a chair near the aisle. Someone always asked whether she was keeping well.

That morning, Rabbi Adler was speaking.

He was a renowned man, though Barbara had never found fame to be the most interesting thing about him. What she remembered was his way of pausing before a difficult sentence, as if he were setting it carefully on a table. He spoke without rushing, and people listened without fidgeting. Even the children, who usually swung their feet beneath the pews, seemed to settle when he began.

Barbara had one hand resting on her belly and the other folded around Daniel’s. The baby pressed up beneath her palm. She whispered, “There you are.”

Daniel leaned toward her. “Already impatient.”

“Must run in the family.”

A soft ripple of laughter passed between them. Then Rabbi Adler raised his eyes from the page, and the room grew quiet.

Barbara never remembered what he was saying when the doors opened. Later, people would tell her they had heard the hinges, the echo of shoes on the floor, the first startled turn of heads. But memory, she learned, did not keep events in orderly rows. It kept what it could. A shaft of winter light on the polished wood. Daniel’s fingers closing around hers. The baby suddenly still.

A young man stood near the entrance.

For a moment, the congregation seemed to expect him to take a seat. His coat was dark. His face looked pale in the bright room, or perhaps Barbara only remembered it that way. Then he raised a gun.

The first shot split the sanctuary open.

Barbara did not think. She rose so quickly that pain flashed through her back. Around her, people shouted and surged toward the aisles. A second shot rang out. Someone fell against the pew in front of her; someone else grabbed her sleeve. Daniel was saying her name, but the sound seemed to come from a long way off.

At the front of the room, Rabbi Adler crumpled.

Barbara saw him go down, and the sight fixed her to the spot. The sanctuary had become a storm of bodies—people pushing toward the doors, stumbling over coats, reaching for one another. She saw the young man turn the gun on himself. A final sharp sound. Then a silence more terrible than the noise.

Daniel pulled her toward the aisle.

“No,” she said.

She was looking at the young man. He had fallen, and the space around him was suddenly empty. Barbara couldn’t see his face clearly. She couldn’t have told anyone what she wanted to do. She only knew that a person was lying alone on the floor, and that she was still standing.

“Barbara!” Daniel shouted.

The crowd swept across them. A shoulder struck her; hands pressed at her back and arms. She tried to move toward the front, but a woman clutched her coat and cried, “Get out, get out!” Daniel wrapped himself around her, shielding her belly as they were carried sideways, then backward, toward the doors.

“I’m all right,” Barbara said, though she wasn’t certain.

No one heard her.

Outside, people spilled onto the steps into the freezing air. Some ran. Some stood in the snow, looking back at the open doors as if they might close by themselves. Daniel kept one hand at Barbara’s elbow and the other over her stomach. She was shaking so hard that her teeth clicked.

“Are you hurt?”

“I don’t know.”

He looked at her face, her hands, her coat. She tried to answer his worried expression with a smile, but the smile would not come.

A woman wrapped a scarf around Barbara’s shoulders. Someone called for a doctor. Someone else said the police were coming. The words drifted around her, pieces of a language she knew but could no longer understand.

She looked at the people gathered on the steps. She searched for the young man, though she knew he was still inside. She had wanted to reach him. The thought came to her with a strange, unreasonable certainty: she had wanted to ask him something, or tell him something, or put a hand on his arm. She did not know which. The crowd had taken that choice from her.

In the car, Daniel drove her home with both hands tight on the wheel. The heater rattled. Barbara kept one hand on her stomach. The baby began to move again, a small, steady pressure beneath her palm.

“Maybe that’s good,” Daniel said.

“Maybe.”

At home, she sat on the edge of the bed while he called their doctor. She answered questions and let him listen for the baby’s heartbeat. The doctor told them to rest, to come in if anything changed. Daniel thanked him. Barbara looked at the room around her—the folded clothes waiting for the baby, the lamp they had chosen together, the book lying open on the nightstand.

It seemed indecent that everything should still be where it had been.

That evening, the phone rang and kept ringing. Friends asked if they were safe. Someone brought soup. A neighbor came by and stayed in the kitchen, saying little. Barbara’s mother arrived before sunset and sat beside her, holding her hand.

“You’re here,” her mother said. “You’re safe.”

Barbara nodded. But she did not feel safe. She felt as though the day had taken a door off its hinges and left her to watch the wind move through the house.

In the weeks that followed, people spoke about the shooting in lowered voices. They spoke about Rabbi Adler’s kindness, his learning, the lives he had touched. They spoke about the young man too, but not for long. No one seemed to know what could be said. Some insisted there must have been a reason. Others said there could be no reason. Barbara heard the words, but neither explanation brought her closer to the sanctuary she remembered.

She returned once, before the baby came. The building was quiet. Sunlight fell across the floor near the entrance, and the chairs had been set in neat rows. Someone had placed flowers near the front. Barbara stood in the aisle and felt the familiar pressure of the child inside her.

For a while, she could not move.

Then she walked to the front. She did not kneel. She did not speak. She simply stood where she could see the place Rabbi Adler had stood, and the place where she had seen him fall.

The space between them was only a few yards. In her memory, it had become immeasurable.

The baby was born in March, during a night of wet snow. Barbara labored for hours with Daniel at her side, his hand in hers, just as it had been in the synagogue. When the child finally cried, the sound was thin and fierce and alive.

They named her Miriam.

Months later, Barbara brought Miriam to the synagogue. She carried her through the doors in a warm blanket, careful of the threshold. Daniel walked beside her. The sanctuary had filled again, though some faces were missing and some new ones had appeared. People came to greet the baby. They spoke gently, as if the room itself were sleeping.

Barbara took her place near the aisle.

Rabbi Adler’s successor began the service. His voice was not the same. It could not be. But when he paused over a difficult sentence, Barbara felt the old quiet settle around her—the quiet that made space for listening, and for grief, and for all the things no one could explain.

Miriam stirred beneath the blanket.

Barbara lowered her face and touched the child’s cheek. She could not undo that morning. She could not go back through the crowd, or reach the fallen, or make the world pause before the first shot. But she could hold the warm, living weight in her arms. She could stay. And when the people around her rose together, she rose with them.
