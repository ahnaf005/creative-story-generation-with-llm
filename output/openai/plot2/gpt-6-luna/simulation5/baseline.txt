### The Space Between

On Saturday, February 12, 1966, Barbara Weiss woke before the alarm.

She had been awake for most of the night anyway, shifting from one side to the other, trying to find a position that didn’t make her back ache or the baby press insistently beneath her ribs. By morning, the child had settled low, and every step felt like a negotiation. Her husband, David, offered to stay home with her.

“I’m pregnant, not made of glass,” she said, though she accepted his arm on the stairs.

The February air was sharp enough to sting. In Southfield, outside Detroit, the snowbanks along the road had gone gray at their edges, and the sky looked as if it had been rubbed clean of color. David drove slowly. Barbara watched the neighborhoods slip by: brick houses, bare trees, the occasional plume of smoke rising from a chimney. She had been coming to the synagogue with her family since childhood, and the building’s low stone walls and tall windows felt as familiar as a relative’s face.

Inside, warmth gathered around her. Coats rustled. People greeted one another in the aisle, speaking in the hushed, cheerful way of those who were glad to see each other but had come for something quieter than conversation. Barbara took her usual seat near the middle. David sat beside her, close enough that their sleeves touched.

The baby moved once, a firm little thump.

“Already eager for the service,” David whispered.

“Or protesting the seating,” Barbara whispered back.

At the front, Rabbi Adler stood with his prayer book open. He was a renowned man, though Barbara had never thought of him as famous. To her, he was simply the rabbi who knew when her father was ill, who had spoken gently at her mother’s funeral, who remembered people’s names and asked after their children. His voice was warm and steady, carrying through the sanctuary without seeming to press on anyone.

Barbara watched him and felt the child settle. For a few minutes, she forgot the ache in her hips and the nervousness she had felt since dawn. The service continued; the congregation rose, sat, and answered together. Familiar words passed through the room, shaped by many voices into one.

Then a young man appeared near the front.

At first, Barbara thought he was late. He stood awkwardly, as if uncertain where to go, his face pale beneath the dark hair falling over his forehead. The people nearest him turned to look. Rabbi Adler lowered his prayer book.

The young man lifted a gun.

The sound broke the sanctuary open.

There was no time for Barbara to understand what she had seen. She heard David cry out. Somewhere a woman screamed. The congregation surged, one body with hundreds of frightened minds. People ducked, stumbled over the pews, pulled at one another’s sleeves. Rabbi Adler fell from sight behind the lectern, and the young man turned the gun on himself.

Barbara was on her feet before she knew she had moved.

For one clear instant, she saw the young man standing alone in the space where the crowd had been. He seemed suddenly very small, a boy in a dark coat, his arm hanging at his side. She thought of the face she had just seen—more lost than angry—and felt an impossible certainty that someone ought to reach him. Someone ought to put a hand on his shoulder, tell him he was not alone, that there was still time for something different.

She stepped toward him.

Then the crowd struck her.

A shoulder hit her from behind. David’s hand closed around her arm, but someone pulled him away. Barbara was carried sideways against the pews, her feet skidding on the floor. A man shoved past with his head down. Another caught her coat and dragged her backward, shouting for everyone to get out. She reached toward the front, but her hand met only air, then a woman’s sleeve, then the hard edge of a seat.

“David!” she called.

The sound vanished beneath the rush of bodies and voices. She tried to turn, to push against the current, but the press of people forced her toward the aisle. A woman clung to her. An old man stumbled, and Barbara caught him with one arm. Her belly tightened as she was pushed onward.

“Careful,” she gasped, though she could not tell whom she was warning.

They spilled through the doors into the cold. Outside, the crowd broke apart in panicked fragments. People ran toward the parking lot, then stopped, looking back. Some wept openly. Others stood rigid and silent, as if they were still waiting for the next sound.

Barbara found herself on the steps with one hand on her stomach and the other gripping the railing. The cold air filled her lungs too quickly. She drew a breath, then another. The baby shifted beneath her palm.

David appeared in the doorway.

He reached her in three strides and held her shoulders. “Are you hurt?”

“No.” She shook her head, then looked past him at the open doors. “Rabbi Adler—”

David pulled her against him. His coat smelled of wool and the faint soap he used each morning. Around them, people were calling names. Someone asked for a doctor. A man spoke into a telephone, his voice thin and urgent. Through the doorway, Barbara could see only the backs of those who remained inside.

She wanted to go back. The impulse was so strong that she leaned away from David before she understood what she was doing.

“Barbara, no.”

“I can help.”

“There are people inside helping.”

She looked down at her hands. One was trembling. The other still rested protectively over the child. She had thought she could reach the young man, as if kindness were a path that stayed open simply because someone wanted to walk it. Now all she could remember was the crowd closing around her, the brief glimpse of his face, and the silence after the second shot.

At the hospital, a nurse checked her over and told her the baby was moving well. David sat beside the examination bed, pale and quiet. He kept his hand wrapped around hers. Barbara thanked the nurse, though the words came out strangely small.

Later, at home, they sat without turning on the television. The telephone rang several times. David answered, listened, and said little. Friends and relatives wanted to know if they were safe. Barbara told them yes. She did not know what else to say.

That evening, she stood by the living-room window. Snow began to fall, softening the dark street and covering the old gray ridges along the curb. David came up behind her and rested his chin against her hair.

“I keep thinking I could have reached him,” she said.

David did not answer immediately. “You were trying to reach everyone,” he said at last.

She turned her hand over. He was holding it so tightly that the shape of his fingers remained when he let go. “I only reached the crowd.”

“You reached the baby,” he said. “And you reached me.”

It wasn’t enough, but it was true.

In the weeks that followed, Barbara learned the shape of the news from other people’s voices. The young man had died. Rabbi Adler had died too. People said the rabbi’s name in the past tense, then stopped, unsettled by the sound of it. At the synagogue, flowers filled the rooms, and chairs were arranged for mourners who came in silence or spoke until their voices failed.

Barbara went back once before the baby was born. She did not sit in her usual place. The sanctuary looked almost unchanged, which seemed to her a kind of cruelty. The pews were polished. Sunlight lay in pale squares on the floor. Someone had placed a small arrangement of white flowers near the front.

She stood at the rear, one hand on her belly, and listened as the congregation began to pray. Rabbi Adler’s voice was gone, but the words continued. They rose unevenly, carried by people who had come to grieve and by people who had come because they could think of nowhere else to go.

The baby moved. Barbara closed her eyes.

She remembered the young man’s face, though she could not say why that was the part she kept. She did not know his name or what had brought him to that room with a gun. She could not make his final act mean anything sensible. But she could remember that he had been young, and that for one instant she had seen him alone.

Then came the crush of people, the cold outside, David’s arms around her, the child turning safely beneath her heart. None of it repaired what had happened. None of it brought Rabbi Adler back.

Still, Barbara let the prayer carry her. She stood among the living, feeling the small, steady movements within her, and understood that reaching someone was not always the same as saving them. Sometimes it was only the beginning of a promise: that when the frightened gathered, when the room filled with grief, she would not turn away from the person standing alone.
