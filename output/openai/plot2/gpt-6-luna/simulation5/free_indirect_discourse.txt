### The Space Between

On February 12, 1966, Barbara Feld was so pregnant that she had to turn sideways to pass between the pews.

“Careful,” Daniel whispered, though she was already moving carefully. She had been moving carefully for weeks: down stairs, across rooms, through the world. The baby pressed upward beneath her ribs and downward against her bladder, as if trying out every possible objection to being born.

“I’m fine,” she whispered back.

Of course she was fine. She had been fine through the night, through the drive, through the argument about whether the doctor would mind if they went to services. She was fine now, in the synagogue in Southfield, with the winter light pale on the windows and Rabbi Adler’s voice rising above the congregation.

She liked his voice. It had a grain in it, a small roughness that made his words seem handled rather than merely spoken. People came from all over Detroit to hear him. He could make a passage they had heard since childhood feel newly uncovered, like something found in a drawer beneath old letters.

Barbara rested one hand on her belly. The baby shifted.

Not now, she thought. You’ve chosen the wrong morning for a performance.

The thought amused her. The synagogue smelled of wool coats and floor polish. Someone behind her coughed discreetly. The congregation stood, and she stood with them, slower than the others. Daniel’s hand hovered near her elbow, ready to help. She wished he would stop hovering. She wished he would never stop.

Rabbi Adler was speaking about responsibility. Not the grand sort, he said, the sort people praised in speeches. The smaller kind. The duty to notice who was standing beside you. To see a person before you saw an argument, a name, a problem.

Barbara looked down at her shoes. One lace had come loose. She could not bend far enough to fix it. She would have to ask Daniel later.

Then the side door opened.

At first the young man who came through it seemed simply late. He wore a dark coat buttoned high at the throat, and his hair was damp, as if he had walked through the snow that had begun in the morning. He stood just inside the doorway, looking toward the front.

Someone turned to see who had come in. Then someone else did. A ripple of attention moved across the room.

Rabbi Adler stopped speaking.

The young man lifted his arm.

Barbara saw the gun. There was no time to understand it as a gun, no time for the mind to fit this thing into the familiar room. It was only an object in the young man’s hand, small and black and wrong.

A sound cracked through the sanctuary.

For one impossible instant, no one moved. Rabbi Adler’s hand had risen slightly, palm outward, the way it did when he asked a question. Then he fell.

The room broke apart.

People shouted. Benches scraped. Someone was crying; someone else was saying, “Down, get down,” with the steady urgency of a person giving directions in a kitchen. Barbara tried to take a step toward the young man.

Daniel caught her arm. “Barbara!”

“He’s—” she began.

She could not say what he was. A boy. A man. Someone who might still be reached. She had seen his face for one brief moment, pale and astonished, as if he had expected the world to be different once the gun was raised. People were pushing toward the exits now, a surge of coats and shoulders, a whole room moving as one frightened body.

Let me through, she thought. He’s still there.

The crowd swept her sideways. Her hip struck a bench. Daniel’s grip slipped from her arm and caught at the back of her coat. The baby, startled by the jostling, turned hard beneath her hand. Barbara gasped and clung to the wooden rail.

No, not that way. Not out. Someone was stepping on her skirt. A woman’s elbow drove into her ribs, and the pressure of the crowd pressed her breath out. Barbara tried to say she was pregnant, though surely everyone could see it, surely the whole room could see her.

“Please!” she shouted.

The word disappeared in the noise.

She caught sight of the young man again over the heads around her. He was still standing near the doorway. For a fraction of a second his eyes met hers. Or she thought they did. She was close enough to see that he was very young, younger than Daniel, perhaps younger than her brother. Young enough to have once been a boy someone had sent out into the snow wearing a scarf.

Barbara reached toward him.

The crowd pulled her backward.

There was another shot.

The sound seemed smaller than the first, though it should not have been. Then came a silence so sudden and complete that Barbara heard the hiss of the heating pipes beneath the window. Nobody was moving now. Nobody could. The young man had fallen.

She could not see Rabbi Adler.

She could not see Daniel.

She could see the loose lace on her shoe.

It looked absurdly ordinary.

“Barbara!” Daniel’s voice called from somewhere behind her.

“I’m here,” she answered, but her voice sounded thin, as if it belonged to somebody on the other side of the building.

When he found her, he put both hands on her shoulders and searched her face. He was shaking. His mouth worked once before he spoke.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“I’m sure.” The baby kicked. “The baby’s fine.”

He closed his eyes briefly. Barbara wanted to tell him that this was not the time to look relieved, though it was, of course, exactly the time. She wanted to ask after Rabbi Adler. She wanted to ask after the young man. Most of all she wanted to know whether there had been anything she could have done. But that was a question without a safe answer, and the room was full of unsafe things.

They sat her down in a small office while people called for doctors and police. Someone brought a cup of water. Barbara held it without drinking. Her hands would not stop trembling, and the cup clicked against her teeth when she tried.

The baby had gone quiet. That frightened her more than the shouting had.

“Move,” she whispered, pressing her palm to her stomach. “Come on, sweetheart.”

A faint roll. There you are.

Someone said Rabbi Adler had died. Another person said the young man had died too. There were names, then corrections, then no names at all. A woman in a gray hat sat on the floor and rocked gently, though she did not seem to know she was doing it. At the door, a police officer asked questions. Barbara heard the words “what did you see” and “how many shots.” People answered as if reporting the weather, or trying to recall a dream they had not wanted.

She had seen a young man with a gun.

She had seen the rabbi fall.

She had tried to move toward the person who had done it.

How could those things all be true of the same morning? They did not fit together. Rabbi Adler’s voice had been talking about the duty to notice another person. Then the gun. Then the crowd, which had not been cruel, had only been terrified. People had pushed because they wanted to live. She had wanted to go forward because she had thought—what? That a hand on his sleeve would change anything? That she could speak a sentence loud enough to stop a bullet already fired?

Foolish. Brave. The two were not always different.

Daniel sat beside her, holding her hand. His thumb moved over her knuckles, again and again. She let him do it.

“I couldn’t get to him,” she said.

He looked at her. “You couldn’t get anywhere.”

“I saw him.”

“I know.”

“He looked like a boy.”

Daniel’s face tightened. He did not tell her not to think about it. There were some things people said because silence frightened them, and Daniel was usually better than that.

The synagogue filled with strangers and uniforms. Outside, snow dusted the cars in the parking lot and gathered along the curb. The day continued, offensively ordinary. Somewhere in the building, a telephone rang and rang until someone answered it.

Barbara imagined the young man at another time: a child in a kitchen, a teenager waiting for a bus, a person walking alone through a winter evening. She could not know whether any of those pictures were true. They were only the mind’s stubborn effort to give a face a past, to make it more than the last thing it had done.

She would not forgive him. She would not pretend she understood him. But he had been a person before he was a gun in a doorway, and after this morning she could not stop seeing the difference.

A doctor examined her in another room. He listened for the baby and nodded. “Strong heartbeat,” he said.

Strong. Barbara held on to the word.

Later, when they were finally allowed to leave, Daniel helped her into her coat. She stood at the synagogue entrance and looked back at the sanctuary, now dim and crowded with police officers. The place seemed smaller than it had that morning. The benches were still there. The windows. The lectern where Rabbi Adler had stood.

Her loose lace dragged against the floor.

Daniel bent to tie it for her. She watched the back of his head and thought how odd it was that a life could be made of things like this: a knot tied, a hand held, the baby turning under her palm. Not grand responsibility. Not speeches. The small kind. The kind that remained after the room had emptied.

Outside, the snow was beginning to stick.

Barbara put one hand on Daniel’s shoulder and the other on her belly. They went slowly to the car. There was nowhere else to go but forward, and this time the crowd was gone.
