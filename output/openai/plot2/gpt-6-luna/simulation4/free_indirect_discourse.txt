### The Space Between

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara had to stop twice between the parking lot and the synagogue doors.

The first time, she blamed her shoes. The second, the baby. There was no arguing with either one. The child had spent the ride from home pressing its heels—or elbows, or whatever it was—against the underside of her ribs, and now seemed determined to make the walk inside a test of endurance. Barbara paused beneath the gray winter sky, one hand on the swell of her belly.

A few more steps. That was all.

She was eight months pregnant, which was long enough to have learned that strangers felt entitled to tell her what she should and should not do. Don’t stand too long. Don’t carry anything. Don’t climb stairs. Don’t worry. As if worry were a coat she could hang up beside the door.

Inside, the synagogue was warm, and the familiar sounds steadied her: the rustle of coats, quiet conversation, a child being hushed. Barbara knew these sounds. She had heard them since she was little, and still they made the world seem orderly. There were places for people to sit, words to be said, melodies to follow. Even the winter light, dim through the high windows, looked as though it belonged.

Rabbi Adler was speaking. His voice traveled easily through the room, unhurried and sure. Barbara had heard him many times, but never casually. He had a way of making a familiar teaching sound newly discovered, as if everyone in the room had been waiting for him to find the right words. She liked to watch people listen to him. Their faces softened. Even the restless children seemed to understand that something important was happening.

Barbara lowered herself onto a bench. The baby shifted, and she pressed a palm to her stomach.

Quiet now, she thought. Let me hear him.

The rabbi spoke about mercy. Barbara tried to follow, though her attention wandered. Her back ached. The collar of her dress felt tight. Somewhere behind her, a man coughed, and a woman whispered to her husband. Ordinary things. Good things. She could almost hear her mother telling her that after the baby came, she would miss the peaceful parts of a Saturday morning. She would miss sitting still.

Then the young man stood.

At first, Barbara thought he had only come in late. She noticed his movement before his face, the way heads turned and then turned back. He was young—how young, she could not tell. He looked pale beneath the lights, and held himself stiffly, as if every part of him had been ordered to stay where it was.

The rabbi’s voice faltered.

A hand came up. Something dark and small was in it.

For one suspended instant, the whole room seemed to hold its breath. Barbara had no name for what she was seeing. The young man’s arm, the stillness around him, the sudden silence. It could not be what it looked like. People did not bring guns into synagogues. Not here. Not while Rabbi Adler was speaking.

Then came the shot.

The sound broke the room open. Barbara flinched so hard the baby kicked. People shouted. Benches scraped against the floor. The rabbi fell from sight, and all the careful order of the morning vanished at once.

Get down.

Someone pulled at Barbara’s arm. Someone else was crying. The crowd surged toward the exits, and the force of it caught her before she could decide what to do. Her body, already cumbersome, became something she had to drag through the crush. She was pressed between shoulders, stumbled against a bench, caught herself on a stranger’s sleeve.

The young man was still standing.

The gun was still in his hand.

Barbara saw him looking down toward the place where the rabbi had fallen. She saw the emptiness in his face—or thought she did. She could not have said what it meant. Shock, perhaps. Terror. A dreadful resolve. There was no time to understand it. There was only the movement of the crowd and the knowledge that he was still there, still holding the weapon, still young.

Don’t leave him alone.

The thought arrived with the blunt certainty of a command. Barbara tried to turn back. She reached one hand toward the young man, though there was no way she could cross the space between them. She wanted to call out, but the words caught behind the noise. If he could hear her, perhaps he would stop. If he could see her—her hand, her belly, her face—perhaps he might remember that she was a person, and so was he. People could still be reached. They had to be.

“Wait,” she tried to say.

The crowd swept her sideways.

She clutched at the nearest shoulder, but the woman attached to it was moving too, stumbling, terrified. Barbara’s feet slid on the floor. Her hip struck the end of a bench. A sharp pain flashed through her lower back, and she gasped. For a moment she could see nothing but coats and faces, eyes wide, mouths open. She was being carried away from the young man by people who were not looking at him, not looking at anything except the open doorway.

She had never felt so large and so powerless.

The second shot sounded.

The crowd moved harder. Barbara was thrust toward the entrance, pushed through it into the cold air. Her heel caught on the threshold. She went down onto one knee, hands flying to her stomach.

“Barbara!”

Someone was there, helping her up. She couldn’t tell who. The baby kicked once, a bright, startling knock against her palm. Alive. She was alive. The child was alive. The words should have been a comfort. They were, and they weren’t.

People spilled onto the steps and the pavement. Some cried. Some stared back at the doors as if the building had become a thing that might strike again. A man shouted for someone to call for help. Another was already running toward the parking lot. The winter air stung Barbara’s face, but she barely felt it.

“Rabbi Adler,” she said, though it came out as a question.

No one answered. Or perhaps someone did, and she could not hear. She wanted to go back inside. She wanted to go home. She wanted to find the young man and ask him what had happened to make the world narrow down to a gun in his hand. She wanted to know if he had meant to die. She wanted, impossibly, to undo the moment before the first shot.

Instead, she stood with one hand on the railing and the other over her stomach.

A woman in a blue hat took her arm. “You’re hurt?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

Barbara nodded. She wasn’t sure of anything. She could feel the baby moving, and that was something. It had to be something.

The minutes afterward came apart into fragments. Sirens. A voice asking her name. Someone offering a coat. The bright flash of a camera across the street. Barbara answered questions without remembering what she had said. She watched the synagogue doors, waiting for someone to emerge and tell them they had misunderstood. The rabbi had been hurt, not killed. The young man had dropped the gun. Someone had stopped him. They were all going back inside in a moment. The service would continue, perhaps, a little shaken, but whole.

But nobody came out to say that.

By the time her husband reached her, she had begun to tremble. He put both arms around her, careful of her belly, and she pressed her cheek against his coat. He asked what she had seen. She tried to explain, but every sentence seemed to end too soon.

“I tried to get to him,” she said.

Her husband held her tighter. “I know.”

How could he know? She had not reached him. She had barely moved toward him. Her effort had been no more than a hand lifted in a room of rushing bodies. Still, she had tried. It was not much. It was not nothing.

In the days that followed, people asked Barbara what happened. They wanted the sequence: the young man stood, the gun came out, the rabbi fell, the room emptied. She gave them what she could. She did not tell them that the thing she remembered most clearly was not the gun or the shouting, but the space between herself and the young man. A few yards, perhaps. Less. A distance that had seemed crossable until the crowd filled it.

The baby was born weeks later, healthy and loud. Barbara held the child against her chest and listened to that fierce, indignant cry. She thought of the synagogue, of Rabbi Adler’s voice, of the young man whose face she could still see when she closed her eyes. The child’s tiny fingers curled around hers.

There were things she could not protect anyone from. There were moments when a room changed too quickly for a person to act. She knew that now. She also knew that, in the instant before the crowd carried her away, she had turned toward someone in danger, toward someone who had become dangerous, and tried to make herself seen.

The baby’s hand tightened around her finger.

Barbara stayed very still, letting the small grip hold her there. Outside the window, winter light rested on the glass. The world beyond it remained what it had been: cold, ordinary, and waiting.
