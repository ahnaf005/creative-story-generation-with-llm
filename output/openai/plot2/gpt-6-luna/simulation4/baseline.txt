### The Space Between

On February 12, 1966, Barbara Weiss woke before dawn to the sound of snow sliding off the roof.

She lay still, one hand spread over the high, taut curve of her belly. The baby shifted beneath her palm, a small rolling movement, as if turning over in sleep. Outside, Southfield was buried under a clean white hush. The city beyond it—Detroit, with its factories and busy avenues—seemed very far away.

“Still awake?” David murmured beside her.

“I don’t think the baby sleeps.”

“Then the baby has something in common with your father.”

She smiled. David reached across the bed and rested his hand over hers. They listened to the snow settle.

At eight months pregnant, Barbara had learned to take the world slowly: one step at a time, one stair at a time, one breath at a time. She had also learned that strangers believed they could tell her what to do. Sit down, dear. Don’t lift that. Let me get the door. She accepted their kindness, mostly, and ignored their advice when necessary.

That morning she insisted on attending services.

“You’ll be uncomfortable,” David said, fastening his coat.

“I’m uncomfortable everywhere. At least at the synagogue there are people who’ll pretend not to notice.”

The congregation had been gathering for weeks to hear Rabbi Adler speak about courage. He was famous, though Barbara disliked that word for him. Famous suggested a man made of headlines and introductions. Rabbi Adler was a man who remembered the names of children, who stopped in the corridor to ask after the sick and the lonely, and who could make even the most difficult questions seem worth asking.

Barbara had first heard him speak as a girl, when her mother brought her to the synagogue after her father died. She remembered the rabbi’s voice filling the room, not loudly but steadily, like a hand resting on a shoulder.

“You don’t have to understand grief to sit beside it,” he had told her then.

She had never forgotten.

The synagogue stood among low buildings and snow-laden trees, its broad windows dim in the winter morning. Inside, warmth and wool and the faint smell of old wood gathered around them. People greeted Barbara with concern and affection. Someone took her coat. Someone else asked whether the baby had chosen a name.

“Not yet,” she said. “We’re waiting to meet.”

“That’s wise,” said Mrs. Kaplan. “Babies are stubborn.”

They found their seats near the middle. Barbara eased herself down, one hand at her back. David sat beside her. Ahead, Rabbi Adler spoke quietly with the cantor, his white hair bright beneath the sanctuary lights.

The service began.

For a while, the morning was ordinary. The congregation rose and sat, voices joining and falling away. Barbara followed the familiar words as best she could. Her back ached. The baby pressed a heel—or perhaps an elbow—beneath her ribs. She shifted, trying not to disturb David.

Then the doors at the rear opened.

A young man stepped inside.

He was thin and pale, wearing a dark coat that looked too large for him. Barbara noticed him only because he stood still while everyone else was moving. He did not remove his coat or look for a seat. His eyes travelled over the room, past the rows of people, toward the front.

For one brief moment his gaze met Barbara’s.

She thought he looked frightened.

Then he drew a gun.

The sound was so sharp that Barbara did not at first understand it as a sound at all. The room seemed to crack open. People shouted. Someone fell against the pew behind her. Rabbi Adler dropped from sight.

The congregation surged.

David seized Barbara’s arm. “Come on!”

But she was looking toward the young man. He stood near the back, the gun still in his hand, his face stunned, as though he had watched himself do something impossible.

Barbara pushed herself upright.

“Wait,” she said.

David pulled her toward the aisle. Bodies pressed around them. People who had been singing together a moment before were suddenly strangers with only one thought: get out. A woman sobbed. A man called for his children. The doors seemed impossibly far away.

Barbara saw the young man’s hand tremble.

She did not know his name. She did not know why he had come. She knew only that the fear in his face had become unbearable to look at, and that if everyone left him alone with what he had done, something worse might happen.

“Let me through,” she said.

David turned to her, white with alarm. “Barbara, no.”

“I can reach him.”

The crowd swept sideways. She tried to move against it, one arm protecting her belly, but each step was taken from her. A shoulder struck her. Someone’s coat caught around her wrist. She heard David calling her name, but the sound was lost in the panic.

The young man took a step toward the front.

Barbara reached for the nearest pew, pulling herself free. “Please!” she shouted. “Put it down!”

For an instant, the words seemed to reach him. His face lifted. His eyes found hers again. She thought he might answer. She thought he might lower the gun.

Then the crowd shoved her backward.

She fell hard against a wooden bench. Pain flared across her hip. A dozen hands caught her, or perhaps pushed her; she could not tell. David forced his way back to her, gripping her shoulders.

“Are you hurt?”

“I’m all right,” she gasped. “The baby—”

The child moved beneath her hand, a fluttering insistence. Still there.

Another shot rang out.

The crowd’s cries rose into one terrible sound. David bent over Barbara, shielding her with his body. She shut her eyes, not because she could not bear to see, but because she could not bear to watch the young man become, in a single instant, only the thing he had done.

Then there was no more movement near the front.

People pressed through the doors into the snow. David helped Barbara up. She leaned on him, dizzy and breathless, and together they were carried outside with the others. The cold struck her face. She heard sirens in the distance, growing louder.

Someone sat her on the low stone wall beside the entrance. David crouched in front of her, his hands shaking as he examined her face and arms.

“Look at me,” he said. “Are you hurt?”

“My hip. I don’t know.”

“You’re bleeding?”

“No.”

“Can you feel the baby?”

She placed his hand against her belly. The child shifted.

David bowed his head. For a moment he could not speak. Barbara wrapped her fingers around his wrist.

“He looked so young,” she whispered.

David looked back at the synagogue doors. People stood in clusters in the snow, crying, praying, staring at nothing. An usher emerged and told them that Rabbi Adler was dead. The young man, too, had died.

A woman nearby began to wail.

Barbara lowered her head. The cold worked through her coat, but she could still feel the warm, steady pressure of the baby beneath her hand. She thought of Rabbi Adler’s voice, measured and gentle, telling her years ago that grief did not need to be understood before someone sat beside it.

She wanted to go back inside. She wanted to kneel beside the rabbi, or beside the young man, or simply stand in the room where the morning had split in two. But the ambulance attendants were already guiding people away. David insisted she be checked. The crowd had taken her where it wanted her, and it had taken the rest of the congregation, too.

At the hospital, doctors listened to the baby’s heartbeat. The rhythm filled the examination room: quick, strong, untroubled.

Barbara held David’s hand and listened.

She had not reached the young man. She could not change what he had done, or what had been lost. But she remembered the pause before the final shot: his frightened face, the thin space between them, the words she had tried to send across it.

Years later, when her daughter asked what courage looked like, Barbara would think first of Rabbi Adler, who had taught her that grief could be met with presence. She would think of the people who had run, because fear was not a failure. And she would think of a young man in a dark coat, standing at the edge of a crowd, and of how desperately she had wanted him to put the gun down.

She would tell her daughter that sometimes courage was only a voice raised once in a room full of panic.

Sometimes it was a hand reaching out.

And sometimes, even when the world swept you away, you kept reaching in your heart.
