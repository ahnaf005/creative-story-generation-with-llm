On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara buttoned her coat over the hill of her belly and found that the last button would not meet its hole. It hovered there stubbornly, a little brown eye refusing to blink. She laughed, because what else was there to do? If she did not laugh at her body these days—at the way it grew strange and royal and uncooperative—she would spend all morning in tears.

“Fine,” she told the button. “Have it your way.”

Outside, Southfield lay under a thin crust of winter. The streets were salted white at the edges, the trees bare and black against the pale sky. Detroit was only a short drive away, but out here everything had begun to spread itself wide—lawns, driveways, low brick houses with picture windows. It still seemed new to Barbara, this suburban synagogue with its clean lines and broad doors, this promise of space. She had grown up with narrower hallways, narrower streets, neighbors who knew when your mother burned the brisket and when your father stayed home sick from work. Here people waved from cars.

The baby pressed one heel—or elbow, or fist; Barbara could never tell—against her ribs.

Yes, yes, we’re going, she thought. Don’t rush me.

She drove slowly, both hands tight on the wheel. Her husband had wanted to come with her, but he had a fever and a cough that rattled in his chest. “Stay home,” she’d said, standing in the bedroom doorway with her gloves in one hand. “You’ll infect half the congregation.”

“And you won’t overdo it?” he asked.

Overdo it. As if attending Shabbat services were a mountain expedition. As if she had not been carrying this child for what felt like several lifetimes already.

“I will sit,” she said. “I will pray. I will eat cake if there is cake. Then I will come home.”

He smiled weakly. “Promise?”

“I promise cake.”

Now, as she pulled into the synagogue parking lot, she saw clusters of people moving toward the entrance, dark coats, hats, scarves, polished shoes stepping carefully over the crusted snow. The building stood calm and pale against the morning. Inside there would be warmth, the smell of wool drying, perfume, old prayer books, children’s hair tonic, and the faint, comforting scent of coffee from the social hall.

Barbara parked farther away than she would have liked. Of course. A woman in her ninth month should be issued a reserved space by divine decree. She climbed out carefully and waited a moment beside the car until a tightening passed across her belly, not pain exactly, more like a hand gathering cloth. Braxton Hicks, the doctor had said. Practice. Her body rehearsing for its great performance.

Practice, practice. Everyone wanted practice from her. Breathing practice. Nursing practice. Practice folding diapers. Practice being calm.

Inside, the lobby was full of voices. Someone touched her sleeve; someone else exclaimed over the size of her. Was she still walking? Was she sure there weren’t two in there? When was she due? Any day now? How wonderful. How uncomfortable. How exciting.

Barbara smiled and answered as required. Late February. No, not twins. Yes, kicking all the time. No, they hadn’t chosen a name. Yes, she would call when there was news.

What a funny thing, to be so public and so private at once. Her body invited conversation from near strangers, but the child inside remained secret, known only in pressures and pulses. A boy, perhaps. A girl. A swimmer. A scholar. A comedian, with all this kicking. Someone who would one day roll eyes at her, refuse soup, ask for the car keys.

She entered the sanctuary and paused at the back.

It always quieted her, that room. The high ceiling seemed to collect the breath of everyone beneath it and hold it gently overhead. Light fell through the windows in gray winter panels. On the bimah, the ark waited. The eternal light glowed above it, steady as a promise.

Rabbi Adler was already there, moving among the men near the front with his familiar gravity. Renowned, people said. Brilliant. Important. He had the kind of face that made one sit up straighter—not severe, exactly, but awake. He noticed people. That was the thing Barbara had always felt. When he looked at you, he did not seem to be glancing over your shoulder for someone more significant. He looked.

She took a seat near the aisle, halfway back, because she needed room to maneuver. Lowering herself required strategy now. One hand on the pew. Turn slightly. Do not drop. The baby shifted as if disapproving of the arrangement.

A young man sat several rows ahead and across the aisle. Barbara noticed him because he turned once, sharply, as though someone had called his name, though no one had. His face was pale, his hair dark and neat, his suit too large in the shoulders. A college boy, perhaps. Or older. It was hard to tell with young men now; some wore beards and looked like prophets, others shaved and looked twelve. He held himself rigidly, hands together.

Nervous, she thought. Maybe he has to speak.

The service began.

The familiar prayers rose and settled. Barbara followed where she could, losing her place now and then when the baby shoved hard beneath her ribs. She placed one gloved hand over her belly. Enough. But there was no sternness in it. She loved these interruptions, even when they stole her breath. Proof of life. Proof that after all the waiting, all the nausea, all the sleepless nights and swollen ankles, something was coming.

Rabbi Adler’s voice carried through the sanctuary, warm and resonant. Barbara let it wash over her. She had heard people speak of him with pride, as if his reputation belonged to them collectively, like a family heirloom. Our rabbi. Our Rabbi Adler. Invited here, quoted there, respected everywhere. Yet in the sanctuary he was not distant. He belonged to the room.

A rustle moved through the congregation. Prayer books shifted. A cough. A child whispered and was hushed. Barbara’s back ached. She changed position, which helped for three seconds and then did not help. Well, this was motherhood, wasn’t it? Discomfort without applause.

The young man ahead had leaned forward. His shoulders had risen, sharp under the loose fabric.

Poor boy, she thought again. Whatever it is, let him get through it.

Then he stood.

At first nothing in the room understood. Bodies register before minds do; Barbara would remember that afterward. A change in the air. The wrongness of a movement. Heads turned, but slowly, almost politely. The young man stepped into the aisle and moved toward the front. He had something in his hand.

A book? A note?

No.

The sound cracked the sanctuary open.

For one stunned second Barbara did not know what had happened. A noise, impossibly loud, striking the walls and returning. People froze in the pews, faces lifted. Then another sound—screams, but not yet many, not yet the full human animal panic, only a few torn cries as comprehension leapt from person to person.

Rabbi Adler staggered.

No. That could not be the image. Her mind rejected it even as her eyes held it: the rabbi, who had been upright, who had been speaking, folding under some invisible blow. Men near him lunged forward. Someone shouted. The young man still stood there, arm extended, the gun dark in his hand.

Gun. In shul. On Shabbat. This sentence would not form properly. The words refused to stand beside one another.

Barbara’s hand clamped over her belly. The baby had gone still.

Move, someone cried. Get down. Run.

But Barbara was already on her feet, though she had no memory of deciding. The pew tilted beside her. Her prayer book fell to the floor with a slap. The young man’s face was turned slightly toward the congregation, and she saw not fury, not triumph, but terror. A terrible, naked terror, as if he too had heard the shot and could not understand who had fired it.

He is just a boy, she thought.

This was absurd. He had a gun. He had shot Rabbi Adler. Men were shouting. Women were screaming. The sanctuary was coming apart in waves of coats and arms and pale hands clutching hats, purses, children.

But he was a boy.

Barbara stepped into the aisle.

“Don’t,” she said, or thought she said. Her voice could not possibly have carried. “Please don’t.”

Someone collided with her shoulder and spun away. Another person seized her sleeve, then lost it. The crowd was rising now in earnest, bodies forcing toward the exits. A woman sobbed, “My son, my son,” though whether she had lost him or was calling to him Barbara did not know. A man climbed over a pew. Prayer books fell like startled birds.

Barbara moved forward one step. Then another. The aisle seemed at once very short and endlessly long. The rabbi lay ahead surrounded by dark suits. The young man had turned the gun inward, toward himself.

No. No, no, no.

It came over her with a clarity so bright it seemed almost calm: if someone reached him, if someone touched his arm, if someone said his name—did she know his name? no—then perhaps the next second could be changed. The terrible machinery had not yet finished. There was still one gear to stop.

She lifted both hands, palms out, as if approaching a frightened animal.

“Wait,” she said. “Wait.”

The baby shifted suddenly, a rolling pressure low in her abdomen, and the sensation nearly buckled her. Not now, she thought wildly. Not now, sweetheart. Hold on.

She saw the young man’s eyes.

For the rest of her life she would mistrust anyone who spoke too easily of evil. Evil had not looked back at her that morning. Despair had. Confusion. A loneliness so complete it had become a room with no doors.

He raised the gun.

Barbara tried to hurry.

She had forgotten she could not hurry. Her body was enormous, clumsy, bound in wool and winter weight. She pushed forward, but a surge of people swept from the pews and struck her from the side. An elbow caught her ribs. Someone screamed directly into her ear. She lost sight of the young man behind a wall of shoulders.

“No!” she cried.

Hands grabbed at her—not to help, not knowingly, but because everyone was grabbing anything. She was turned around, shoved backward. The current took her. She fought it, furious now, more furious than frightened. Let me through. Let me go. You don’t understand. He will do it. He will do it if nobody—

The second shot came.

The crowd convulsed.

Barbara’s knees weakened, and for a moment she thought she was falling. Someone’s arm hooked around her waist from behind, hard under her belly, and dragged her upright. A man’s voice near her shouted, “She’s pregnant! Careful!” As if that word—pregnant—had any power in the chaos, as if it were a badge that might part the sea.

But it did not part. It carried her.

She was swept down the aisle toward the doors, backward glances chopped into fragments: the bimah, men bending, a woman with both hands over her mouth, a hat crushed underfoot, the eternal light burning steadily above the ark as though nothing on earth could teach it to flicker.

In the lobby, cold air knifed in from the open doors. People poured outside without coats fastened, without hats, without one another. Some stood stunned on the steps. Some ran to cars. Some turned back and were pulled away. The winter morning had not changed. That was the first cruelty of it. Snowbanks, parking lot, pale sky. The world had not stopped to mark what had happened inside.

Barbara found herself beside a pillar, one hand pressed against the stone, gasping. Her other hand held her belly. The baby moved again, a slow, deep turn.

Alive, she thought. You’re alive.

Then shame flooded her. Alive—yes. And Rabbi Adler? And that boy? That boy whose name she still did not know? What good was her aliveness to them?

A woman she recognized but could not name came toward her. Lipstick smeared, hair undone, one glove missing. “Barbara, are you hurt?”

Hurt. The word seemed childish. Hurt was a skinned knee, a burnt finger, a husband’s careless remark. What was the word for seeing a sacred room torn open? What was the word for being too slow?

“I tried,” Barbara said.

The woman leaned closer. “What?”

“I tried to get to him.”

“Oh, honey.” The woman’s face changed—pity, horror, perhaps disbelief. “You couldn’t have.”

But Barbara heard the shape of the comfort and rejected it. People said you couldn’t have when what they meant was let us not look too closely. Let us cover the unbearable with a clean cloth. Yet she had seen the distance. She had seen her own hands raised uselessly. She had felt the crowd decide her direction for her. She had been carried away like driftwood.

Sirens arrived, faint at first, then swelling. Police cars. Ambulances. Men in uniforms moving briskly, asking questions no one could answer in order. Who fired? How many shots? Where is he? Is the rabbi breathing? Did you see? Did you see?

Yes, she had seen. No, not enough. Too much.

Someone brought her a chair from inside, and she sat because everyone insisted. A blanket appeared around her shoulders. She did not remember being cold. Her teeth chattered anyway. The baby kicked hard, indignant now, alive with protest.

All right, all right, Barbara thought. I’m here.

But where was here? The synagogue doors stood open behind her, and from within came movements muffled by distance, urgent footsteps, low commands. People huddled in clusters across the walkway. Some prayed aloud. Some stared at their hands. A boy of perhaps thirteen stood alone near the steps, his tie crooked, face blank. Barbara wanted to call him over, to straighten the tie, to tell him some lie about safety. But she remained in the chair with the blanket slipping from her shoulders.

Her husband arrived much later, or perhaps only minutes later; time had become unreliable. He came running across the parking lot without a hat, coughing, his face gray. When he saw her, something in him collapsed.

“Barbara.”

“I’m all right,” she said quickly. “The baby’s all right.”

He knelt in front of her, hands hovering, afraid to touch too hard. “What happened?”

She looked past him to the doors.

How to tell it? A young man stood. A rabbi fell. A crowd became a river. The eternal light kept burning. She had tried to walk toward a gun because she had mistaken herself for someone who could alter the world by wanting it badly enough.

“I don’t know,” she said.

That night, after statements and phone calls and the exhausted return home, Barbara lay in bed with the lamp on. Her husband slept fitfully beside her, one hand resting over the blanket near her knee, as though afraid she might disappear. Every time she closed her eyes, the sanctuary assembled itself again. The young man’s shoulders. Rabbi Adler’s voice interrupted. The gun turning. Her own body, too slow, too heavy, too alive.

The baby did not sleep. Or perhaps Barbara’s wakefulness stirred the child. A heel pressed outward, held, withdrew.

“You were there,” she whispered.

It frightened her, this thought. Before birth, before first breath, the child had already been present at violence, at prayer, at death. What did babies know in the dark? Did fear pass through blood? Did a mother’s racing heart become the world’s first lesson?

No. She would not allow that to be the first lesson.

She placed both hands on her belly and tried to breathe as they had taught her in class. In through the nose, out through the mouth. Ridiculous breathing, theatrical breathing. But slowly something softened. The child rolled beneath her palms, not frightened now, perhaps only crowded, perhaps dreaming whatever unborn children dreamed.

“You were there,” she said again, quieter. “But you were also here.”

Here, in this room with yellow lamplight. Here, with a father snoring through a fever. Here, under her hands, insisting on February and March and summer, on milk and diapers and lullabies sung badly at three in the morning. Here, demanding a future with the audacity of the unborn.

Barbara thought of Rabbi Adler’s face, the way he looked directly at people. She thought of the young man’s eyes. She had not saved either of them. This would remain true. It would sit inside her like a stone. But another truth moved beneath her hands, restless and warm.

In the morning, people would call. They would ask for details, and she would give some and withhold others. In the weeks to come, the story would harden in newspapers, in official accounts, in whispers after services. It would become an event, a tragedy, a thing people remembered where they were when they heard. Her own memory would stay less orderly: the unfastened coat button, the fallen prayer book, the current of bodies, the unblinking light.

For now, she lay awake and waited for dawn.

The baby kicked once, sharply.

Barbara almost laughed. Even here, even after everything, life made its unreasonable demands.

“Yes,” she whispered into the dim room, to the child, to herself, perhaps to the souls that had already gone where she could not follow. “Yes. I’m listening.”
