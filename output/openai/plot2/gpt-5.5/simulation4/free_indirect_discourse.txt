## The Day the Doors Opened

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara could not button her good wool coat.

She stood in the front hall with one hand braced against the wall and the other worrying the stubborn row of buttons, her breath coming in small, irritated bursts. Eight months gone, maybe more—who could tell anymore? The baby had become a season of its own, a hard round winter beneath her ribs. Her husband, Daniel, waited by the door with the car keys in his gloved hand, trying not to look amused.

“Leave it,” he said. “It’s not that cold.”

Not that cold. Easy for him to say, with his flat stomach and his shoes tied without prayer. Outside, the sky over Southfield had the pale tin color it got in February, and the lawns were crusted with old snow. The trees along the street looked like black writing on a gray page.

Barbara gave up on the middle button and fastened the top one only. Let the whole congregation see her blouse, then. Let them see that she was a house with the front porch still under construction.

In the car, she pressed both palms low on her belly as Daniel drove toward the synagogue. The baby shifted, a slow rolling pressure, as if turning away from the radio news. Barbara had been told that babies heard everything now: voices, music, the creak of the mother’s bones. What would this one know before it ever saw daylight? The hum of tires on icy roads. Daniel’s soft whistling. Her own heart, busy and foolish.

At Congregation Shaarey Zedek, the parking lot was already nearly full. Men in dark coats walked carefully over the slick pavement, their hats held against the wind. Women bent their heads and clutched handbags. Children broke from their parents and were called back in whispers that were not whispers at all.

Inside, warmth struck Barbara’s cheeks. The familiar smell rose around her—wool drying, perfume, old prayer books, polished wood, someone’s cough drop sweet with menthol. She liked that smell. It was not a holy smell, exactly, but it belonged to holy things. It meant people had come out of their houses and brought their whole lives with them: their colds, their arguments, their recipes, their griefs folded into coat pockets.

They found seats toward the middle. Daniel wanted an aisle in case she needed to get up. In case she needed to get up. As though she were an elderly aunt or a kettle close to boiling. Still, she was grateful. Sitting down required planning. Standing required faith.

The sanctuary filled. Barbara watched the movement of shoulders, hats removed, prayer books opened. The ark stood at the front, the eternal light glowing with its small, patient flame. Above and around them, the ceiling gathered every rustle and murmur and held it.

Rabbi Adler was to speak that morning.

Even before he appeared, his presence seemed arranged throughout the room. People straightened a little. Conversations lowered. He had that effect—not sternly, not by demanding reverence, but by making people remember they had brought their better selves with them and should perhaps take them out. Barbara had heard him speak many times. His voice could travel to the back of the sanctuary without strain, warm and exact. He made ancient words sound as if they had been waiting all week for this particular Saturday.

There he was, at last: Rabbi Adler, moving with dignity toward the pulpit. Renowned, beloved, quoted at dinner tables and in newspaper columns, but in that moment simply a man adjusting his papers. Barbara felt the baby stir. Yes, yes, she thought. Listen. This is what a good voice sounds like.

The service moved on with its ordinary majesty. Prayers rose and settled. Pages turned. Someone behind Barbara whispered the wrong page number and someone else corrected him. A boy in the row ahead swung his feet until his mother placed one firm hand on his knee. Light fell through the high windows, thin and wintery, making the dust visible.

Barbara’s back ached. A hard band tightened around her middle and released. Not labor, she told herself. Just one of those practice pains. Even the body rehearsed. Everyone rehearsed for things they could not imagine.

Rabbi Adler began to speak.

She would later try to remember his exact words and fail. That failure would trouble her more than she expected. A person wanted the last words before disaster to be carved cleanly somewhere, a tablet held up against forgetting. But memory did not work that way. It kept the color of the light, the angle of a hand, the itch beneath a glove. It let sentences scatter.

He spoke, she thought, about responsibility. Or perhaps about youth. About the burdens placed on the next generation. About not turning away from suffering because it made one uncomfortable. That sounded like him. He did not flatter people with easy goodness. He invited them toward a harder kind.

Barbara shifted in her seat. The baby pressed upward; her ribs protested. Daniel glanced at her. She gave the smallest shake of her head. I am fine. Everyone must stop asking. She was fine.

Then a young man rose near the front.

At first, no one understood this as an interruption. People rose in synagogues—for honors, for prayers, to soothe children, to excuse themselves. Barbara noticed him because he moved too quickly, or because he did not move like a man aware of being watched. He was thin, dark-haired, with a face that seemed both young and used-up. He stepped forward.

There was a sound—a crack so sharp it did not belong to the room.

For one suspended moment, Barbara thought a book had fallen. A stack of boards, perhaps, dropped in some hidden maintenance corridor. The mind was merciful in the first instant; it offered nonsense before truth.

Then came the second crack, and the room tore open.

Rabbi Adler fell.

No, not fell—not at first. He changed. His body lost the argument it had been making with the air. People gasped, but the gasp was collective, one creature drawing breath. A woman screamed. Someone shouted, “Get down!” Prayer books thudded to the floor. Daniel’s arm came across Barbara’s chest, pressing her back, but she was already halfway standing.

The young man had a gun in his hand.

A gun. In the sanctuary. In the morning light. In front of the ark.

Barbara stared at it with an astonishment so pure it was almost childlike. Such a small object. Such a ridiculous, ugly little authority. The young man’s hand trembled. His face was pale, emptied of whatever had brought him to this place. He did not look triumphant. He looked lost.

The crowd surged.

Bodies rose and collided. Coats brushed her face. Someone knocked into her shoulder; pain flashed bright and hot. Daniel shouted her name, but she could not see him. The aisle filled all at once, people pushing toward the exits, toward anywhere that was not here. The baby tightened inside her, a frightened fist.

Barbara should have crouched. She should have protected herself. She should have done what Daniel said, if he was still saying anything. But the young man—he was turning the gun now, turning it awkwardly toward himself.

No.

The word formed in her without sound.

He was a boy. Not a boy, perhaps, but young enough that some mother still saw him that way. Young enough to have owned a winter hat with a pom-pom once, to have been told to finish his milk, to have stood in a doorway too ashamed to say what sorrow had found him.

No, don’t, she thought, and began to move toward him.

It was not bravery. Later people might call it that if they heard some version of the story, but they would be wrong. Bravery suggested decision, and she had made none. Her body moved because his body was about to vanish from the world by his own hand, and somebody had to reach him before he did. That was all. Somebody had to say, Wait. Even if there was nothing after wait.

She pushed past a man frozen in the aisle. Her belly struck the back of a pew; she cried out but kept going. The sanctuary seemed both enormous and impossibly narrow. She saw the young man’s eyes. Did she? Or did she invent that afterward? Dark eyes, unfocused. Terrified. Human.

“Stop!” she tried to call, but the word drowned in screaming.

An elbow caught her near the ear. Hands gripped her sleeves—not to help, only to move her aside. The fleeing crowd became a river, and she was too heavy, too slow, too rooted in the wrong direction. Someone slammed into her from behind. She stumbled, caught herself on the pew, felt a tearing pain low in her abdomen that vanished as quickly as it came.

The gun fired again.

A different sound now because she knew what it was.

The young man collapsed.

For a heartbeat, the room emptied of noise. Or Barbara emptied of hearing. She saw mouths open, arms waving, people ducking, running, bending over Rabbi Adler, bending over the young man, though no one seemed able to decide where help belonged first. Everywhere, help belonged everywhere.

Then the crowd took her.

A woman seized Barbara’s hand. “Come on, come on!”

“No,” Barbara said. Or thought she said. She twisted back toward the front, toward the rabbi, toward the shooter, toward the impossible work of undoing what had been done. But the woman was strong with terror. Others pressed from behind. Barbara’s feet slipped, found purchase, slipped again. She was carried down the aisle, not walking so much as being delivered by panic.

Daniel appeared at her side, white-faced, one lens of his glasses smudged. “Barbara!”

“I have to—”

“No!”

He wrapped an arm around her shoulders and another around her belly, shielding, steering. She fought him for one foolish second. Her own husband. Her own child inside her. And still she fought, because the sanctuary was behind them and the world had broken there and how could they be leaving?

Cold air struck her like water.

They were outside. People spilled down the steps, some crying, some silent, some looking back with faces of disbelief. A man stood bareheaded in the snow, rocking back and forth, saying, “My God, my God,” though whether prayer or accusation, Barbara could not tell. Sirens began somewhere far away, then multiplied.

Daniel guided her to the side of the building, away from the doors. “Sit,” he said.

“There’s nowhere.”

“Then lean on me.”

She leaned. The brick wall was hard against her shoulder. The baby moved—one strong kick beneath her ribs. Alive. Insistent. Offended, perhaps, by all this commotion. Barbara put both hands over the place.

All around her, fragments of sentences flew loose.

“Was it blanks?”

“No, no—blood—”

“Rabbi Adler—”

“Who was he?”

“I saw him—”

“Don’t look, don’t go back in—”

An ambulance arrived, then police cars, their lights staining the snow red and blue. Men ran inside carrying equipment. The doors opened and shut, opened and shut, releasing brief gusts of warmth and horror. Barbara watched each opening as if the building might explain itself.

Daniel kept asking if she was hurt.

No, she said.

Was she sure?

Yes.

Any pain?

No.

But there had been pain. There was always pain now. How could she separate the ordinary pains of becoming a mother from the extraordinary pain of standing too near death? Her back hurt, her side hurt where the pew had struck her, her throat hurt from the word she had not managed to say. Stop. Wait. Please.

She looked down and saw that one of her coat buttons had come loose and hung by a thread. The sight of it nearly undid her. That button had endured all morning—her tugging, her impatience, her body’s refusal to fit into its old life—and now here it was, dangling stupidly after gunfire. She began to laugh, one short breath that turned into a sob.

Daniel pulled her close. “It’s all right.”

It was not all right. She hated him for saying it and loved him for having nothing else.

In the days afterward, people asked what she had seen.

This was how they asked: gently, greedily, fearfully. They brought casseroles and questions. They lowered their voices in her kitchen, as if the baby might be sleeping and should not be disturbed by tragedy it had already attended. Was she very close? Did she see the gun? Did Rabbi Adler say anything? Did the young man speak? Was it true Barbara had tried to go to him?

She answered as best she could until the answers became smooth and false from handling. Yes, she had been there. Yes, Rabbi Adler had fallen. Yes, the young man had turned the gun on himself. No, she did not know why. No, she had not reached him. No, she did not know what she would have done if she had.

Privately, she imagined it again and again.

In one version, the crowd parted. In one version, she placed her hand on the young man’s wrist, not hard, just enough. In one version, he looked at her belly and remembered he had once been carried safely inside someone. In one version, Rabbi Adler rose unharmed, annoyed by all the fuss, and finished his sermon on responsibility. In one version, nobody died because Barbara had crossed the sanctuary in time.

Foolish. Worse than foolish. Vanity dressed as grief. She knew this. And still, at night, when Daniel slept and the baby practiced its secret swimming, she lay awake and walked that aisle.

The child was born three weeks later, a girl with a furious cry and a dark cap of hair. They named her Miriam, after Daniel’s grandmother, though Barbara privately thought the name belonged also to the prophetess who had stood with a tambourine at the edge of the sea. A woman who watched waters close over danger and still found a song afterward. Imagine such a woman. Imagine such nerve.

When the nurse placed Miriam on Barbara’s chest, the baby’s body was slick and warm, astonishingly heavy for something so new. Barbara counted her fingers. Ten. She counted again. The baby opened her eyes, unfocused and dark.

You heard it, Barbara thought. You were there.

But she did not say this aloud. What would be the point of giving a child such an inheritance at her first hour? The world would hand her enough sorrow in time. Let her begin with milk, with blankets, with her father weeping clumsily beside the hospital bed.

Still, Barbara wondered what had entered the child that day along with the sound of shots and screams. Fear, perhaps. Or urgency. Or the knowledge that rooms believed safe could break open without warning. She hoped something else had entered too: the impulse to move toward a person on the edge, even when the crowd ran the other way. Not because one could always save him. Not because courage guaranteed anything. It guaranteed nothing. But because a human being turning toward death should not have only death looking back.

Years later, Miriam would ask about the scar on Barbara’s hand, a thin white line near the thumb from where a splintered pew had caught her as she was swept away.

Barbara would say, “That happened before you were born.”

“Did it hurt?”

“Yes,” Barbara would answer, after a moment. “But not as much as you’d think.”

And Miriam, being a child, would accept this and run back outside, where the afternoon waited with its ordinary miracles: bicycles, chalk dust, the neighbor’s dog barking at nothing.

Barbara would stand at the window and watch her daughter go. The world remained dangerous. It also remained full of doors opening, children calling, light falling through glass. On some mornings, she could almost forgive it.

But never completely.

And perhaps forgiveness had never been the task. Perhaps the task was simply to keep walking toward the living, even after the river of fear had carried you away.
