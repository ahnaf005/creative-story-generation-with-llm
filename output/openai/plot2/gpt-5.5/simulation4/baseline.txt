On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara Feldman stood before her bedroom mirror and tried to decide whether the navy dress made her look like a ship.

“It’s respectable,” her husband, David, called from the hallway, where he was wrestling their three-year-old, Sammy, into a wool coat.

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It’s lovely,” he amended.

Barbara turned sideways. The child inside her shifted, a slow rolling motion beneath her ribs, as if in agreement or complaint. She was due in less than three weeks, and every dress had become a negotiation. She smoothed her hands over the dome of her belly and looked at herself with the sternness she usually reserved for underbaked kugel.

“It’s too tight.”

“Everything is too tight,” David said, appearing in the doorway with Sammy’s hat in one hand and Sammy’s left shoe in the other. “That’s what happens when you’re carrying a basketball with opinions.”

Barbara tried not to smile, failed, and took the hat from him. “We are going to be late.”

“It’s shul. Half the congregation will be later than us.”

“Not today,” she said. “Rabbi Adler is speaking.”

At that, David’s teasing faded into something warmer. “Then we’d better hurry.”

They drove through the cold brightness of a Michigan winter, past lawns sealed under snow and houses with smoke unraveling from their chimneys. Southfield was still new enough to feel like a promise—streets wide, houses square, families arriving with furniture, children, and the quiet hope that here, finally, they could plant themselves. Barbara had grown up in Detroit, in the crowded rooms of her parents’ flat above her father’s dry-goods store. Southfield, with its cul-de-sacs and tidy driveways, seemed to her both ordinary and astonishing.

She held Sammy’s mittened hand as they crossed the synagogue parking lot. The cold pinched her cheeks. Inside, the air was warm with wool coats, perfume, damp boots, and the faint sweetness of polished wood. Voices rose and folded into one another—greetings, coughs, the rustle of prayer books, the shrill protest of a child too young to understand reverence.

Barbara loved the sanctuary most in the minutes before services began. There was an expectant hush beneath the noise, like the pause before a song. Sunlight came through the high windows in pale bars, touching the rows of pews, the bimah, the ark. People nodded to one another across the aisles. Mrs. Rosenblatt leaned over to ask Barbara how she was feeling, and Barbara gave the answer all heavily pregnant women learn to give: “Fine.”

“You look beautiful,” Mrs. Rosenblatt said.

“I look enormous.”

“You look blessed.”

Barbara accepted that because it was Shabbat and because Mrs. Rosenblatt had survived enough in her life to know the difference.

They found seats midway down the sanctuary. Barbara lowered herself carefully, one hand on the back of the pew, one on her belly. Sammy wedged himself between her and David, immediately dropping his mittens and whispering, “Can I have a candy?”

“After,” David whispered.

“You always say after.”

“And after always comes.”

Barbara opened her prayer book, though the words swam. She was tired in the deep, mineral way of late pregnancy; even her bones seemed to be waiting. But when Rabbi Adler stepped forward, the sanctuary settled.

He was a man who carried calm with him like a garment. Not softness—Barbara would never have called him soft—but steadiness. His hair had silvered at the temples; his face held the alert kindness of someone who had listened to more sorrows than could ever be answered. He spoke often of responsibility, of justice, of a Judaism that lived not only in the synagogue but in kitchens, union halls, school board meetings, and the way a man treated the clerk who shortchanged him.

Barbara remembered once hearing him say that faith was not a shelter from the world but a lantern to carry into it. She had written the line on an envelope and kept it in her recipe drawer.

That morning, he spoke of Moses, of leadership, of the dangerous distance between zeal and righteousness. Barbara listened with one hand resting on the life inside her. She wondered if the baby could hear him. She hoped so. Let this be one of the first voices you know, she thought.

A young man sat several rows ahead and to the side, near the aisle.

Barbara noticed him only because he seemed alone in a room of pairs and families. He was thin, dark-haired, in a suit that did not quite fit at the shoulders. He leaned forward, elbows on knees, hands clasped so tightly that the knuckles stood pale. Once, he turned his head, and Barbara saw his profile: sharp nose, strained mouth, eyes fixed on the rabbi with an intensity that made her uneasy.

She told herself not to stare. A synagogue held all kinds of people: the grieving, the lonely, the troubled, the merely tired. Perhaps he was a student. Perhaps he had lost someone. Perhaps she was noticing him only because pregnancy had made her senses animal-sharp; lately she could smell oranges from two rooms away and detect sorrow in a stranger’s shoulders.

Rabbi Adler’s voice continued, measured and rich.

Then came the sound.

Barbara did not understand it at first. It cracked through the sanctuary, impossibly loud, flattening every other noise. For the smallest fraction of a second, no one moved. The human mind is mercifully slow before catastrophe; it offers disbelief as a final courtesy.

Then Rabbi Adler fell.

The sanctuary broke.

A woman screamed, and then everyone seemed to scream. David’s arm shot across Barbara and Sammy. Prayer books tumbled. People surged up from the pews, faces open with terror, mouths forming questions no one could hear. Another crack split the air. Barbara saw the young man standing now, arm extended, the dark shape of a gun in his hand.

Later, she would remember details in pieces: a tallis slipping from someone’s shoulders like a shed skin; Sammy’s face turned upward, white and stunned; the smell of wool and panic; the sunlight, still pure and indifferent on the floor.

David grabbed Sammy under the arms. “Barbara. Move!”

But Barbara’s body had gone strangely still.

The young man wavered near the aisle. His face had changed. The terrible focus had drained from it, leaving a look so young, so lost, that Barbara felt something inside her lurch—not fear, though fear was everywhere—but recognition. He looked like someone at the edge of a roof who had only just realized how high he had climbed.

“No,” she heard herself say.

David pulled at her sleeve. “Barbara!”

The crowd pushed toward the exits. Someone slammed into her from behind, and pain sparked across her back. She clutched the pew to keep from falling. Her belly made her clumsy, anchored, vulnerable. Around her, bodies pressed and shoved, driven by the simple animal need to live.

The young man lifted the gun again, but not toward the congregation.

Barbara understood.

“No!” she shouted, louder this time, though the word vanished beneath the cries.

She moved into the aisle.

It was not courage. She would resist that word for the rest of her life. Courage sounded clean, deliberate, like something chosen after reflection. What took hold of her then was more primitive: a refusal as sudden as lightning. Not another death. Not in this place. Not before the ark, beneath the lamps, in front of children.

“Stop!” she called to him. “Please!”

His head turned. For an instant, their eyes met.

He was barely more than a boy.

Barbara began to reach out her hand.

Then the crowd swept her away.

A man barreled into her shoulder, and she spun sideways. Someone’s elbow struck her arm. David shouted her name from somewhere beyond the crush. Sammy was crying now, high and terrified. Barbara tried to plant her feet, but the tide of people carried her backward, away from the aisle, away from the young man, toward the doors.

“I’m pregnant!” someone yelled—perhaps Barbara, perhaps another woman—and hands appeared, not cruel hands but desperate ones, guiding, pushing, saving and endangering at once.

There was one final sound behind her.

Afterward, silence did not come. Not true silence. There were sobs, sirens, shouted instructions, prayers spoken too quickly, the scrape of chairs, the pounding of feet. Outside in the freezing air, Barbara found herself on the synagogue steps without knowing how she had arrived there. David held Sammy against his chest. Sammy’s face was buried in his father’s coat.

“Are you hurt?” David asked her. His voice cracked. “Barbara, are you hurt?”

She looked down at herself. Her dress was wrinkled, one sleeve torn at the seam. Her hands shook violently. “I don’t think so.”

“The baby?”

At that, fear pierced the numbness. She pressed both hands to her belly and waited.

Nothing.

“Come on,” David said. “We’ll get you to a doctor.”

“Wait.”

“Barbara—”

“Wait.”

She stood amid the crowd of congregants spilling into the snow, their Saturday faces transformed. Men without hats. Women crying into gloves. Children confused by adult terror. Someone had blood on his cuff. Someone else kept repeating the rabbi’s name as if repetition could call him back.

Then the baby moved.

A firm, indignant kick beneath her palm.

Barbara gasped, then laughed once—a broken, astonished sound—and began to cry. David put his free arm around her, pulling her and Sammy close, the three of them clinging together in the cold.

Behind them, the synagogue doors opened and closed. Men ran in and out. The police arrived. An ambulance came, then another. The clean snow at the edge of the walkway was trampled into gray slush.

Barbara kept seeing the young man’s eyes.

That evening, after the doctor had examined her and declared the baby stubbornly healthy, after Sammy had fallen asleep between them in their bed because none of them could bear to place him alone in his room, Barbara sat at the kitchen table with the lights off.

David found her there near midnight.

“You should sleep,” he said.

“So should you.”

He pulled out a chair and sat across from her. For a long time they listened to the house—the furnace ticking, Sammy murmuring in dreams, the wind pressing against the windows.

“I tried to get to him,” Barbara said.

David closed his eyes. “I know.”

“If I had—”

“No.”

“You don’t know what I’m going to say.”

“Yes, I do.”

She looked down at her hands. They still seemed to be reaching.

“He was so young,” she whispered.

David’s face tightened. “He killed Rabbi Adler.”

“I know.”

“He could have killed you.”

“I know that too.”

“Then why—”

“Because for one second he looked like he wanted someone to stop him.”

David said nothing.

Barbara turned toward the dark window. Her reflection hovered there: round-bellied, pale, older than she had been that morning. “Maybe I imagined it.”

“Maybe you didn’t.”

“I couldn’t reach him.”

“No,” David said softly. “You couldn’t.”

The words should have comforted her. They did not. They only named the fact.

In the days that followed, the city seemed to speak in lowered voices. Rabbi Adler’s death moved through homes and shops and schools like a winter fog. People who had heard him lecture, people who had shaken his hand once, people who knew him only by reputation—all felt somehow personally bereaved. At the funeral, Barbara stood in the back because sitting had become difficult, and because she feared being trapped again in a crowd.

The sanctuary was full, though not the same. It could never be the same. Every creak made people turn. Every sudden motion drew the eye. Grief had entered the wood and walls.

Speaker after speaker rose to honor the rabbi: his learning, his moral force, his humor, his belief that a synagogue must have windows facing the world. Barbara listened, one hand on her belly, and thought of the envelope in her recipe drawer. Faith is not a shelter from the world but a lantern to carry into it.

When the baby came two weeks later, it was during a storm.

Snow flung itself against the hospital windows while Barbara labored through the night. David sat beside her, looking helpless and devoted, feeding her ice chips as if they were medicine. At dawn, with the sky just beginning to lighten, their daughter was born red-faced and furious, announcing herself with a cry strong enough to startle the nurse.

“A girl,” David said, weeping openly.

Barbara held the baby against her chest. The child’s head was damp, her fists tightly closed, her mouth rooting blindly for comfort. Life, Barbara thought, was outrageously insistent. It came wailing into a world that had given it every reason to hesitate.

“What should we call her?” David asked.

They had discussed names, of course. Sensible names, family names. But Barbara looked at her daughter and knew.

“Liora,” she said.

David smiled through his tears. “My light.”

“Yes.”

Years passed, as years do, both quickly and one breath at a time.

The synagogue repaired what could be repaired. The congregation mourned, argued, prayed, continued. Children grew. Old men died in ordinary ways. Babies were named. Couples married beneath canopies. Teenagers slouched through services and later returned with children of their own.

Barbara did not speak often of that morning. When people asked, she gave simple answers. Yes, she had been there. Yes, she had seen. No, she did not like to discuss it. But memory has its own schedule. It rose at odd moments: when a door slammed, when a young man stood alone at the edge of a gathering, when Liora, at sixteen, shouted that no one understood anything and ran upstairs in tears.

Barbara would pause then, hand on the banister, and remember another young face, another unbearable loneliness.

She did not forgive him. Forgiveness, to her, was not a door one opened because people admired the gesture. Some acts remained terrible no matter how much sorrow surrounded them. But she found, over time, that she could mourn the rabbi and also mourn the boy who had destroyed him. This was not balance. It was not justice. It was simply the shape her heart had taken.

On Liora’s eighteenth birthday, Barbara gave her the envelope from the recipe drawer. The paper had yellowed; the handwriting had faded but remained legible.

Faith is not a shelter from the world but a lantern to carry into it.

“Rabbi Adler said that?” Liora asked.

“Yes.”

Liora traced the words with her finger. She had Barbara’s dark eyes and David’s crooked smile. “Is that why you named me Liora?”

Barbara looked at her daughter—alive, impatient, luminous—and thought of a cold morning, a crowded sanctuary, a hand reaching and not reaching.

“One reason,” she said.

“What’s the other?”

Barbara touched her cheek. “Because even on the worst day, there was still light.”
