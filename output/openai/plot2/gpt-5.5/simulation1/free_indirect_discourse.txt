On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara could not fasten the top button of her good blue coat.

She stood in the bedroom with one hand beneath the shelf of her belly and the other worrying the button through its woolen slit, as if persuasion might accomplish what tailoring could not. Eight months, nearly nine, depending on which aunt was asked and whether she counted from the doctor’s date or from the day Barbara herself knew. The baby pressed outward, not kicking so much as insisting. All right, all right, she thought, you are coming too.

Outside, Southfield wore a crust of old snow along the curbs. The sky hung low and pewter-colored over the new ranch houses and half-finished streets, over yards where saplings had been wrapped in burlap against the winter. Detroit was only a short drive away, yet here everything seemed newly arranged, streets bending around subdivisions with names that promised serenity. Cranbrook this, Evergreen that. A place made for starting over, for planting roots, for saying yes, this will do.

Her husband, David, called from the front hall. “Barb? We should go.”

She found him holding her gloves, her prayer book tucked beneath his arm. He looked at her belly, then at the rebellious button, and smiled with the helpless tenderness that had begun to irritate her.

“If you say I look beautiful,” she said, “I’ll throw something.”

“I was going to say we’re late.”

That was better. She allowed him to help her into the car.

The synagogue rose broad and pale against the winter morning, its modern lines seeming both majestic and unfinished, as though faith itself had decided to move to the suburbs and was still learning the roads. Cars filled the lot. Men in dark coats hurried with shoulders hunched against the cold; women shepherded children toward the entrance, lifting them over icy patches. Barbara moved slowly, her boots careful on the salted pavement. Everyone told her to take care, to watch her step, to let someone else carry the kugel, the purse, the prayer book, the whole enormous world. As if a woman with child became glass. As if she were not also bone, blood, appetite, temper.

Inside, the lobby smelled of damp wool, perfume, and coffee from some distant kitchen. Voices gathered and overlapped. Shabbat greetings. Mazel tovs. A baby admired here, an engagement whispered there, a business trouble tucked into a sigh. Barbara felt the baby shift at the sound of it all, a private swimmer in a sea of public murmuring.

They found seats not far from the aisle. David wanted her near the end, in case she needed to stand, stretch, escape. Escape. She nearly laughed. Where would she go? To the ladies’ room with its pink soap and stern fluorescent light? To the car? To a hotel in Miami until labor began? No, she would sit. She would rise. She would pray. She would do what women had always done while the world conducted itself around them.

At the front, Rabbi Adler stood with the grave warmth that made people lean toward him before they knew they were leaning. He was not a tall man in Barbara’s memory, though perhaps he was; what mattered was the steadiness. His voice had a way of smoothing the room without softening the words. People said renowned. People said brilliant. People said he had marched, spoken, written, counseled, argued. Barbara thought of him as the man who had once placed a hand on David’s shoulder after David’s father died and said nothing at all, which had been precisely right.

The service unfolded, familiar as breathing. Pages turned. The congregation rose and sat in waves. The cantor’s voice climbed, silver and sure, and Barbara closed her eyes. Her lower back ached. The baby pressed a heel—or was it an elbow?—beneath her ribs. She imagined, as she often did now, the child’s face: dark hair like David’s, perhaps; her mother’s stubborn chin; eyes still sealed against the light. A boy, a girl. They had argued over names in bed, whispered them into the dark like secrets being tested for strength. Daniel. Ruth. Miriam. Samuel. Nothing settled. There was still time.

There was always still time until there wasn’t.

A young man sat several rows ahead and across the aisle. Barbara noticed him only because he moved differently from the others. Not rudely. Not even nervously, exactly. He had a contained look, shoulders drawn inward, face pale above his collar. Young enough to be someone’s son home from college, old enough to be mistaken for a man by those who did not look carefully. He stared toward the front with an intensity that made her uncomfortable.

She knew that look from nowhere and everywhere. From boys waiting outside principals’ offices. From men at bus stops after bad news. From her own mirror, once, years ago, after the doctor had told her the first pregnancy had not held and everyone had advised cheerfulness. A person could sit among others and still be locked in a room no one else could see.

Stop staring, Barbara told herself. It was unkind. He was only listening.

Rabbi Adler began to speak. His sermon moved through the sanctuary with measured force. Barbara did not catch every sentence. Pregnancy had made her mind porous; words entered, glowed briefly, and slipped away. Yet certain phrases stayed. Responsibility. Covenant. The sanctity of every human life. To turn toward suffering rather than away. How easy, the rabbi said, to praise peace in the abstract; how difficult to make peace with the frightened, the bitter, the wounded soul beside us.

The young man stood.

At first it seemed part of the service, though it was the wrong moment. Heads turned. A cough stopped mid-throat. The rabbi paused, not startled so much as attentive. Barbara’s hand went to her belly.

The young man moved toward the front.

No one stopped him. Why would they? This was shul; people rose unexpectedly to adjust a tallit, to tend to a child, to leave if they were faint or ill. He took several steps. His coat hung oddly, one side heavier than the other.

Then there was the gun.

Later Barbara would understand that there must have been an instant before the first shot when everyone saw and no one believed. The mind, merciful fool, refused the offered picture. A gun in the sanctuary? No. A child’s toy. A prop. A mistake of eyesight, a trick of winter light.

The sound tore through the room.

It was not like in pictures. It was larger, flatter, final. A crack that entered the body and stayed there. Someone screamed. Someone shouted the rabbi’s name. Rabbi Adler staggered, his hand lifting as if to quiet the room, still the teacher, still the shepherd for half a breath after violence had found him. Then he fell.

Barbara did not stand. She was already standing. How had that happened? David’s arm caught her, but she pulled away. The young man held the gun with both hands now, or perhaps one; she could not see clearly because people were rising, ducking, surging. His face—she saw his face. Not triumphant. Not monstrous. Empty and astonished, as though the shot had surprised him too.

No, she thought. No, no, no. Not here. Not in front of children. Not in front of God. Not in front of her unborn child, who rolled inside her as if the whole little sea had overturned.

The young man turned the gun toward himself.

Barbara moved into the aisle.

She did not decide. The body decided before thought could lay claim. He was somebody’s baby too. The sentence appeared in her with terrible clarity. Somebody had once counted his fingers, warmed bottles, pressed a palm to his fevered forehead. Somebody had bought him shoes and wondered if he was lonely. If she could reach him, touch his sleeve, say wait—what? Wait, darling? Wait, please? She was not his mother. She was no one. But no one was still someone.

“Barbara!” David shouted.

The crowd broke.

It came from behind and beside her, a living wall of fear. Men shoved toward the exits. Women clutched handbags, children, one another. Prayer books fell open on the carpet and were trampled. A chair overturned. Someone’s glasses cracked beneath a heel. The sanctuary, which a moment before had held the ordered choreography of worship, became all elbows, backs, open mouths.

Barbara tried to push forward. Her belly struck someone’s hip; pain flashed low and hot. She gasped. A man’s shoulder drove into her arm and spun her sideways. She saw the young man only in fragments now: dark sleeve, white cheek, the black impossible object in his hand. So close. She was so close. If everyone would stop, if they would only stop, she could—

Another shot.

The crowd made a sound then unlike anything human, though of course it was made only of humans. Barbara’s knees weakened. David reached her again, or perhaps she reached him. His hands were under her arms, pulling, shielding, cursing in a voice she had never heard from him. She fought him.

“Let me go,” she said, though no sound seemed to leave her mouth.

“Barbara, move!”

“He’ll die.”

But he had already turned the gun. Or was about to. Time had broken into shards and each shard showed something different. The rabbi fallen. The young man fallen. People falling, kneeling, crawling. A woman sobbing the Shema. A child calling for his father. The eternal light above the ark burning steadily, obscenely steady.

David dragged her toward the side aisle. She stumbled over a coat. Her hand struck the end of a pew and pain rang up her wrist. The baby kicked hard, once, twice, furious. Good, Barbara thought wildly. Good, be angry. Be loud. Stay with me.

In the lobby, cold air knifed through open doors. People poured out into the February morning without hats, without coats, without dignity, which was to say with nothing but life. A woman had lost one shoe. An old man leaned against the wall, his face gray, murmuring in Yiddish. Someone shouted for an ambulance. Someone shouted for the police. Someone vomited into a planter.

Barbara bent forward, palms braced on her knees, though there was little room to bend. Her lungs would not fill. David kept saying her name. She wished he would stop. Names belonged to a world where things could be summoned and answered.

“Are you hurt?” he asked.

She looked down. No blood on her coat. No blood on her dress. Only salt stains at the hem, a smear of dirt on one glove, the round blue enormity of herself intact. Inside, the baby moved again, slower now. A turning. A listening.

“I tried,” she said.

“What?”

“I tried to get to him.”

David stared at her. His face was wet, though she did not know if from tears or melted snow or fear. “You what?”

She could not explain. It sounded foolish already, obscene even. The rabbi had been shot. A man had shot him. Then the man had shot himself. What did trying matter against accomplished facts? She had not reached anyone. She had been carried backward by the tide of the living, away from the dying, away from whatever last word might have been possible.

Sirens approached, thin at first, then swelling. Police cars came hard into the lot, tires spitting slush. Men in uniforms pushed through the doors. Ambulance attendants followed with stretchers. The synagogue kept releasing people, each one blinking as if expelled from underground. Some clung to one another. Some stood alone, mouths open to the cold.

Barbara sank onto a bench near the entrance. No one told her not to sit; at last the world had found something more urgent than her pregnancy. She placed both hands over the baby and felt the taut drum of her body beneath the coat.

Rabbi Adler’s voice returned to her in pieces. To turn toward suffering rather than away. Had he said that? Or had she made it up afterward because it was the sort of thing he would have said, the sort of thing people needed him to have said? The mind was already repairing, rearranging, laying boards over the hole.

A woman beside her, Mrs. Klein from two rows back, whispered, “How could this happen here?”

Here. That was the word everyone would use. Here, meaning among Jews. Here, meaning in synagogue. Here, meaning Southfield, not some battlefield, not Birmingham, not Europe, not the old nightmares wrapped in her parents’ accents. Here, where the parking lot was full of Buicks and Oldsmobiles, where children learned Hebrew after school and mothers compared obstetricians, where men argued about bonds and baseball and whether Detroit was changing too fast or not fast enough.

But Barbara knew, with a certainty that frightened her, that here had never been separate from there. Doors did not keep out despair. Sanctuaries did not dissolve anguish. A man could carry a winter inside him anywhere.

They would say his name later. They would print it. People would repeat it in kitchens, lowering their voices. They would ask what he had wanted, whether anyone had known, whether something could have been done. They would say disturbed, troubled, brilliant, sick, lost. They would make of him a warning or a riddle. Barbara would remember instead the pale face, the stunned emptiness after the first shot, the unbearable fact that before he became the shooter he had been a boy, and before that a baby, and before that hidden beneath some woman’s heart.

The ambulance doors slammed. A stretcher passed, surrounded by men moving fast. Someone cried out, “Rabbi!” and the sound tore the morning open again. Barbara tried to rise, but David pressed a hand to her shoulder.

“Don’t,” he said.

She did not know if the shape beneath the sheet was Rabbi Adler or the young man. That uncertainty shamed her, then seemed exactly the point. Two bodies carried from the same holy place. One beloved by hundreds. One perhaps by no one present. Both had mothers. Both had names.

Hours later—though perhaps it was twenty minutes—David brought the car around. He helped her in as if she were breakable, and this time she let him. The seat was cold. The baby had gone quiet, which frightened her until a small shove answered beneath her ribs.

On the drive home neither of them spoke. Houses passed, tidy and impossible. A boy in a red hat dragged a sled up a driveway. Smoke rose from chimneys. At a stoplight, Barbara watched a woman in the next car apply lipstick in the rearview mirror, carefully shaping her mouth as if the world had not altered. Of course it hadn’t, not for everyone. That was one of the cruelties. Catastrophe chose a room, a family, a date on the calendar. Elsewhere, lipstick. Elsewhere, toast browning. Elsewhere, babies not yet born.

At home, David wanted to call the doctor. She said no. Then yes. Then no again. She removed her blue coat and saw that the top button, the stubborn one, was gone. Torn loose in the crowd. She held the empty thread between her fingers and began to cry—not for the button, though for the button too; for the rabbi; for the young man; for David’s shaking hands; for the child inside her who had heard gunfire before lullabies; for a sanctuary breached; for the terrible knowledge that love did not always arrive in time.

That night she lay awake while David slept badly beside her. The house creaked in the cold. Her belly rose under the blanket like a moon. She pressed her palm there and waited until the baby moved.

Listen, she thought, though to whom she could not say. To the child, to God, to the dead, to the living who had fled because living insists upon itself. Listen. I tried.

But another thought came, quieter and less forgiving: trying was not reaching.

Then, beneath her hand, the baby turned—a slow, firm movement, not absolution, not answer, simply life shifting in the dark. Barbara closed her eyes. In a few weeks, perhaps days, there would be pain of another kind. Blood, cries, a child lifted into the light. She would count fingers. She would warm bottles. She would press her palm to a fevered forehead. She would teach a name to answer when called.

And someday, not soon, she would tell that child about a winter morning when the world broke open in a holy room and everyone ran. She would tell it carefully. Not to frighten. Not to make a legend. Only to say: there is suffering beside us, even when we cannot see it. Turn toward it if you can. Hold fast if you cannot. And when the crowd sweeps you away, remember what your heart tried to do before fear carried your body elsewhere.

Outside, the snow began again, softening the tire marks in the street, covering the salt, the dirty curbs, the whole wounded suburb in silence. Inside, Barbara kept her hand on the child and listened to the small insistence of life beneath her palm.
