On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara could not get her left boot zipped.

This seemed, at first, the whole trouble of the day.

She sat on the edge of the bed with her belly lifted between her elbows, a great forward fact, and bent as far as she could. The leather wrinkled stubbornly over her ankle. Snowlight pressed at the bedroom curtains; outside, Southfield lay under a hard February glitter, the lawns white and the streets scraped into gray lanes. From downstairs came the clink of her husband’s spoon against a coffee cup, his radio murmuring about Vietnam, the mayor, the temperature, the usual inventory of a world determined to be busy.

“David,” she called, but not loudly. She hated needing help for such small humiliations.

He came up with the newspaper folded under his arm, already wearing his overcoat. “Boot?”

“Don’t laugh.”

“I wouldn’t dare.”

He knelt before her. The sight of him there, tugging gently at the zipper, made her suddenly tender and cross at once. All winter she had been expanding out of her own life: out of dresses, out of sleep, out of patience, out of the ability to bend down and claim herself. The baby shifted as if objecting to the angle.

“Your son,” she said, “is sitting on my lungs.”

“Daughter,” David said. He had decided it was a daughter because he wanted someone in the family to look like Barbara.

“Your daughter, then, is impossible.”

“There.” The zipper gave way, slid up, and the boot became a boot again.

She smoothed her coat over her belly, though no smoothing could disguise anything now. Eight and a half months. Nearly nine. Any day, people said, with that cheerful cruelty of those who were not themselves waiting to split open and become two people.

At the synagogue, there would be congratulations and touching. Women she barely knew would place their hands on her as if she were a mezuzah, something to kiss for luck. She would endure it. It was Shabbat, after all; she would see Rabbi Adler speak; she would sit beside David beneath the high ceiling among the rustle of prayer books and wool coats, and for a little while the baby might be persuaded by Hebrew and song to stop kicking at her ribs.

They drove carefully. The car heater coughed out warmth that smelled faintly of dust. Barbara watched the bare trees pass, black script against the snow. She had grown up in Detroit proper, in a neighborhood that had changed street by street while adults discussed it in lowered voices. Southfield was supposed to mean quiet, safety, good schools, lawns wide enough to prove a person had arrived somewhere. And yet she missed the old grocery with sawdust on the floor, the women leaning from porches, the butcher who saved marrow bones for her mother. Safety, she had learned, had its own loneliness.

At the synagogue entrance, men stamped snow from their shoes. Women shook crystals from fur collars. Someone exclaimed over her size before even saying good Shabbos.

“You’re still here?” Mrs. Feld asked, laughing.

Where else would she be, Barbara wondered, on the moon?

“Still here,” she said.

“And glowing.”

Sweating, yes. Swelling. Glowing if one were charitable and half blind.

They found seats not too far from the aisle because Barbara liked knowing she could leave if she needed air. The sanctuary was full and bright. Light poured through high windows, touching the polished wood with an almost watery sheen. On the bimah, Rabbi Adler stood with that particular stillness he had, a collected quality that made even his silence feel prepared.

He was not a tall man, not imposing in the ordinary sense, and yet the room leaned toward him. People loved him for his learning, yes, but also for the way he seemed to see them without reducing them to their troubles. When Barbara had lost her father two years before, Rabbi Adler had taken both her hands in his office and said nothing for a full minute. At the time she had thought, absurdly, that silence might be a form of shelter.

Now his voice rose over the congregation, firm and warm. Barbara opened her prayer book, lost the place, found it, lost it again. The baby rolled. She pressed her palm beneath her belly and imagined a small heel, a curled fist. Be still, little one. Listen. This is part of what you are entering: men in dark suits, women with lipstick on coffee cups, children whispering in the rows, old melodies carrying grief and gratitude in the same breath.

David’s shoulder brushed hers. He sang softly, not well. She loved him most when he sang badly.

The service moved onward. A cough. A page turning. The faint perfume of face powder and damp wool. Barbara’s back began its familiar ache, a low band tightening from spine to hip. She shifted, and the woman behind her made a sympathetic sound, as if Barbara were a public clock counting down.

After the Torah reading, there was a stir near the front. A young man stood where Barbara had not noticed him before. Thin, pale, with dark hair combed too carefully. He wore a suit that did not sit right on him, as though borrowed for an occasion someone had failed to explain. For a moment she thought he was a student, perhaps one of the college boys who came to ask Rabbi Adler fierce questions about God and injustice and whether old men understood the new world at all.

The young man moved toward the bimah.

Barbara felt, before she understood anything, a small wrongness enter the room. Not fear yet. Fear required a shape. This was only a tear in the fabric, a draft under a closed door.

Rabbi Adler turned toward him.

The young man raised his arm.

There was a crack—sharp, obscene, impossible in that holy space.

For half a second no one moved. The sound did not belong to anything they knew. Not here. Not among prayer books and velvet coverings and the eternal light. Barbara’s mind, faithful to ordinary life, offered explanations: a fallen chair, a burst bulb, some foolish prank. Then Rabbi Adler staggered.

A woman screamed.

The second shot seemed to happen inside Barbara’s bones.

People rose at once, benches scraping, prayer books dropping, voices breaking loose into a single animal cry. David’s hand clamped around her wrist.

“Barbara. Come on.”

But she was looking at the young man.

He stood with the gun still in his hand, his face emptied out, or filled with something too dense to read. He looked younger now, terribly young. A boy who had done a thing and could not return to the moment before doing it. Around him bodies surged backward, away, away, away, but he remained near the front as if stranded on an island he had made himself.

Rabbi Adler was down. Men bent over him. Someone shouted for a doctor. Someone else cried, “Get down!” Children wailed without understanding why their mothers’ arms hurt them.

David pulled harder. “We have to go.”

No, Barbara thought. No, wait.

She could not have said what she intended. To help the rabbi? Others were already there. To stop the young man? From what? He had already fired. To ask him why? As if why were a cup of water she could carry back to the congregation, passing it hand to hand until everyone’s thirst was relieved.

The baby kicked violently.

Barbara stepped into the aisle.

“Barbara!”

The aisle was no longer an aisle but a river. People collided, shoved, fell against one another. Hats tilted. Glasses dropped and shattered underfoot. An old man clutched the back of a pew, his mouth open, no sound coming out. Barbara saw all of it in fragments, each one bright and separate: a girl’s white glove on the carpet, a prayer shawl dragging, lipstick smeared across a woman’s cheek like blood though it was only lipstick, only lipstick.

The young man’s eyes flicked toward the congregation. Toward her? Impossible. And yet for one thin instant Barbara felt he had seen her belly first, then her face. Seen not Barbara exactly, but the absurdity of life insisting on itself ten yards from death.

Put it down, she wanted to say. Sweetheart, put it down.

Sweetheart. Where had that come from? She was not his mother. His mother might be in this very room, or at home setting out lunch, or dead, or never properly his mother at all. Still the word rose in her with unbearable force. Sweetheart, no more.

She tried to move forward.

A man slammed into her side. Her balance went. David caught her around the shoulders, but the crowd caught them both and took them. She was turned, pressed, carried backward. Her belly was crushed against someone’s coat; panic flared white. Not the baby. Not the baby. She drove her elbows out, heard herself making a sound she did not recognize, a deep furious noise.

“Pregnant!” David shouted. “She’s pregnant! Let her through!”

Let her through where? There was nowhere. Everyone was trying to get through everyone else.

Another crack came from the front.

The crowd convulsed.

Barbara did not see the young man fall. She only felt the sanctuary change after the shot, as if a cord had been cut and all the terrible tension dropped at once into chaos. Someone behind her sobbed, “He shot himself, he shot himself,” and the words passed from mouth to mouth, less information than contagion.

David forced a path toward the side doors, shielding her with his body. She wanted to tell him she had to go back. The rabbi. The boy. Somebody’s child. Somebody’s husband. But her breath had shortened to scraps. The baby pushed hard beneath her ribs, alive, indignant, demanding space in a world that had suddenly become all elbows and doors and winter air.

Outside, the cold struck her face.

People spilled onto the synagogue steps and into the parking lot, stumbling over packed snow. No one seemed to know where to stand. Safety should have been farther away, but they stopped just beyond the doors, turning back, weeping, praying, searching for relatives. Sirens had not yet arrived, or perhaps they had and she could not hear them over the blood in her ears.

David guided her to a low wall near the entrance. “Sit.”

“I’m all right.”

“Sit.”

She sat. The stone’s cold came through her coat. Her hands shook in her lap, ridiculous hands, empty hands. She stared at them. Why had she thought she could reach him? What vanity, what foolishness. A pregnant woman waddling toward a gun with a word like sweetheart in her mouth. She would have been killed. The baby killed. David widowed before becoming a father. For what? To prove she had not run?

But she had run. Or been run. What was the difference? Her legs had moved away from the gun. Her body had chosen life with the dumb wisdom of animals.

David crouched in front of her. His face was gray. “Are you hurt?”

She looked at him carefully, as if he were someone who had asked from across a great distance. Hurt. What counted as hurt? No blood. No broken bone. The baby still moving, thank God, thank God, thank God.

“No.”

“You’re sure?”

She nodded, then began to cry without breath, silently at first, then with a harshness that embarrassed her even as it took her over. David held her, and she pressed her face to his shoulder. His coat smelled of snow and wool and him.

Around them the world resumed in grotesque pieces. A police car swung into the lot, then another. Men ran inside. Women called names. Someone recited Psalms loudly, stumbling over the words. A boy of about twelve vomited into a snowbank while his father rubbed his back. The synagogue doors opened and closed, opened and closed, releasing glimpses of men moving with purpose and others standing uselessly aside.

Barbara lifted her head. “Rabbi Adler?”

David did not answer.

Of course he did not know. Nobody knew. Or they knew and could not bear to say.

She thought of the rabbi’s hands folded on his desk, his silence after her father died. She thought of his voice only minutes before, steady as a bridge. Could a voice still be in the air after the body fell? It seemed to her that it must. The sanctuary was full of it, surely, caught among the rafters with the smoke of old prayers.

An ambulance arrived. Its red light turned the snow pink, then white, then pink again.

Barbara placed both hands on her belly. “Don’t come today,” she whispered.

David leaned close. “What?”

“Not today.”

The baby answered with a slow, turning pressure. Not a kick now. A shift, almost gentle.

The hours afterward became a corridor with missing doors. They were questioned briefly, told to go home, then told to wait, then told they could leave. At some point a woman Barbara did not know brought her a paper cup of water, and Barbara drank because the woman watched as if drinking were a moral obligation. At some point she realized she was still holding her prayer book. She must have clutched it all the way out. The cover was bent where her fingers had dug in.

In the car, David sat behind the wheel without starting the engine.

“Give me the keys,” she said.

He blinked.

“You’re shaking.”

“So are you.”

“Yes, but I’m always shaking now. I’m pregnant.”

He gave a laugh that broke in the middle. Then he put his forehead on the steering wheel. Barbara reached for the back of his neck. She had never seen him do that, fold like a boy. Men were allowed to cry at funerals, during illness, perhaps at births if joy overtook them, but not in parked cars under a blank Michigan sky. She stroked his hair.

He said, “I couldn’t get you out.”

“You did.”

“I pulled you—too hard maybe. I’m sorry.”

“You got us out.”

“You tried to go toward him.”

She looked through the windshield at the synagogue entrance. Policemen stood there now, dark coats against brick. The building had not changed. That was monstrous too. Walls should crack when such things happened. Stone should confess.

“I thought he might listen,” she said.

David turned his head. “Barbara.”

“I know.”

What did she know? That guns did not listen. That boys with guns did not become boys again because a woman asked nicely. That mercy was not magic. That she had almost mistaken her own shock for courage.

And yet, under that, stubborn as a root beneath frozen ground, another knowing remained: for one instant she had seen him not as monster but as a person falling. Was that wrong? Everyone would speak of the murderer. They would be right. A man lay shot because of him. A congregation was shattered because of him. Still, he had stood there with a face like the last room in a house after everyone had moved out.

Maybe compassion could arrive too late to change anything. Maybe it arrived anyway, useless and unwanted, because the heart did not consult the facts before opening.

David started the car.

At home, the house seemed indecently intact. The breakfast cup still in the sink. The newspaper on the table, folded to a story that already belonged to another century. Barbara took off her coat slowly. Her fingers had stiffened around the prayer book; David had to ease it from her hand.

“You should lie down,” he said.

“If I lie down, I’ll see it.”

“If you stand up, you’ll see it.”

That was true. She went upstairs.

In the bedroom, one boot zipper stuck again. She laughed then, a single ugly sound, and David knelt as he had that morning. Morning. It seemed impossible that the same man, the same boot, the same woman could exist on both sides of the day.

When the boots were off, she lay on her side facing the window. Snow had begun again, small flakes moving diagonally through the dusk. David lay behind her, one hand resting lightly on her belly. Neither spoke for a long time.

Inside her, the child slept or waited.

Barbara thought of telling this child someday. Not soon. Not as a bedtime story, not as family lore polished into meaning. But someday the child would ask about the world before he or she was born, and Barbara would have to decide what to say. You were there, she might say. You were almost born into terror but chose patience. Or: Your mother wanted to be brave and was carried away. Or: A good man died, and a lost one too, and the rest of us walked out under a winter sky not knowing why we had been spared.

None of these would be enough.

Downstairs, the telephone rang and rang, relatives calling, friends calling, the whole city beginning to speak in alarm. David rose to answer it. Barbara remained still.

She placed her hand where David’s had been. The baby shifted toward her touch.

All right, she thought. All right, little one. Come when you must. The world is terrible. The world is waiting.

And from somewhere deep within, beneath shock, beneath grief, beneath the cold imprint of the crowd carrying her away, she felt the smallest answering pressure, as if life itself had put out a hand in the dark and found hers.
