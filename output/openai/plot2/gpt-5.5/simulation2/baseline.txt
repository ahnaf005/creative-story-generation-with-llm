**The Space Between Breaths**

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara Rosen could not tie her own shoes.

She sat on the edge of the bed, breathless from the small effort of bending forward, while the winter light collected in pale squares across the floor. Her husband, Daniel, had already tied his tie twice and untied it once, unable to get the knot quite right. He was nervous, though he would not say so. He only frowned at the mirror and smoothed his dark hair with wet fingers.

“Come here,” Barbara said, holding out one foot.

Daniel turned. “You want me to—?”

“If you expect me to walk into synagogue barefoot, I suppose.”

He smiled then, the first real smile of the morning, and knelt before her. His fingers worked the laces of her black shoes carefully, as though they were made of thread so fine it might break. Barbara watched the crown of his head and felt the baby turn inside her, a slow rolling motion beneath her ribs.

“He’s awake,” she said.

“She,” Daniel corrected automatically.

Barbara leaned back on her palms. “You don’t know that.”

“I have instincts.”

“You have opinions.”

He tied the second shoe and looked up at her. “How do you feel?”

The question had become ritual by then. At eight and a half months pregnant, she felt like a house with all the furniture pushed against the walls. She felt too full of life and too short of breath. She felt heavy, awkward, impatient, astonished. But there was something else that morning too, something she could not name: a tightness under her breastbone, as if she had swallowed a small stone.

“I feel,” she said, “like everyone is going to stare at me.”

“They’re going to say you look beautiful.”

“They’re going to say I look enormous.”

“Beautiful and enormous.”

She laughed despite herself. Downstairs, her mother-in-law honked the car horn twice, though she had been told not to. Barbara pulled on her coat, a navy wool tent that no longer closed properly. Daniel wrapped her scarf around her neck, tucking it beneath her chin.

Outside, the cold struck cleanly. Snow lay in gray ridges along the curb, and the sky above Southfield was the flat white of unglazed porcelain. Detroit breathed smoke to the south; here, the streets were quieter, lined with new homes and thin young trees staked upright against the wind.

Daniel’s parents waited in the car. His mother, Miriam, turned from the front seat and exclaimed over Barbara’s face, her belly, her shoes, her courage. His father, Saul, drove with both hands on the wheel and the radio low, some orchestra murmuring beneath the heater’s rattle.

They were going to hear Rabbi Adler speak.

Everyone said his name with a certain warmth and gravity, as if placing a hand over the heart. Rabbi Adler had led them through losses and weddings, arguments over Israel, arguments over parking, arguments over what kind of cake should be served after services. He was learned, yes, but more than that, he made learning seem like a light left on in a dark room. Barbara had once heard him tell a boy preparing for his bar mitzvah, “God gave you a mind, so don’t insult Him by refusing to use it.” The boy had blushed; the adults had laughed; Barbara had remembered.

Congregation Shaarey Zedek rose broad and solid against the February morning, its stone walls pale beneath the clouds. Cars moved slowly into the lot. Men in dark coats hurried wives and children across the icy patches. The doors opened and closed, opened and closed, swallowing everyone into warmth.

Inside, the lobby smelled of wet wool, perfume, and floor polish. Voices rose and overlapped. Barbara was greeted by women she knew and women she only half-knew, all of whom placed a hand on her arm or shoulder and asked when the baby was due.

“Soon,” Barbara said each time.

“How soon?”

“When it decides.”

“You must be exhausted.”

“Only when I’m awake.”

They laughed, and Barbara smiled, though the tightness beneath her breastbone had not gone away. She placed one gloved hand on the curve of her belly. The baby had grown still.

In the sanctuary, light fell through tall windows in muted panels. The room was already filling. Daniel guided Barbara down the aisle, one hand hovering behind her back, not touching, ready. They found seats not far from the front because Miriam liked to hear every word clearly and Saul claimed the acoustics in back were “for people who don’t mind guessing.”

Barbara settled with effort. Her knees did not fit comfortably; her coat bunched beneath her. Daniel helped her arrange herself, whispering, “All right?”

She nodded.

The service began. Familiar prayers unfurled, the congregation rising and sitting as one body. Barbara moved a little more slowly than the rest, but she moved. Hebrew words surrounded her—ancient, worn smooth by use, yet alive each time they were spoken. She closed her eyes during one passage and imagined the baby hearing not words but rhythm, a tide of voices, the first music it might remember without knowing why.

Then Rabbi Adler stood to speak.

He was not a tall man, but he had presence, a way of inhabiting silence before filling it. His voice carried evenly, rich and measured. Barbara remembered only fragments later, though for years she tried to reconstruct the sermon whole. He spoke of responsibility. Of the bonds between generations. Of how no community was strong simply because its walls were strong, or because its members agreed with one another, or because its leaders were wise. A community was strong, he said, when each person understood that another’s life had been entrusted, in some measure, to him.

Barbara’s hand tightened on Daniel’s sleeve.

Entrusted.

The word stayed with her.

A movement at the side of the sanctuary drew her eye. A young man had risen from somewhere near the aisle. At first she thought he was ill, or perhaps leaving early. He looked too young to carry the darkness that seemed wrapped around him. His suit hung strangely on his frame. His face was pale, intent, almost emptied of expression.

Barbara saw Rabbi Adler turn toward him.

There are moments in life that do not arrive as moments. They split open and become rooms one wanders for the rest of one’s days. Barbara would remember the next seconds as sound before sight: a crack so sharp it seemed to tear the air itself. Then another. Screams rose before she understood why. People ducked, surged, froze. Someone shouted the rabbi’s name.

Daniel stood so fast his prayer book fell to the floor.

Barbara did not move. Her body refused. She saw the rabbi collapse—not in detail, not as in nightmares, but as a terrible lowering, a vanishing from where he had been. She saw men rush forward and then stumble back. She saw the young man with the gun.

He was close enough that she could see his hand shaking.

And then, in the strange stillness that lived inside the panic, Barbara thought: He is someone’s child.

Not monster. Not stranger. Not yet a word like murderer, though that word was already forming in the room around him. She saw a young man, lost beyond language, holding death in his hand and looking suddenly terrified by what he had done.

Barbara pushed herself up.

“Barbara!” Daniel grabbed her arm.

“He’s going to—” she began, but did not finish.

She did not know what she meant to do. She only knew, with an urgency stronger than fear, that someone had to reach him before the next shot, before the world closed around him completely. Perhaps she would speak. Perhaps she would say, “Give it to me.” Perhaps she would say his name, if she knew it. Perhaps, absurdly, she would put her hand on his sleeve the way women had touched her that morning, reminding him he was still flesh and could still be called back.

She stepped into the aisle.

The crowd broke.

It happened all at once. A wave of bodies rushed from behind her, people crying, pushing toward the exits, pulling children, stumbling over coats and prayer books. Daniel tried to hold her, but the force of the fleeing congregation wrenched them apart. A shoulder struck Barbara’s back. Someone slammed into her side. She wrapped both arms around her belly and cried out, not from pain exactly but from animal terror.

“I’m pregnant!” she shouted, though no one could hear.

She was carried backward, her feet barely finding the floor. The young man disappeared behind a blur of coats, hats, hands. A woman fell near the pews and was dragged up by two men. A child screamed for his father. Barbara fought to turn, to look, to reach—not because she was brave, she would insist later, but because the need had seized her and did not care whether she was brave.

Then came the final shot.

The sound ended the pushing for half a breath, as if the whole congregation inhaled and forgot how to exhale. Then the panic resumed, wilder for knowing it was too late.

Barbara found herself in the lobby, pressed against the wall beside a table of pamphlets. Her scarf had come loose. One shoe was untied. Daniel appeared with a face she did not recognize, white and stricken, and gathered her into his arms.

“Are you hurt? Barbara, are you hurt?”

She shook her head, but she was not sure. Her hands traveled over her belly, searching for the baby’s answer. For one long, unbearable moment there was nothing.

Then a kick. Firm, offended, alive.

Barbara began to sob.

Outside, sirens approached through the cold streets of Southfield. People stood in clusters on the steps and sidewalk, some hatless, some weeping openly, some silent with their mouths slightly open. Snow began to fall—not gracefully, but in small hard grains that ticked against coats and hair. Daniel kept one arm around Barbara and one hand pressed over his eyes.

Miriam said again and again, “The rabbi. The rabbi.” Saul stared at the doors as if waiting for someone to come out and announce that a mistake had been made.

No such announcement came.

In the days that followed, the city seemed to speak in whispers. The newspapers printed photographs. The telephone rang constantly. Women brought food Barbara could not eat. Men stood in the Rosen kitchen with cups of coffee gone cold, repeating details no one wanted and everyone needed to say aloud.

Barbara sat in the living room with her swollen feet propped on a cushion, listening to the muffled voices from the kitchen.

“You shouldn’t think about it,” Miriam told her.

“How do I stop?”

“For the baby.”

But the baby had been there. The baby had heard the shots, the screams, the prayers before and after. The baby had felt Barbara’s heart race like something trapped in a box. There was no keeping the child separate from the world now. Perhaps there never had been.

At night, Barbara woke with the urge to stand, to step into the aisle again. In dreams she reached the young man. In some dreams he handed her the gun and wept like a boy. In others she spoke and no sound came. Once she dreamed Rabbi Adler continued his sermon from the floor, voice calm, telling them all that responsibility did not end when terror began.

Two weeks later, Barbara went into labor during a rainstorm.

Daniel drove too fast to the hospital. Miriam prayed in the back seat. Barbara gripped the door handle and thought, irrationally, of the synagogue aisle, of being carried by a force larger than herself. Pain rose and fell. Between contractions she watched the windshield wipers slash at the rain and felt the baby moving downward, insisting on arrival.

Their daughter was born just before dawn, red-faced and furious, with a dark cap of hair and fists clenched as though prepared to argue with creation. When the nurse placed her on Barbara’s chest, the child quieted at once.

“What will you name her?” the nurse asked.

Barbara looked at Daniel. They had discussed names for months, none chosen. Family names, modern names, Hebrew names that sounded beautiful in the mouth. Now Daniel’s eyes were wet, and he nodded as though he already knew.

“Leah,” Barbara said.

It was not the rabbi’s name. It was not the name of anyone lost that day. It was simply a name that meant weary, some said, or delicate, or wild cow depending on who was doing the explaining. To Barbara it meant breathing. It meant a small warm body rising and falling against her own. It meant the world had not stopped, though it had cracked.

Years passed, as they do, not healing so much as layering. The synagogue remained. People returned because they had to, because worship required gathering, because grief alone becomes too heavy to carry. Rabbi Adler’s absence became part of the architecture: present in pauses, in memorial plaques, in stories told to children who had not known his voice.

Barbara returned too.

The first time, she stood in the lobby until Daniel asked if she wanted to leave.

“No,” she said.

Inside the sanctuary, she walked slowly down the aisle with baby Leah against her shoulder. Her legs trembled. She paused near the place where she had tried to step forward and had been swept back. For years she would pause there, even when Leah grew old enough to squirm from her arms and run ahead, even when another child came, a son this time, even when her own hair began to silver.

Sometimes people called her brave when they heard the story.

She disliked that.

Bravery, to her, suggested a clean decision, a noble posture, something polished enough to admire. What she remembered was confusion, fear, the smell of wet wool, the press of bodies, the terrible shaking of a young man’s hand. She remembered wanting to save everyone and saving no one. She remembered the baby kicking after the final shot, a private announcement: I am here.

On Leah’s sixteenth birthday, Barbara told her the whole story.

They sat at the kitchen table after the cake had been cut and the relatives had gone home. Leah was tall by then, serious-eyed, with Daniel’s quick smile and Barbara’s habit of twisting a napkin when thinking. She had heard pieces before, softened and scattered. That night she asked directly.

“Was I there?”

Barbara looked at her daughter across the table. Rain tapped the windows, gentle as fingertips.

“Yes,” she said. “You were there.”

Leah absorbed this, her face changing in small ways. “Were you scared?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know what to do?”

“No.”

“But you tried to go to him. The man with the gun.”

Barbara folded her hands over the old memory of her belly. “I tried to reach him.”

“Why?”

It was the question Barbara had asked herself for sixteen years. She had answered it differently depending on the season, the mood, the ache in her bones. Because she was foolish. Because she was pregnant. Because she did not understand. Because she understood too well. Because she had heard Rabbi Adler say entrusted, and the word had entered her blood.

At last she said, “Because for one second, I thought someone might still be able to call him back.”

Leah was quiet.

“And could they have?”

Barbara looked toward the dark window, where their kitchen appeared reflected: mother and daughter, table and chairs, the ordinary shelter of a home.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Most of life is not knowing. But I think we have to live as if calling back is possible.”

Leah reached across the table and took her hand.

Barbara held it tightly. Her daughter’s hand was warm, alive, strong. Outside, cars hissed along the wet street. Somewhere far off, a siren rose and faded, swallowed by the night.

And in the space between breaths, Barbara heard again the rabbi’s voice—not as a memory of death, but as something stubbornly living.

Entrusted.

She closed her eyes. Then she opened them.
