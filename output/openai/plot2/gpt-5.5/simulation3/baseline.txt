**The Day the Doors Opened**

Barbara had been told that a woman in her ninth month should not climb stairs, should not stand too long, should not go anywhere alone, and should most certainly not argue with her mother on the telephone before breakfast.

But on the morning of February 12, 1966, she did all of those things.

She woke before the winter light had reached the bedroom curtains, one hand resting on the hard swell of her belly, the other searching for the small flutter that had become her private greeting from the child inside her. The baby answered with a slow roll, like a fish turning beneath ice.

“Good morning to you too,” she whispered.

Beside her, David snored softly, one arm flung across his face. He had worked late at the pharmacy the night before, filling prescriptions in the blue fluorescent hum until near midnight. Barbara let him sleep. The synagogue was only a short drive, and she had promised Mrs. Rosen she would come early to help arrange prayer books for the special service.

“Help?” her mother had said when Barbara called the night before. “You are not helping anyone by giving birth in the coatroom.”

“I’m pregnant, Ma. I’m not made of glass.”

“You are made of stubbornness.”

This was true enough that Barbara had laughed.

By eight-thirty, she had dressed in her navy maternity suit, the one with the pearl buttons that made her feel less like a ship under full sail. She pinned her hat carefully, tied her boots, and took her good wool coat from the closet. Outside, Southfield lay under a pale February sky. The snow at the curbs had gone gray and crusted, and the bare branches along the street looked like black writing against the clouds.

In the car, the heater rattled and coughed before offering any warmth. Barbara drove slowly, both hands on the wheel, feeling the baby shift each time she turned. She passed brick ranch houses, tidy lawns buried beneath snow, and cars lined like beetles along driveways. The city was changing; everyone said so. Detroit spread and strained, and families moved north and west, carrying with them their recipes, their candlesticks, their arguments, their griefs.

The synagogue rose ahead of her, broad and modern, its clean lines softened by the winter morning. Barbara had loved it from the first time she saw it. It seemed to say that faith could live not only in old stone and dark wood, but also in glass, light, and new beginnings.

Inside, the air smelled of damp wool, floor polish, and faintly of the flowers someone had placed near the bimah. Voices echoed in the lobby. Men shook snow from their hats. Women unbuttoned coats and leaned close to kiss each other’s cheeks. Children darted between adults, sternly retrieved by mothers with gloved hands.

“Barbara!” Mrs. Rosen called, hurrying over. She was small, silver-haired, and always slightly breathless, as if life were a train she had just managed to catch. “You came.”

“I said I would.”

“And David?”

“Sleeping. If I woke him, he’d make me stay home.”

“A sensible man.” Mrs. Rosen eyed her belly. “How soon?”

“Any day, according to the doctor. According to me, yesterday.”

Mrs. Rosen laughed and took Barbara’s elbow. “Come then. Sit. No lifting.”

Barbara allowed herself to be guided, though she did not sit right away. She helped place a few prayer books along the rows, moving slowly between the seats. The sanctuary filled gradually, a murmur rising and falling like water. Sunlight came through the high windows, white and cold, laying bright rectangles on the carpet.

Near the front, Rabbi Adler stood speaking with two men, his head inclined, his expression attentive. Barbara had known him since she was a girl, though “known” was too large a word for the way congregants know a rabbi: through sermons, handshakes, hospital visits, weddings, funerals, and the brief but piercing moments when he seemed to see straight through ordinary politeness into the question beneath.

He had married Barbara and David beneath a canopy of white roses. He had visited her father in the hospital three weeks before he died. He had once stopped her after services when she was seventeen and angry at God for reasons she could not name.

“You may be angry,” he had told her gently. “God has heard worse from better people.”

She had never forgotten that.

Now he looked older than he had at her wedding, his face lined, his hair grayer, but his presence still filled the room without effort. He turned and saw her, and his face warmed.

“Barbara,” he said, coming toward her. “This child keeps good company, I see.”

She smiled. “I thought it was time he learned some manners.”

“He?”

“David says boy. My mother says girl. I say whichever one lets me sleep again.”

The rabbi laughed, a deep, quiet sound. He placed his hand lightly over hers, not touching her belly but acknowledging it, blessing it without display.

“May the child arrive in peace,” he said.

“In peace,” Barbara repeated.

Later she would remember those words so vividly that they would seem less like a blessing than a fragile object handed to her, one she had failed to keep from breaking.

The service began.

Barbara sat halfway down the aisle, on the left side, where she could stretch one leg a little when her back began to ache. Around her, pages turned. People rose and sat. The familiar prayers carried her along. She did not always know what she believed, but she knew the rhythm of standing with others, the ancient syllables passing from mouth to mouth. She knew the comfort of being one voice among many.

From time to time, the baby pressed against her ribs, and she shifted carefully. A boy in front of her, perhaps twelve, kept glancing back at her belly with frank curiosity until his older sister elbowed him.

Then came the moment that did not belong to any service.

At first, Barbara did not understand what she was seeing. A young man had risen near the front. He looked thin, almost delicate, in a dark suit that seemed too large at the shoulders. There was something strained about him, some terrible brightness in the way he held himself.

Voices faltered.

The rabbi turned.

The young man spoke, though Barbara could not catch the words. She saw faces change before she understood why—confusion tightening into alarm, alarm becoming disbelief. Then she saw the gun.

For one impossible second, no one moved.

Barbara’s first thought was absurdly practical: That cannot be real. Not here.

The sound cracked through the sanctuary.

It was louder than any sound she had ever heard, and yet afterward she would remember a strange silence opening around it, as though the world had inhaled and forgotten to breathe out. People screamed. Someone knocked over a chair. The rabbi staggered.

Barbara tried to stand, but her body, heavy and slow, resisted her. The baby lurched inside her as if startled awake.

Another shot.

The sanctuary broke apart.

People surged toward the doors, pushing, crying, stumbling over prayer books and one another’s feet. A man shouted, “Get down!” A woman screamed a name again and again. Barbara saw the young man turn the gun upon himself, saw the terrible collapse of his body, and still she thought: He is so young. Someone must go to him.

She did not know why that thought came. Perhaps because he had been standing alone. Perhaps because in his face, before the gun fired, she had seen not only violence but anguish, and anguish was a thing she had been taught one did not leave unattended. Perhaps because she was about to become a mother, and every young face had begun to look like someone’s child.

She moved toward him.

Or tried to.

“Stop!” someone cried.

Barbara pushed against the flow of bodies, one hand beneath her belly, the other reaching for the end of a pew. Her hat slipped over one eye. A shoulder struck her hard enough to spin her sideways. Pain flared low in her back.

“Please,” she said, though she did not know to whom. “Let me through.”

But the crowd had become a single frightened creature, blind with the need to escape. It caught her and carried her backward. Her feet barely found the floor. Someone’s elbow hit her arm. A prayer book tumbled open beneath her boot. She saw, in flashes, the bimah, the fallen rabbi, the young man on the floor, a dark shape against the carpet, and then she saw only coats, hands, mouths open in terror.

“No,” she whispered.

The doors burst wide. Cold air rushed in. Barbara was swept into the lobby, then against the wall near the coatroom. For a moment she could not breathe. Her body tightened around the baby in a hard band of pain.

A woman grabbed her. “Are you hurt?”

Barbara shook her head, though she was not sure.

“Sit down. Sit!”

“I have to—” Barbara began, but another wave of people pushed between them. “The rabbi—”

“Ambulance is coming,” someone said. “Police are coming.”

“I tried,” Barbara said.

No one heard her.

She found herself outside without remembering how she got there. Snow squeaked beneath her boots. People stood in clusters in the parking lot, coatless, weeping, staring back at the building as if it had become unrecognizable. A man held his hat in both hands and twisted the brim until it bent. A teenage girl sobbed into her father’s overcoat. Sirens rose in the distance, thin at first, then swelling.

Barbara leaned against a car that was not hers and pressed both hands to her belly.

“Stay with me,” she whispered to the baby. “Stay quiet. Stay safe.”

The baby did not move.

Panic slid cold beneath her ribs.

“Please,” she said.

At last there came one small answering push, firm and indignant.

Barbara began to cry.

The police arrived, then ambulances, then more police. Questions moved through the crowd before answers could catch them. Was the rabbi alive? Who was the young man? Why had he done it? Was anyone else hurt? The questions struck Barbara like sleet. She wanted David. She wanted her mother. She wanted to wake up in bed with the pale light behind the curtains and the whole day not yet chosen.

A policeman asked if she had seen anything. His face was kind but tired already, as though the day had aged him in minutes.

“I saw him stand,” Barbara said. “I saw the gun.”

“Did he say anything?”

“I couldn’t hear.”

“Do you know him?”

She closed her eyes, trying to place the young man’s face among the many faces that passed through synagogue life—boys grown taller, college students returned for holidays, sons of friends of friends.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Maybe. I don’t know.”

The policeman looked at her belly and softened. “You should go home, ma’am.”

Home. The word seemed to come from another language.

Mrs. Rosen found her near the curb and insisted on driving her. Barbara did not argue. In the car, neither woman spoke for several blocks. The heater blew weakly against the windshield. Mrs. Rosen’s hands trembled on the steering wheel.

Finally Barbara said, “I tried to go to him.”

“To the rabbi?”

Barbara looked down. “To the boy.”

Mrs. Rosen made a small sound, half sob, half protest. “Barbara.”

“He was just lying there.”

“He had a gun.”

“He was somebody’s son.”

Mrs. Rosen’s mouth tightened, and tears ran silently down her cheeks. “So was Isaac,” she said.

Barbara understood. Mrs. Rosen’s son had died in Korea at nineteen. For the rest of the drive, they said nothing.

David was on the porch before the car fully stopped. Somehow the news had outrun her. He pulled open the passenger door and gathered her awkwardly, desperately, as if he could wrap his arms around both her and the unborn child and hold them in the world by force.

“I’m all right,” she said into his coat.

“You’re shaking.”

“I’m all right.”

He brought her inside. Her mother arrived twenty minutes later, hair unpinned beneath a scarf, face white with fear. She scolded, prayed, wept, made tea, and called the doctor, in that order. The doctor said rest, watch for pains, come in if anything changed.

Everything had changed, Barbara thought, but she lay down as instructed.

That night she did not sleep. Each time she closed her eyes, she saw the young man standing in the aisle. She heard the crack of the gun. She felt the crowd taking her, carrying her away from what she had decided, however foolishly, was hers to do.

Near dawn, David woke and found her sitting by the window.

“You should be in bed,” he murmured.

“I keep thinking, if I had reached him—”

“No.”

“You don’t know what I was going to say.”

“Yes, I do.” He sat beside her, his face hollow with exhaustion. “And no.”

“He was alive for a moment. I think he was. Maybe I could have—”

“Barbara, you could have been killed.”

She placed both hands on her belly. “I know.”

“Then why?”

She watched the first gray light gather over the street. Across the road, the Feldmans’ porch lamp glowed above the snow.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Because everyone was running away. Because someone should have gone toward him. Because I am bringing a child into this world, and I need to believe that when someone is broken, someone else tries to reach them before the end.”

David said nothing. After a moment, he took her hand.

Two weeks later, their daughter was born during a snowstorm.

Labor began before supper with a pain Barbara mistook for indigestion and ended near midnight in a hospital room bright as noon. When the nurse placed the baby in her arms, Barbara stared in astonishment. The child’s face was red and furious, her fists clenched, her dark hair damp against her head.

“A girl,” David said, laughing and crying at once.

“I see that,” Barbara whispered.

They named her Leah Miriam. Leah for David’s grandmother, Miriam because Barbara liked the way it sounded like water over stones. But privately, in the hidden chamber of her heart, Barbara also thought of the name as a promise: that her daughter would be raised among songs, not gunfire; questions, not silence; courage, not the kind that rushed blindly into danger, but the kind that refused to let fear have the final word.

The synagogue mourned. The city mourned. Men spoke solemnly on television. Newspapers printed photographs and explanations that explained nothing. People argued in kitchens and parking lots about madness, politics, faith, security, forgiveness. Some said the young man’s name with anger. Others with pity. Most with both, though they did not admit it.

Barbara returned to the synagogue in spring, carrying Leah in a white blanket.

The first time she stepped into the sanctuary, her knees weakened. The room looked the same and not the same. Light still entered through the high windows. The seats still lined themselves in patient rows. Prayer books rested where hands could find them. Yet Barbara saw, beneath the restored order, the memory of chaos. She wondered if everyone did.

Mrs. Rosen came over and touched the baby’s cheek.

“She’s beautiful,” she said.

“She’s hungry,” Barbara replied, and they both laughed more loudly than the joke deserved.

During the service, Leah slept against Barbara’s shoulder, warm and heavy and alive. Barbara stood when the congregation stood. She sang when they sang. Her voice shook at first, then steadied.

At one point, she looked toward the front and saw again the place where the rabbi had fallen, the place where the young man had stood, the impossible distance between them. She knew now that she could not have crossed it. Not through the crowd, not through time, not through whatever darkness had opened before any of them arrived that morning.

But she also knew this: she had tried.

It was not enough to save anyone. It did not alter the fact of blood on a holy floor. It did not answer the questions that would follow her for years.

Still, as her daughter stirred and sighed against her neck, Barbara decided that trying was not nothing.

Outside, the last snow of winter had begun to melt. Water ran along the curb in bright threads, carrying bits of salt and old leaves toward the drains. The synagogue doors stood open after the service, and people lingered there in the mild air, reluctant to leave, reluctant perhaps to turn their backs.

Barbara paused on the steps with Leah in her arms.

Behind her, voices rose and mingled—grief, gossip, blessing, complaint, laughter. Ahead of her lay the parking lot, the wet pavement shining under a pale sun. She looked down at her daughter, whose tiny mouth moved in sleep as if practicing words.

“What shall we tell you?” Barbara whispered.

The baby gave no answer.

So Barbara began with the only truth she could bear.

“We will tell you,” she said softly, “that the world can break. And we will tell you that when it does, you must look for the people who reach.”

Then she carried her child into the light.
