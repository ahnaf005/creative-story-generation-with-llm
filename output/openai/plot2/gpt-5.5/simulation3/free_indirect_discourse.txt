On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara could not find her blue gloves.

They were not in the drawer by the front door, where gloves belonged if people in a household had any sense. They were not tucked into the sleeve of her good coat, as she had hoped, nor abandoned in the car, nor hidden beneath the newspapers Morton kept meaning to take down to the basement. She stood in the little vestibule of their house in Southfield, one hand pressed to the shelf of her belly, and felt the baby turn as if impatient with the whole business.

Yes, yes, she was coming. Everyone was coming. The whole world, it seemed, was waiting on Barbara and her missing gloves.

From the kitchen Morton called, “We’ll be late.”

He said it mildly, because he knew better than to sound accusing when she was eight months pregnant and already sweating under her hat. Still, she made a face in the mirror. We’ll be late. As if she had misplaced time itself between the scarves and the umbrellas.

At last she found the gloves in her purse, of course. She must have put them there the night before, showing remarkable foresight, and then forgotten she was a woman of such intelligence. She pulled them on finger by finger and followed Morton out into the clean, hard cold.

Snow lay in gray ridges along the curb. The morning light was white, not warm, and the bare trees looked drawn in pencil against the sky. Southfield still seemed unfinished to Barbara, half suburb, half promise—new ranch houses, muddy lots, shopping centers, roads widened faster than memory could follow. Detroit was behind them but not gone; it followed in accents, recipes, parents, obligations, the old neighborhoods people spoke of as if they had been swallowed by history rather than left by choice.

In the car, Morton drove carefully over patches of ice. Barbara sat beside him, breathing shallowly, both hands on her stomach. The baby had been restless all morning. A boy, her mother insisted, because of how low she carried. A girl, Morton said, because he wanted the pleasure of being contrary. Barbara herself imagined no face, only a presence: heel, elbow, roll, mystery. Someone knocking from the inside.

At the synagogue, cars filled the lot and spilled into every available space. Congregation Shaarey Zedek rose ahead of them in its modern angles, so different from the older sanctuaries of Barbara’s childhood that she still sometimes expected to enter and find the wrong congregation inside. But there were familiar coats, familiar hats, the same perfume of damp wool and face powder, the same murmured greetings: Good Shabbos, how are you feeling, any day now, you poor thing, you look wonderful.

Wonderful. She looked like a sofa in a hat.

Morton offered his arm as they walked in. She accepted it, though she did not need it. Or needed it only a little. There was no shame in needing a little.

Inside, warmth closed around them. The sanctuary shimmered with voices settling, prayer books lifted, pages turned. Barbara loved this interval before the service truly took hold, when people were still people and not yet a congregation: men clearing throats, women sliding purses beneath seats, children being shushed with no conviction. She and Morton found places not far from the aisle. Good, she thought. If she had to get up, she could do so without performing an entire apology ballet.

Rabbi Adler stood at the front, dignified and calm, his presence filling the space before he even spoke. Barbara had heard him many times. Everyone had. He had a voice that seemed to gather people toward their better selves. It did not flatter them; it expected something. When he spoke of justice, of obligation, of the world beyond their polished shoes and Saturday lunches, Barbara sometimes felt both uplifted and indicted. Fine, Rabbi, she would think, but first let her get through the laundry.

That morning his voice rolled through the sanctuary like warm water over stone.

The baby quieted.

There, Barbara thought. Even you know.

She shifted in her seat and pressed two fingers beneath her ribs, easing a stitch. Around her, heads tilted toward the pulpit. Morton’s profile was serious, attentive. He had the look he wore when he wanted to remember something exactly, as though later he might be called upon to repeat the rabbi’s words at dinner. Barbara wondered what they would eat after services. There was chicken salad at home. Rye bread if Morton had not finished it. Pickles. She would need a nap, absolutely, no visitors unless they came bearing cake and left quickly.

A young man rose.

At first she noticed him only because movement drew the eye. He was several rows ahead, slender, in a dark suit that did not sit quite right on him. He stepped into the aisle with a stiff purpose, not the embarrassed shuffle of someone going out to cough or take a child to the restroom. Barbara frowned. Did she know him? His head was slightly bowed, his shoulders set. There was something terribly private about him, though he stood in public.

He moved toward the front.

Perhaps he had a question. No, one did not interrupt in that way. Perhaps he was ill. Barbara straightened, the way women did when someone might faint—already preparing to locate water, a chair, someone’s mother.

Then she saw the gun.

For one suspended second, the object made no sense. It belonged to another kind of story, one from newspapers folded beside coffee cups: gas station robberies, arguments in bars, men in alleys, far away places. Not here. Not between the prayer books and the ark. Not in a young man’s hand beneath the high ceiling of the sanctuary.

A sound cracked the air.

Barbara did not understand it as a shot until people screamed.

The rabbi staggered.

No. That was the first clear word inside her. Not spoken, not even thought exactly, but existing whole and solid as a stone. No.

Another terrible noise, or maybe the echo of the first, rang in her ears. The sanctuary, which had been a vessel for prayer, broke open. People rose all at once. Prayer books fell. A woman cried out in a voice so raw Barbara felt it in her teeth. Someone shouted, “Get down!” Someone else, “Call a doctor!” Chairs scraped, bodies lurched, the aisle vanished.

The young man stood there with the gun.

He looked astonishingly young. That was what Barbara saw. Not monstrous, not even angry in any ordinary way. Young. Pale. Lost and rigid, as if the act had carried him to a place he had not expected and now he did not know where to put his feet.

Barbara’s body began moving before she decided anything.

She stepped toward the aisle.

Morton seized her wrist. “Barbara!”

She pulled against him. She did not know what she meant to do. Take the gun? Speak to him? Put herself between? How absurd. She was heavy and slow and could barely bend to tie her shoes. But someone had to reach him, because he was still standing there, because the gun was still in his hand, because if one person could get close enough and say—say what? Sweetheart? Stop? Look at me? Because he looked as if he might hear one human voice if it came near enough.

“Barbara, no!”

Morton’s grip tightened, but then the crowd surged.

A wave of bodies struck from behind and from the side. Her hand slipped from Morton’s. Someone’s elbow caught her shoulder. A man stumbled into her, apologized without seeing her, pushed on. She tried to widen her stance, to protect the baby, but the floor seemed to tilt beneath the force of everyone’s fear.

Don’t fall.

That was all now. Not the rabbi, not the young man, not the impossible gun. Don’t fall. Don’t let them knock you down. The baby. Her hands flew to her belly, making a poor shield of fingers and wool.

She called Morton’s name, but the sound disappeared.

The aisle carried her backward. No, not carried—tore. She was torn away from the front, away from the young man, away from the fallen figure she could not bear to look at and could not stop seeing. Coats pressed against her face. A woman’s hat veil scratched her cheek. Someone sobbed, “Oh God, oh God,” and someone else shouted for people to move. The sanctuary doors widened ahead like a mouth, and the crowd pushed through.

Barbara tried to turn, but there was no turning in a river.

The baby kicked.

All right, all right, she told it. I know. I am trying.

In the lobby the crush loosened enough for her to catch the edge of a table. Announcements fluttered beneath her gloved hand. Sisterhood luncheon, adult education, youth program, normal life printed neatly in black ink. She clung to the table and gasped. Her hat had slipped over one ear. Her coat was open. One glove was gone.

Where was Morton?

People streamed around her. Some ran outside into the cold without coats buttoned; some huddled against walls; some tried to go back and were held by others. Names flew past. Doctor. Police. Rabbi. Mother. A child wailed with the indignant terror of one who had been promised safety by every adult in his world and now saw the lie.

Barbara pushed herself upright.

She had to go back in. The thought arrived plainly. She had meant to reach him, and she had not. She had been swept away like a leaf, like luggage, like a woman too pregnant to be useful. She had to go back.

But when she moved toward the sanctuary doors, a man blocked her. Mr. Levitt? Mr. Levin? His face was gray.

“You can’t go in there,” he said.

“My husband—”

“They’re bringing people out. Stand back.”

“My husband is in there.”

He looked at her belly then, and his expression changed into that unbearable gentleness people used when deciding for her. “Please. Sit down.”

She hated him for being kind.

Another sound came from inside.

Not as loud as the first in her memory, perhaps not even loud at all beneath the shouting, but she knew. Everyone in the lobby seemed to flinch together. For one heartbeat, no one moved.

The young man had turned the gun on himself.

Barbara understood this before anyone told her. She saw again his pale face, the stunned set of his mouth. She had not reached him. She had been five yards away, ten, a lifetime. If she had pulled free sooner. If Morton had not grabbed her. If the crowd had not—no, what nonsense. What arrogance to think she could have stepped into that distance and altered the bullet’s path with a word. But another part of her, deeper and more stubborn, refused. A person was alive, and then there was a moment, and in that moment another person might enter. Was that not what life was? Entering in time?

Morton found her by the table.

His glasses were crooked. His face, usually so composed, had collapsed into naked fear. He took her shoulders, then her face, then her hands, as if counting her.

“Are you hurt?”

She shook her head.

“The baby?”

She nodded, then shook her head, then began to cry because neither answer was right. Morton pulled her against him. His heart hammered through his coat. She pressed her forehead to his scarf and breathed in cold air, wool, aftershave, husband. Alive. He was alive. She was alive. The baby shifted between them, insistently alive.

Around them, the lobby filled with sirens before the sirens arrived. People spoke in fragments. The rabbi was down. A doctor had gone to him. Someone knew the shooter. Someone did not. Someone had seen everything. Someone had seen nothing. The mind, desperate, began its ugly work of arranging horror into sentences.

Barbara sat eventually on a bench near the coatroom. She had no memory of agreeing to sit. Morton crouched before her, one hand on her knee, looking at her as if she might vanish. A woman she barely knew brought water in a paper cup. Barbara drank and tasted dust.

Her missing glove lay on the floor by the wall, trampled, palm up.

She stared at it.

That morning she had searched the house for that ridiculous glove while the day waited with its mouth full of fire. She had worried about being late, about chicken salad, about looking like a sofa. The smallness of those thoughts should have comforted her—life had been ordinary because ordinary life was what people had before anything happened. Instead it made her feel betrayed. The world had allowed her to fuss over gloves while a young man put a gun in his pocket and came to synagogue.

Did he sit at breakfast? Did someone say good morning to him? Did he button his coat carefully? Did he pass other cars on the road, stop at lights, breathe into his hands against the cold? Somewhere, before the sanctuary, he had been merely walking. There had been time then. So much time, surely. And yet no one had entered it.

A police officer asked questions. Morton answered most of them. Yes, they had been seated near the aisle. No, they did not know the young man personally. Yes, Barbara had seen the gun. No, she could not describe much more. Dark suit. Young. Pale. His hand. The sound.

The officer’s pencil moved.

Barbara wanted to tell him that the young man had looked lonely. This seemed important and also useless, possibly offensive, possibly unforgivable. A lonely person could still pull a trigger. A dead rabbi was no less dead for the loneliness of the hand that killed him.

She said nothing.

Later, someone drove them home. Morton’s hands shook too badly, though he insisted he was fine until Barbara looked at him and he gave the keys away. The afternoon sun flashed on dirty snowbanks. The streets of Southfield seemed indecently calm. Houses stood with curtains open; children’s bicycles lay on lawns rimmed with ice; smoke rose from chimneys. At one corner a woman carried groceries from her car, a paper bag braced against her hip. Barbara watched her as if from underwater. How could the woman not know? How could anyone be carrying oranges?

At home, Morton helped her off with her coat. The foyer was just as they had left it: newspapers stacked, umbrella stand crooked, the drawer by the door still half open from the search for gloves. Barbara removed her one remaining glove and placed it inside. Then she took it out again and laid it on the table. It could not be paired now. It would remain a witness to something, though to what she could not say.

She went to the bedroom and sat on the edge of the bed.

For a long while, Morton moved quietly through the house, making calls, answering calls, saying the same few words in different orders. Yes. We were there. She’s all right. The baby is all right. No, we don’t know. Yes, terrible. Terrible.

Terrible was too small a word. It sat in the mouth like a button.

Barbara removed her hat. Pins fell into her lap. Her hair ached where they had held it. She looked down at her belly, round and firm beneath the dark maternity dress, and placed both hands on it.

The baby was still.

Panic rose so fast she nearly called out, but then came a slow roll beneath her palms. Not a kick. Not alarm. A turning, deliberate and secret.

“Oh,” she whispered.

The child did not know about guns, rabbis, sanctuaries, blood, widows, sirens, men too young to be so lost. The child knew only warmth and rhythm, the thunder of Barbara’s heart, the muffled world beyond. Soon enough it would learn. That was the cruelty. That was the promise. Children entered a world already broken and expected milk.

She thought of Rabbi Adler’s voice before the shot, steady and summoning. She could not recall his exact words. This seemed another failure. Surely if a man’s final sermon had been interrupted by violence, those who heard it owed him remembrance. But memory would not obey. She could summon the timbre, the rise and fall, the sense of being asked to become better than she was. Not the words.

Maybe the asking was enough.

That evening, when the phone finally quieted, Morton came into the bedroom. He looked older by years. He sat beside her without speaking. For a while they listened to the furnace click on, the low rush of heat through vents, the house doing its ordinary best against the cold.

“I shouldn’t have let go of you,” he said.

She turned to him. “You didn’t.”

“I did.”

“The crowd—”

“I had your hand.”

She wanted to comfort him, but comfort felt like thin cloth over a wound. “I tried to go toward him,” she said.

Morton closed his eyes. “I know.”

“You stopped me.”

“I know.”

“I was angry.”

“I know.”

She looked at his hands, folded now, helpless. The same hands that opened jars, balanced checkbooks, rubbed her back when she could not sleep. He had held her because he loved her. He had held her because a gun was a gun and she was carrying their child. He had held her because sometimes love was a restraint. She had pulled away because something in her believed even then that terror could be met, that a person walking toward death might be called back by name.

Both things were true. They sat between them on the bed, irreconcilable and married.

“I wouldn’t have reached him,” she said, not knowing whether she believed it.

Morton took her hand. “Thank God you didn’t.”

She almost said, Thank God? Is that what we are saying today? But his fingers trembled around hers, and she let the words pass.

Weeks later, when their daughter was born in a rush of pain and astonishment, Barbara would remember the sanctuary. Not at first. At first there would be only the animal work of labor, Morton’s pale face, a nurse telling her to breathe as if breathing were an idea she had not considered. Then the baby would cry—a furious, ragged sound—and Barbara would be back for an instant in that lobby full of screams.

But this cry meant arrival.

They placed the child on her chest, slick and furious, fists clenched as if already objecting to the terms of existence. Barbara laughed and sobbed together. Here she was, then. Here was the one who had turned beneath gunfire, who had floated in darkness while the world broke open, who had insisted afterward with a slow roll of the body: still here.

“What should we name her?” Morton asked, though they had discussed names for months.

Barbara looked at the baby’s face, crumpled and magnificent.

She thought of the rabbi’s voice, of the young man’s pale bewilderment, of the lost glove, of the table beneath her hand in the lobby, of being swept away when she had tried to move forward. She thought of time—how it opened, how it closed, how sometimes no one entered soon enough.

“Hope,” she said.

Morton blinked. It had not been on their list.

Barbara touched the baby’s damp hair. Hope was not prettiness. Hope was not denial. Hope was the hand reaching before it knew whether it could help. Hope was the child breathing against her skin in a world where sanctuaries were not always safe, and still, somehow, sanctuaries were built.

“Yes,” Morton said softly. “Hope.”

Outside the hospital window, late winter pressed its gray face to the glass. Inside, the baby rooted blindly, angrily, alive. Barbara gathered her close and felt the fierce, impossible weight of her.
