On the last Friday of the residency, the van would not start.

It sat in the gravel lot behind the studios, nine seats, one bald tire, and the smell of old sunscreen baked into its upholstery. Everyone else had already left for the airport or the train station, dragging suitcases over the courtyard stones, promising visits they all knew would dissolve into email threads and birthday emojis. Only Mara and Theo remained, each with one more night in the converted convent, one more breakfast of underripe melon, one more chance to pretend they were not counting down.

Theo turned the key again. The van coughed.

Mara leaned against the open passenger door. “It’s a sign.”

“You believe in signs?”

“I believe in bad maintenance.”

He laughed, and she looked away too quickly, toward the field where the dry grass combed itself in the wind. For six weeks they had done this: reached the edge of something, then stepped back. A hand passing a mug. A conversation after midnight in the laundry room. His shoulder brushing hers as they read the noticeboard, both of them staying very still.

The beach was forty minutes away. They had talked about going since the first week, when they were still strangers comparing stipends and allergies. Somehow there had always been a lecture, a storm, a group dinner, a deadline. Now the day stretched before them bright and final, and the van refused to move.

“We could walk to the bus stop,” Mara said.

Theo checked his phone. “Next bus is in an hour.”

“Perfect. More time for misfortune.”

They packed badly: two towels, a dented thermos of coffee, cherries stolen from the dining hall, and a paperback neither of them would read. At the bus stop, an elderly man told them the coastal bus no longer came on Fridays, or perhaps only came on Fridays; his confidence shifted with every sentence. When the bus finally arrived, it was not the right bus, but the driver said he could get them “near enough,” which turned out to mean beside a shuttered aquarium three miles from the sea.

They walked.

The road gave way to a path through scrub pine and sand. Mara’s sandal broke first. Theo crouched and fixed it with the red string from his residency badge, tying the sole to her foot with grave concentration.

“You’re very handy for a poet,” she said.

“I’m not a poet.”

“You write poems.”

“I arrange failures in lines.”

She smiled, then didn’t. “That’s all anyone does.”

They continued more slowly, Mara’s foot flapping faintly with each step. The sky had gone white at the edges. Somewhere beyond the dunes, gulls screamed like people discovering betrayal.

At last the beach appeared below them: a long pale curve, nearly empty, the water dark blue and restless. Mara stopped at the top of the dune. The wind lifted her hair from her neck. Theo wanted, suddenly and with painful simplicity, to remember her exactly like that—squinting into brightness, one broken shoe, her mouth soft with surprise.

“We made it,” he said.

“Don’t sound so tragic.”

“I don’t know how else to sound.”

They ran down the dune like children and immediately sank ankle-deep in hot sand. The thermos leaked in Theo’s bag. The cherries had bruised into a sticky red pulp. A seagull stole half of Mara’s sandwich while she was unwrapping it, and she shouted after it with such wounded dignity that Theo fell backward laughing.

“Don’t laugh,” she said, laughing too. “That bird knew what it was doing.”

“The birds here are funded better than we are.”

They spread the towels close together, not touching. The sea worried the shore. Far off, a family packed up a striped umbrella; a man with a metal detector moved in patient arcs. Mara took the paperback from the bag, opened it, closed it again.

“What time is your train tomorrow?” Theo asked.

She picked sand from her palm. “Ten eighteen.”

He nodded. His flight was at noon, though he hadn’t told her. It felt crude to discuss the mechanics of disappearance.

“And then?” he said.

“And then Berlin for a month. Maybe longer. A friend has a room. Maybe I’ll finish the project. Maybe I’ll become unbearable.”

“You’re already unbearable.”

“That’s kind of you.”

He looked at the water. “I’m going back to Chicago.”

“I know.”

Of course she knew. Everyone knew everything at residencies—who snored, who cried after calls home, who was sleeping badly, who never mentioned a partner until the third glass of wine.

The wind blew sand across their ankles in a thin veil.

Mara said, “We’re very good at almost saying things.”

Theo’s throat tightened. “Are we?”

“Yes.”

He waited for her to go on. She didn’t.

Instead, she stood and walked toward the water. At the edge, she turned, challenging him without expression. He followed. The first wave struck cold enough to hurt. Mara gasped and grabbed his wrist, just for balance, just because the sand was pulling away under them. Her fingers stayed there.

For a moment they faced the sea together, braced against the undertow.

“I don’t want to make this heavier than it is,” Theo said.

Mara’s hand tightened, then loosened. “Maybe it’s already exactly as heavy as it is.”

The wave withdrew. Their feet sank deeper.

He could have kissed her then. She knew it; he knew she knew it. The knowledge passed between them with the bright cruelty of sunlight. But behind it waited morning: taxis, boarding passes, separate weather. A kiss would not solve that. It would only become another room they had to leave.

Mara let go first.

They swam until they were breathless and numb. On the way back, the sky turned gray with theatrical suddenness, and rain began before they reached the aquarium road. They huddled under the awning of a closed bait shop, soaked and shivering, eating the ruined cherries with red fingers.

“You look like you murdered someone,” Theo said.

“I did,” Mara replied. “A version of myself who made better choices.”

The bus home was late and nearly empty. Mara fell asleep against the window, her head knocking softly with each turn. After a while, without waking, she leaned toward him until her temple rested on his shoulder.

Theo sat very still.

Outside, the coast slipped away into fields, then warehouses, then the familiar hill of the residency with its bell tower and darkening windows. He did not move until the bus sighed to a stop.

That night, in the kitchen, they made tea neither of them wanted. Their wet towels hung over chair backs. Someone had left a note on the refrigerator: SAFE TRAVELS, EVERYONE. SEE YOU IN THE WORLD.

Mara read it and made a small sound—not quite a laugh.

“What?” Theo asked.

“Nothing. Just—‘the world.’ As if we haven’t been in it.”

He wanted to say: I will miss you. He wanted to say: Come to Chicago. He wanted to say: If you asked me, I would change something, though he did not know what, and suspected she would dislike him for offering.

Mara turned to him. “Today was good.”

“It was a disaster.”

“I know.”

They stood in the blue kitchen light, listening to the old refrigerator shudder and hum. Then she stepped forward and pressed her damp forehead briefly against his chest. His arms rose around her, careful at first, then not. They held each other with the solemnity of people who had run out of tricks.

When she pulled away, she did not apologize. Neither did he.

In the morning, her door was open. The bed was stripped. On his desk lay the red string from his badge, untied and coiled beside a paper cup of coffee gone cold.

At the train station, hours later, Mara found sand still caught in the cuff of her jeans. She shook it loose onto the platform and watched it scatter between the tracks.

For weeks afterward, Theo would discover grains of it too—in his suitcase, in a book, in the seam of his bag—small, impossible remainders of a place they had barely reached, and left before they could name it.
