On the last Friday of the residency, Mara woke before the gulls and lay listening to the old house decide whether or not to collapse.

Pipes knocked. Someone’s suitcase zipper worried itself open and shut down the hall. In the room below, Leni was laughing at something, that half-stifled laugh of hers, as if joy were a liquid she was trying not to spill. Mara stared at the ceiling, where water stains had gathered in the shape of a continent no one could name. Three weeks ago, she had arrived with two notebooks, one good dress, and the serene ambition to be transformed. Now she had seven pages of a failed essay, a sunburn on one shoulder, and the embarrassing knowledge of how Leni took her coffee.

Black, unless the day was already ruined. Then with sugar.

Today they were going to the beach. The final outing. A harmless group excursion, according to the director, whose ideas of harmlessness included communal critique and lentils in every meal. They would pack towels, leftover fruit, bottles of warm wine, and drive to the coast in the residency van, which smelled of wet dog though no dog had ever been permitted in it. Tomorrow everyone would leave: Berlin, Montreal, Tucson, Lisbon. Leni would fly east. Mara would take the train south and return to the apartment where her plants had either survived or not, her inbox had multiplied, and no one knew about the particular catastrophe of wanting a person who would soon become an email address.

She got up.

In the kitchen, the others were assembling provisions with the incompetence of artists pretending at practicality. Theo had bought three bags of ice and no cooler. Priya was cutting peaches with a bread knife. Someone had already opened the wine.

Leni stood barefoot by the sink, rinsing grapes, her dark hair pinned carelessly up with a pencil Mara recognized from the studio table. Mara’s pencil, probably. Or no, not hers. What kind of person noticed a pencil in another woman’s hair and felt accused by it?

“Morning,” Leni said.

“Morning.”

“You look like you slept.”

A dangerous observation. “You look like you didn’t.”

“I was packing.”

Of course. Packing. That obscene little word. Mara reached for a mug and found none, because this house contained seventeen residents and four mugs, all chipped, all beloved beyond reason.

Leni handed her one. “I saved you the good one.”

It was the least broken one, with a faded drawing of a lighthouse. Mara accepted it as if it were not a declaration of war.

The van ride began badly and deteriorated from there. Theo insisted he knew a shortcut because he had “a coastal intuition.” Half an hour later they were circling the same roundabout for the third time while Priya read directions from her phone in a voice increasingly detached from life.

“You missed the exit,” Leni said from the seat behind Mara.

“I did not miss it,” Theo said. “The exit moved.”

Mara kept her gaze on the window, where fields flashed by, green and indifferent. Leni’s knee had been pressing into the back of her seat since they left. Not intentionally. Probably not. Every turn, every bump in the road translated itself through vinyl and foam into Mara’s spine. A private Morse code. Ridiculous. She was thirty-two years old and being undone by upholstery.

When they finally reached the coast, a fog had rolled in so thick the sea announced itself before it appeared: a low animal breathing beyond the dunes. Everyone tumbled out of the van with theatrical relief. Theo discovered he had left his towel at the house. Priya discovered the peaches had leaked syrup over her sketchbook. Leni discovered she had lost one sandal.

“How do you lose one sandal while wearing it?” Mara asked.

“It’s a gift,” Leni said, hopping in the gravel lot.

They searched under the seats, among tote bags and sandy floor mats. No sandal. Leni shrugged and set off toward the dunes barefoot, one-handedly carrying the remaining sandal like a philosophical argument she no longer wished to pursue.

Mara should have found this irritating. Instead she loved her a little more, which was inconvenient and unsanitary.

The beach emerged in fragments: a strip of wet sand, a few gulls arranged like punctuation, the horizon erased. It was colder than anyone had planned. They laid out towels anyway and pretended to sunbathe under a sky the color of dishwater. The wine was opened. The grapes were sandy within minutes. Someone produced a speaker, which died after one triumphant chord.

Perfect, Mara thought. Absolutely perfect. A final memory assembled from minor failures. Nothing so grand as heartbreak. Just grit between teeth, gooseflesh, a missing shoe.

Leni sat beside her on the towel, knees drawn up, chin resting on them. Her bare toes were already pink from the cold.

“You’re quiet,” she said.

“I’m storing up remarks for later.”

“Efficient.”

“I may publish them posthumously.”

Leni smiled, but not fully. There it was again, that small reserve, the door left nearly closed. For the first half of the residency she had seemed incapable of reserve. She had talked through meals, through walks, through rainstorms, telling stories about her grandmother’s apartment in Kraków, the anatomy of bad translations, the time she’d accidentally joined a protest march while looking for a bakery. Mara had thought, absurdly, greedily, Here is someone who does not disappear.

Then, sometime after the night they drank too much plum brandy on the back steps and Leni fell asleep with her head briefly, catastrophically, on Mara’s shoulder, she had begun disappearing in increments. Leaving rooms when Mara entered. Smiling without looking. Saying she was tired. Saying she had work. Saying tomorrow.

Tomorrow had run out.

The others drifted toward the water, shrieking at the cold, daring one another deeper. Leni watched them.

“Are you going in?” Mara asked.

“Are you?”

“No.”

“Then no.”

This should not have pleased her. It did.

They sat in the fog, shoulder to shoulder but not touching. Around them, the day proceeded with the blunt symbolism of a story written by someone unsubtle. A dog stole half a baguette and escaped in ecstasy. Theo cut his foot on a shell and announced, with satisfaction, that he was bleeding “for the Atlantic.” Priya’s hat blew into the surf, and Leni ran after it, limping on her one sandal, laughing so hard she nearly fell.

Mara watched her return, hair coming loose, cheeks bright with cold. There would be photographs later, no doubt: Leni blurred at the edge of the frame, Leni bending to wring water from Priya’s hat, Leni standing against the colorless sea as if she belonged to weather more than to any country. Mara would look at them on the train and feel sophisticated about suffering. She would enlarge one by accident. She would not send a message.

Leni dropped down beside her, breathless. “I saved the hat.”

“You’re very heroic.”

“I know. It’s a burden.”

A pause opened between them. Not awkward exactly. Worse: familiar.

Leni picked at the frayed edge of the towel. “What time do you leave tomorrow?”

“Eight-forty train.”

“So early.”

“Coward’s departure.”

“Mine is at noon.” Leni looked out at the water. “Plenty of time to be sentimental in the morning.”

“You? Sentimental?”

“I contain multitudes.”

“Yes, but most of them are evasive.”

The words came out sharper than Mara intended. Leni’s fingers stilled.

Well done. Excellent. Stab the thing because it won’t name itself.

Leni said, “Am I?”

Mara could still laugh it off. She could point at Theo, who was now hopping toward them with his injured foot wrapped in someone’s T-shirt, and let the moment pass. Moments were always volunteering to pass. That was their primary talent.

Instead she said, “A little.”

Leni nodded slowly, as if accepting a critique she had expected but not enjoyed. “Maybe.”

The fog thickened. The others had begun constructing a windbreak from bags and towels, doomed engineering accompanied by much advice. Mara stared at the sea until it blurred. If Leni apologized, what then? If Leni confessed—what? That she, too, had felt the dangerous tilt of certain conversations, the charged absurdity of reaching for the same orange, the way silence became inhabited when they were alone? And then? Airports. Trains. Polite messages across time zones. A tenderness converted into anecdote.

Leni said, “I’m bad at endings.”

Mara almost laughed. Who wasn’t? The world was merely people claiming specialties in universal incompetence.

“I think,” Leni continued, “I start leaving before I leave. It seems safer.”

“Does it work?”

“No.” She pulled her remaining sandal toward her and pressed her thumb into the sand-caked strap. “Not especially.”

Mara’s chest tightened with a feeling too young for her body. Hope, that idiot. It had no manners.

From the windbreak, Priya shouted that lunch was ready, though lunch appeared to consist of bruised grapes, damp bread, and cheese sweating inside wax paper. Everyone gathered. They ate with cold fingers. Wine was passed around. Theo displayed his wound repeatedly until no one would look. The dog returned at a distance, optimistic and unrepentant.

After lunch the fog lifted for exactly eleven minutes.

Sun spilled over the beach with sudden theatrical generosity. The sea turned blue, the kind of blue that made people forgive everything. Everyone cheered as if they had arranged it. Coats came off. Faces tilted upward. For those eleven minutes, the residency became what the brochure had promised: luminous, communal, touched by grace.

Leni lay back on the towel and closed her eyes. Mara looked at the fine shadow of her lashes, the damp curl near her ear, the vulnerable hollow at the base of her throat. She wanted, with a force that embarrassed her, to place her hand there. Not even to kiss her. Just to confirm that Leni was real and warm and not already gone.

Instead she said, “You have sand on your face.”

Leni opened one eye. “Where?”

Mara pointed vaguely.

“There?”

“No. Higher.”

Leni rubbed the wrong cheek. “Now?”

“No, you’re making it worse.”

“Help me, then.”

It was nothing. People touched one another’s faces for smaller reasons. An eyelash. A crumb. Stage makeup. Mara lifted her hand and brushed sand from Leni’s temple with her thumb.

Leni went still.

The beach around them seemed to fall away—the others talking, gulls complaining, the sea dragging itself back and forth like a thought it could not finish. Leni’s eyes were open now, fixed on Mara’s. Neither of them smiled.

There. So this was the shape of it. Not dramatic. Not even enough to tell later without sounding foolish. A thumb against a temple, a breath held too long. The entire edifice of longing reduced to a grain of sand.

Then Theo shouted, “Has anyone seen the van key?”

The spell broke so cleanly it might never have existed.

No one had seen the van key. A search began, first casual, then systematic, then accusatory. Bags were overturned. Towels shaken. Pockets slapped. Theo, the designated driver by virtue of owning sunglasses, insisted he had given it to Priya. Priya insisted she would remember being entrusted with the “key to the dog-smell chariot.” Leni suggested retracing their steps; Mara suggested checking the van door; someone suggested the dog.

An hour passed. The sky dimmed again. The key was finally discovered inside the cheese wrapper, where Theo had placed it “for safekeeping” and then thrown it into the trash bag.

By then everyone was tired and irritable and faintly sticky. They trudged back through the dunes carrying less food and more sand than they had brought. Leni walked beside Mara, still barefoot on one side. Their shoulders bumped once, twice. Neither apologized.

At the top of the dune, Leni stopped.

“Look,” she said.

Below them, the beach stretched emptying into evening, all pewter and lavender. Their footprints crossed and recrossed the sand, already softening at the edges. The tide was coming in. Soon there would be no evidence of any of it: not the lost sandal, not the rescued hat, not Theo’s heroic bleeding, not the place where Mara had touched Leni’s face and almost, almost said something unforgivable.

Mara wanted to say, Stay. Or, I’ll come with you. Or, Write to me in a way that means what we are not saying. All impossible, childish, melodramatic. Also true.

Leni looked at her. “I’m glad you were here.”

A small sentence. A cowardly sentence. A gift, possibly. Mara turned it over, searching for the seam.

“I’m glad you were here too,” she said.

Leni’s expression flickered. Relief? Disappointment? There was no knowing, and perhaps that was the mercy.

In the van, on the ride back, everyone fell asleep except Mara. Leni’s head tipped slowly, inevitably, onto her shoulder as the road curved inland. This time Mara did not move. She watched the dark fields slide past and allowed herself the weight of it, the temporary warmth.

By morning, they would be competent. They would hug in the gravel drive with the others watching. They would promise visits vaguely enough to forgive themselves. Leni would smell of soap and sleep. Mara would board her train and find sand still in the cuff of her jeans, a private ruin.

But not yet.

For now there was the van’s low rattle, the salt drying on her skin, Leni breathing against her sleeve. For now there was the road back to the house, longer than it should have been, mercifully longer, and Mara sat very still beneath the borrowed weight, as if carrying something fragile across a room.
