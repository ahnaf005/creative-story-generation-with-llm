### The Long Wait

The road had been empty for so long that Martin had begun to think of it as a thing the mountain had made for its own use: a narrow gray seam between pine and stone, running upward until it vanished into cloud. He was walking west, though there was nowhere in particular he needed to be. His pack held a flask, a map, and a clean shirt. His phone had been useless since morning.

Then he saw the car.

It had gone through the low stone barrier at a bend and come to rest nose-down among the scrub below. One wheel turned slowly, ticking as it moved. The engine was silent. Martin dropped his pack and scrambled down the slope, catching his sleeve on a thorn bush. At the car window, he saw a woman folded sideways in the driver’s seat, her hair across her face.

“Can you hear me?”

He tried the door. It wouldn’t open. The woman did not stir. Martin reached in as far as he could and touched her shoulder, then her neck. Nothing. He tried again, more carefully, as though there might be a trick to it he’d missed. There wasn’t.

The mountain made no comment.

He climbed back to the road and took the small emergency beacon from his pack. He had bought it after Agnes died, because she’d always said he was too proud of being hard to find. He pressed the button and held it until a light began to blink.

A rescue team would come. Or so the beacon promised. He could keep the light in sight from the bend. He could wait.

Martin sat with his back against the barrier. The woman’s car was hidden below him except for its bright red tail, angled toward the sky. He looked at the empty road, then down at his hands. They were shaking. Cold, he told himself. Only cold.

“I’m Martin,” he said, to the woman, though she couldn’t hear him. “I suppose you know that already.”

He had not spoken to anyone since the last village. At first this had seemed pleasant. He’d been tired of voices—shopkeepers asking where he was headed, strangers telling him the weather, friends saying he should take more care. But a voice could be useful. It kept the world from closing too quickly around a person.

He told her about his father’s dog, Bramble, who had once chased a hare up the side of a quarry and returned three days later with a different dog following him. His father had called Bramble a traitor. Martin had been seven and thought this was a marvelous joke, though he didn’t know why. For years afterward, he and his father had told that story whenever Bramble got out of the yard. Each time the dog had been gone longer, and the stranger following him had become more dangerous: a fox, a wolf, a bear. The facts had worn thin. The story had lasted.

“Your father probably had stories,” Martin said. “Most people do. Even when they claim they don’t.”

A gust moved through the pines. It was not a large wind, but the trees took it seriously. They dipped their branches in a wave, lifted them again. The car’s loose wheel had stopped ticking. Martin checked the beacon. Still blinking.

He told her about the summer he worked in the ferry office at Bellmere, before he met Agnes. The ticket window had been no bigger than a wardrobe, and the lake was so wide that on cloudy days the far shore disappeared. A woman used to come every morning and buy two tickets, though she always crossed alone. She said the second was for her husband, who had gone ahead. Martin had believed her until, one day, she stopped coming. No one told him why. Years later, he’d wondered whether her husband had ever existed, or whether the ticket had been a small, daily way of keeping someone with her.

It was strange what he remembered. Not the faces of the tourists, not the names of the ferries, but the second ticket. Perhaps memory was a kind of weather. Certain things stayed damp long after the rest had dried.

“Agnes would have liked that woman,” he said.

He looked down at the car. A pane of glass caught the afternoon light and flashed. Somewhere above the slope, a raven called once, then again. It sounded impatient. Martin tried to make out the woman’s face through the branches, but from here there was nothing to see.

Agnes had been gone two years. A sudden clot, the doctor had said, as if the word had been something he’d found under a chair. She’d been standing at the kitchen counter, cutting an apple, and then she wasn’t standing. Martin remembered how the knife had fallen and rolled beneath the table. For months afterward, whenever he found it there in his mind, he wanted to kneel and look for it again.

He had kept the house at first. He had kept her green mug, her raincoat, the note she’d left on the counter that said BUY PEARS. Eventually, he sold the house, gave away the mug and raincoat, and threw away the note. A sensible person would have understood that these actions did not remove Agnes from the world. They did remove the objects from the places where he kept running into her.

He’d been walking ever since. Not all the time. He had stayed in towns, taken rooms, sat in cafes and watched people pass with their bright, brief plans. But he was always more comfortable on a path, where a day could be measured in distance instead of hours.

“Maybe you were going somewhere,” he said to the woman. “That’s the trouble with accidents. They don’t check your itinerary.”

The beacon gave a short chirp. Martin lifted it, but its screen showed only that the signal had been sent. He knew there might be a long delay. The road was little used, the mountains steep, and the nearest town small. He could go for help on foot. But if the rescuers came while he was gone, they might miss the car. He had seen enough in the woman’s stillness to know he could not offer her anything. Still, he stayed.

A coldness crept through the knees of his trousers. He found a patch of sun and moved his pack there. The light was already losing its strength. How could it go so quickly? A moment ago, there had been the whole afternoon. Now the ridge was beginning to darken.

He told her the story of the boat.

He and Agnes had rented it one year on the coast, despite neither of them knowing how to sail. They had drifted out beyond the harbor, and the wind had carried them toward a small island where the seals lay on the rocks. Agnes had laughed at Martin as he tried to fold the sail. It kept catching the wind and snapping open like a flag. They’d spent two hours getting back, arguing in a cheerful way, then another hour sitting in the boat after they’d tied it up, neither wanting to be the first to leave.

Nothing important had happened. That was what made it worth remembering. The day had been made of ordinary minutes, and then, without warning, it had become a thing he could no longer enter.

A moth landed on his sleeve. It sat there as if considering the cloth, then lifted off and disappeared into the brush. The trees were black against the sky now. The slope below the road was a tangle of shadows. The wilderness seemed to have been waiting, patiently, for the small disturbance of the car to end.

Martin took out his flask and drank. The water tasted faintly of metal.

“You didn’t ask for this,” he said.

He wondered if those were the words people used because the dead needed them, or because the living did. He wondered whether she had someone waiting for her. A father, a child, a friend who’d just sent a message she hadn’t answered. It seemed impossible that the person in the car had arrived in the world without a trail of connections. Someone, somewhere, would be looking at a screen and deciding that ten minutes was a long time to go without a reply.

The beacon chirped again. Martin read the message: **Signal received. Rescue team dispatched. Estimated arrival unknown. Stay with subject if safe.**

Safe. The word was almost funny. The car, the slope, the falling dark—none of it had been safe. But he was here, and the woman was here, and for the moment there was nothing else he could do.

He stood, stiffly, and walked to the bend. There was no sign of headlights. He cupped his hands around his mouth and called down to the car, though he couldn’t have said what he meant to say. The sound went away between the trees.

When he returned to the barrier, he began another story. This one concerned his father’s hands: large, scarred, always smelling faintly of machine oil. Martin remembered them fixing the loose clasp on his schoolbag, turning the radio dial, holding Bramble by the collar while the dog barked at a snowman. His father had been dead for nine years. Martin had never thought to ask him about the stranger dog.

He could have asked. He hadn’t. There had been time, and then there hadn’t.

The road remained empty. The beacon blinked steadily in his palm, an artificial heartbeat against the immense, indifferent dark. Martin watched it and thought how everything depended on the scale of the looking. The signal was a tiny point. The mountain was vast. Yet the tiny point had reached someone. Somewhere, people had turned toward it, gathered equipment, started an engine. The world was full of distances, but not all of them were impossible.

“Someone’s coming,” he told the woman.

Then he sat beside the road and waited with her. The trees bent and straightened in the wind. Far below, unseen water moved over stone. Martin kept talking—not because he thought the woman could hear, or because he believed stories could undo what had happened, but because the night was large and the waiting was long, and there was still time to fill with a human voice.
