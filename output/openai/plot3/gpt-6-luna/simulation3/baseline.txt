## The Long Wait

The road had no name on the map, only a thin gray line that ended where the mountains began. Elias had been following it since morning, walking beneath a sky the color of old tin. On one side, the slope climbed through dark firs; on the other, it dropped into a valley where a river flashed now and then between the rocks.

He had chosen the road because it went nowhere in particular. That was the point.

At sixty-three, he had become suspicious of destinations. They made promises, and promises, he had learned, were mostly a way of making the present seem inadequate. So he walked. He carried a pack, a flask, a folded map, and a small radio that had not caught a signal since yesterday. His wife’s red wool scarf was looped around the pack’s top flap, though she had been dead for four years.

The bend appeared just after noon, sharp as a hooked finger. Elias heard the crash before he saw it: a harsh metal cry, then the dull, settling thump of something heavy coming to rest.

He ran.

The car had come through the low stone barrier and rolled down the slope, stopping against a stand of young pines. Its front end was crushed inward. The driver’s door hung open. One tire turned slowly in the air, making a soft, uneven ticking sound.

Elias slid down the bank, grabbing at brush. He called out before he reached the car.

“Hello? Can you hear me?”

No answer.

A woman lay several yards away, on her back in the grass. She wore a blue coat and one boot. The other lay near the edge of the road, its laces tangled around a piece of broken glass. Her face was turned toward the sky, eyes closed. One hand rested palm-up beside her hip, as if she had dropped something and was waiting for someone to pick it up.

Elias stopped.

He had seen death before. His father’s, in a hospital bed; his wife’s, in their kitchen, after the ambulance arrived too late. Even so, the sight of a stranger’s stillness felt different. It seemed less like an ending than an interruption, a sentence cut off in the middle.

He knelt beside her without touching her. He called to her again, then checked for breathing from a careful distance. There was none. He looked for movement, for the smallest rise beneath her coat. Nothing.

The emergency number came to him slowly. His fingers shook as he dialed. The dispatcher asked for the road, the nearest landmark, the number of people involved. Elias gave what he could: a bend, a stone barrier, a valley whose name he did not know. She told him help was coming and asked him to stay.

“I will,” he said.

When the call ended, the mountains seemed to lean closer. Wind stirred the grass around the woman’s shoulders. Above them, a raven crossed the sky, black against the low clouds.

Elias sat down on the wet ground. He did not know what else to do.

After a while, he began to speak.

“I used to be a schoolteacher,” he told the woman. “History. I suppose I spent most of my life telling children that the past was made of important things. Kings, borders, wars. It took me years to understand it was mostly made of ordinary afternoons.”

He rubbed his palms together. They were cold.

“There was a boy in my class named Peter Vale. He had a laugh that arrived before he did. He’d come bursting into the room, already laughing, though no one knew why. I can still hear it. I couldn’t tell you what he got on his final exam. I don’t remember what university he went to, or if he went at all. But I remember the day he brought a frog to school in his lunch tin. The frog escaped during a lesson on the Roman Empire.”

He smiled faintly, then looked at the woman.

“Peter’s gone now. I heard from another teacher. Heart trouble, in his fifties. I never called him. I always thought there’d be time.”

The car’s engine made a small clicking noise as it cooled.

Elias glanced at it, then back toward the road. No headlights appeared. The radio in his pack remained silent. The dispatcher had said the nearest ambulance was a long way off.

“People think a life is the big events,” he continued. “The wedding, the funeral, the house you buy. But you can lose a house in a season. A wedding is one day. What stays, if anything, is the other stuff. Someone passing you the salt. A voice from the next room. A hand on your back when you’re standing at the sink.”

He thought of Mara.

They had met at a bus stop in winter. She had been reading a book with a torn cover, and he had asked what it was about. She looked at him for a moment, then said, “Mostly people making mistakes.” He told her that described history too. She laughed, and the bus came before he could think of anything better to say.

For thirty-eight years they had made a life out of small repetitions: coffee at seven, errands on Saturdays, arguments about windows left open. She sang while washing dishes, usually the wrong words. She kept a jar of buttons in the kitchen drawer and refused to throw out a single one. When she got sick, Elias learned how much of love was simply staying in the room.

“I used to think the world would stop when Mara died,” he said. “Not all at once. I thought it would pause, maybe. Like a train waiting at a signal. But the milk still went bad. The post still came. The trees put out leaves in spring.”

He plucked a blade of grass and wound it around one finger.

“I was offended by that. I thought the world should have the decency to notice.”

The woman did not stir. Her pale face looked almost peaceful, though Elias knew better than to trust appearances. The wind lifted the edge of her coat and let it fall.

He told her about the first house he and Mara rented. The stairs creaked so loudly they could tell who was coming down, even in the dark. Their bedroom window looked onto an alley where a stray cat gave birth beneath a delivery van. They watched the kittens grow from a distance, and Mara named them after composers.

“Beethoven was the one with the white paws,” Elias said. “Though I never knew why. I don’t think she did either.”

He paused. In the valley, the river disappeared beneath a grove of trees. A cloud moved across the far ridge, shadowing the snow.

“Everything changes shape,” he said. “Even the things we think we’ve kept.”

For a long time, neither he nor the mountains made a sound. Then the wind came down from the upper slopes, carrying the smell of wet pine and stone. Elias shivered and pulled his collar up. He had no blanket, but he did have the red scarf. He lifted it from his pack and folded it over the woman’s chest without moving her.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “I don’t know if that helps.”

The scarf brightened the dull blue of her coat. He had bought it for Mara on a trip to a harbor town. She had worn it only once, complaining that it made her look like a traffic signal. When she was gone, Elias had taken it on walks because the red seemed stubborn against all the gray.

He told the woman this. He told her about the harbor and the gulls and the café where Mara had spilled coffee on a map. He told her how they had argued over which road to take, and how they had ended up at a beach that wasn’t marked anywhere. He could still see them there, younger, windblown, their shoes in their hands as they walked the wet sand.

The memory was so clear that for an instant he expected to hear Mara behind him.

But there was only the road, and the broken car, and the woman beneath the sky.

A distant sound rose from somewhere beyond the ridge. Elias stood, listening. It faded, leaving only the ticking tire. He sat again.

“I’m here,” he told her. “That’s what I can do.”

He wondered who was waiting for her. Perhaps someone had already called and was watching a phone, willing it to ring. Perhaps she lived alone and no one would know for hours. He pictured a kitchen somewhere, a cup on a table, a light left on. It was easy to invent a life from a single face, and impossible to know whether any of it was true.

The dispatcher’s voice returned to him: Stay with her. Help is coming.

So he stayed.

The clouds began to break, revealing a narrow strip of pale sunlight above the western ridge. It touched the treetops first, then slipped down the mountainside, turning the wet branches silver. The woman’s face remained in shadow.

Elias thought of all the days that had gone on without permission. The day after his father died, when he had bought oranges. The morning after Mara’s funeral, when rain had begun and he had gone out to bring in the washing. Ordinary life did not ask whether a person was ready. It simply carried on, like water finding a lower place.

That did not make it cruel. He was beginning to understand that. The world’s indifference was not the same as malice. The trees did not fail to mourn; they were trees. The mountain did not forget the dead. It had never known their names.

The distant sound came again, clearer now: an engine straining uphill. Elias rose and waved both arms. Around the bend, a white emergency vehicle appeared, its lights flashing silently in the daylight. Behind it came a second vehicle.

The paramedics worked quickly. They checked the woman, spoke to each other in low voices, and covered her with a sheet. One asked Elias what he had seen. He told them: the crash, the call, the time he had arrived. He showed them the spot where the car had left the road.

They thanked him. Someone asked if he needed medical attention. He said no.

A paramedic lifted the red scarf from the woman’s chest and offered it to him. Elias took it carefully, folding the fabric into a square.

When the vehicles were gone, the road grew quiet again. The broken car remained below, its open door facing the trees. Elias stood for a moment at the edge of the slope. There was no right thing to say to the empty place, no story that could turn what had happened into something else.

At last, he put the scarf back on his pack and continued west.

The road climbed. The clouds moved on. Beneath him, the river went on making its bright way through the valley, carrying leaves, rainwater, and light toward a place Elias would never see.
