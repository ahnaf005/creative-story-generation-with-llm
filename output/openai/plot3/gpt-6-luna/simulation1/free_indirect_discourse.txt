### The Long Wait

The road had been built for people in a hurry, though no one hurried along it now. It climbed through the mountains in narrow, patched turns, with a steep drop on one side and rock on the other. By late afternoon the clouds had lowered until the peaks seemed to be disappearing into them.

Elias walked with his hands in his jacket pockets. He had left his car at the trailhead that morning and taken the old service road north, intending to be back before dark. Intending was a soft word. He had been intending things for years.

The sound came up the valley: a scrape, a crash, then silence.

He stopped. A stone clicked down the slope and vanished among the trees. For a moment he waited for another sound—a shout, an engine turning over—but there was only the wind making its way through the firs. He started walking again, faster now, though the road curved and hid whatever had happened.

The car lay below the road, on its side, its front end crushed against a pine. One wheel turned slowly in the air. The driver’s door was jammed against the ground. A woman was visible through the broken passenger window, her face turned toward the pale sky.

Elias climbed down carefully. Loose gravel slid beneath his boots. He called out to her before he reached the car, as if she might be sleeping and would be embarrassed to wake in the wrong place.

“Can you hear me?”

Nothing.

He knelt beside the window. The woman’s eyes were open but did not seem to rest on anything. There was blood in her hair, though not much. Her hand lay palm-up against the seat, fingers slightly curled. He could not see her breathing.

He put two fingers against her neck. Nothing there, or nothing he could find. A person’s pulse was supposed to be obvious in stories. In real life it hid behind bone and cold skin.

His hands began to shake. He made them stop. He had a satellite messenger clipped to his pack, a small orange device meant for emergencies and the occasional message to his daughter. He pressed the button and waited for the signal to find its way out of the valley.

The confirmation light blinked. A rescue team had been alerted. He sent the coordinates and added that the victim was unresponsive. Then he sat down on the gravel, where she could see him if she was able to see anything.

There was no reason to go. The thought came plainly, without drama. They were coming. He could climb back to the road, stand there, make himself easier to find. But someone should stay with her. Someone should keep the world from becoming empty around her before the rescuers arrived.

“Well,” he said, “I’ve got time.”

The wind shifted. High above, a raven circled once and disappeared behind the ridge.

Elias had been alone in the mountains before. He had even liked it. Alone meant no one asked whether he was tired, and no one noticed how long he spent looking at things that had no use: a patch of snow in a shadow, a beetle crossing the trail, a cloud snagged on a peak. But this was a different kind of alone. The woman was there, and he was there, and still the world had found a way to make a loneliness between them.

He cleared his throat.

“When I was twelve, my brother and I stole our father’s rowboat,” he said. “We weren’t supposed to go out past the point. Naturally, that was exactly where we went.”

His brother, Peter, had been nine and fearless in the way children were fearless before they understood what fear was for. He had stood in the bow, legs spread wide, one hand shading his eyes, calling himself captain. Elias had rowed from the stern, pretending the boat was not taking on water. There had been a crack in the hull. A small one, their father had said later. Small enough to be ignored until it wasn’t.

They made it back to shore, drenched and shivering. Their father had shouted at them, then held them so tightly that Peter laughed into his coat.

“That was a good day,” Elias said. “At least, it became one. At the time I mostly thought we were going to die.”

He glanced at the woman. Her face had not changed. The wind moved a strand of hair across her cheek, and he reached through the window to brush it aside. Her skin was cold.

“Sorry,” he said.

The emergency service had acknowledged his message. They would send a helicopter if the weather held, otherwise a ground team from the south road. Estimated arrival: forty-five minutes. The words appeared on the little screen with all the comfort of a grocery receipt. Forty-five minutes. A measure of time that could hold a lifetime if you were waiting for something not to happen.

The light thinned. The trees on the far slope darkened to blue. Elias took a thermos from his pack and drank. Coffee, cold and bitter. He should have brought the good thermos; he always meant to bring it on longer walks. There were a hundred small ways to prepare better. There was no reason to make a list now.

“You know, I used to think every bad thing started with a warning,” he said. “Like the world gave you time to get ready. A sound, a look, a last conversation.”

His wife had been called Anna, and she had hated waiting rooms. During her final weeks, she had made him bring a deck of cards to the hospital. They played gin rummy under fluorescent lights, and she cheated badly, with no subtlety at all. He let her win until she noticed and accused him of insulting her.

“Then she beat me fair,” he told the woman. “Three games in a row. I still think she was lying about that.”

After Anna died, people said she was gone. Elias understood what they meant. He had used the same words himself. But she had not gone anywhere he could point to. Her coat still hung behind the bedroom door for months. He could still hear the exact way she said his name when he forgot something she had asked him to remember. She was absent, yes. But absence was not the same as nothing.

The woman in the car had a silver ring on her left hand. A narrow band, no stone. Elias wondered who had given it to her. Maybe she had bought it herself. Maybe she had worn it so long she no longer remembered where it came from. All the ordinary things about her were now private in a way they would never have been if he’d met her at a roadside café.

He tried not to imagine people waiting for her.

The clouds dragged low over the road. Somewhere beyond the ridge, a helicopter might be trying to decide whether it was safe to fly. The mountain did not care about a schedule. It had been here before the road, before cars, before people named the passes and drew lines across maps. It would remain after the lights had gone out in every house below.

A gust shook the branches. The woman’s open eyes caught the last thin strip of sky.

Elias looked away, then looked back. There was a kind of courage in not pretending the dead person was somewhere else. Not much courage. More like a refusal to leave one more thing unattended.

“When I was young,” he said, “I thought I could keep people safe by knowing what to do. My father taught me knots. My mother taught me how to recognize a storm. Anna taught me how to read a map, because I kept insisting I didn’t need one.”

He smiled. It passed quickly.

“None of it was enough, of course.”

The truth sounded harsh in the open air, but it was not a cruel truth. A knot held until the rope broke. A roof held until the wind took it. People held each other for as long as they could. Then the weather changed, or the body failed, or a car left the road. The world did not wait for anyone to feel ready.

He thought of Peter, grown older, living in a city Elias had not visited in six years. They spoke on birthdays. They meant to visit each other. How easy it was to let a life become a chain of postponed visits and messages beginning, Sorry, just saw this.

He had been walking in the mountains when the car crashed. His phone had been switched off, the way he preferred it. He had planned to return before dark. It was strange how the ordinary plan still seemed to belong to him, as if the day had not already broken into before and after.

A small sound came from the car, a ticking as the engine cooled. It repeated, then stopped.

“There’s nothing I can tell you that will make this easier,” he said. “I know that. I’m only talking because I don’t know what else to do.”

The emergency message updated: ground team approaching from the east. Approximate arrival, twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes. The mountain loosened its hold on the light, and the first stars appeared where the clouds had parted. Elias pulled his jacket closer. The cold came up from the earth and settled in his knees. He could have stood, walked the short distance back to the road, and waved a light. Instead, he stayed where he was.

He told her about the time he and Anna got lost in a city they had visited only once, before either of them knew it was possible to be happy in a place you could not find on a map. They had wandered for two hours, arguing over directions, then found a bakery still open after midnight. Anna had eaten a sugared pastry on the steps outside and gotten sugar all over her coat. She had insisted the night had been better for it.

“Maybe she was right,” he said. “You get somewhere by accident. That doesn’t mean it was the wrong place.”

The air changed before the sound came. A deep tremor rolled between the slopes, and then the helicopter appeared above the ridge, lights sweeping across the trees. It hovered briefly over the road, then moved away to find a place to land. The searchlight washed over the wreck, bright as noon, and the woman’s face disappeared beneath it.

Elias raised one arm. The helicopter’s beam found him. He held still until it moved on.

The rescue team reached the road not long afterward, three people in dark jackets with packs and a stretcher. They climbed down, speaking softly to one another. One of them asked Elias what had happened. He told them. Another touched the woman’s neck and looked at the others. No one said the word dead. They did not need to.

They worked carefully, with practiced hands. One asked him to move back, and he did. There were forms to fill out, questions about his name, the time he had found her, the messages he had sent. He answered as best he could.

When they began to lift the car, Elias walked up to the road. The helicopter’s rotor wash bent the grass. The rescue workers moved the woman toward the light, where the rest of the world was waiting for her now.

He looked once at the strip of road above the drop, the dark trees beyond it, the mountains crowding close around everything. Then he turned toward the trailhead. It was a long walk back. The evening had come without asking permission, and there was nothing to do but meet it.
