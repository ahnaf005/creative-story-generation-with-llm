### The Waiting

The road had been built for a world that no longer existed.

It climbed the mountain in narrow switchbacks, its cracked asphalt slipping between fir trees and bare slopes. In places, the outer edge had crumbled away, leaving only a low guardrail and, beyond it, a long view down into the valley. Daniel had been walking beside it since morning, heading north with his pack and the quiet purpose of a man who did not expect to meet anyone.

He heard the crash before he saw it: a sharp, splitting sound, followed by a deeper thump that seemed to come from the mountain itself.

He stopped.

For a moment the forest went on being a forest. Wind moved high in the firs. A raven called once from somewhere above the road. Then Daniel heard a faint ticking, like a clock trying to keep time.

He hurried around the bend.

A silver car had broken through the guardrail and come to rest against a stand of young pines. Its front end was folded in. One rear wheel spun slowly, then stopped. Glass glittered across the road and among the weeds.

Daniel set down his pack.

“Hello?” he called.

There was no answer. He moved carefully, watching the ground for leaking fuel or loose metal. The driver’s door hung open. A woman was slumped behind the wheel, her head turned toward the window. Her hair, dark and tangled, lay across her cheek.

He said her name, though he did not know it. He said it softly, as if she might be sleeping.

When he reached her, he could see there was nothing to be done. Still, he checked for a pulse. He waited, fingers against her neck, listening for the small insistence of a living body. The ticking from the car went on. The wind stirred the woman’s hair.

Daniel drew his hand back.

He had been alone for a long time, but he had never been alone with death before.

His phone found a weak signal near the road’s outer edge. He called emergency services and gave the dispatcher the mile marker, the direction of travel, the number of people involved. He answered the questions as best he could. Yes, the vehicle was unstable. No, he didn’t see smoke. No, he couldn’t tell how long it had been there. The dispatcher told him not to move the woman, and to stay nearby until help arrived.

“How long?” Daniel asked.

“About forty minutes, maybe longer. The road crew has been notified.”

“Understood.”

He returned to the car and stood beside it, hands hanging at his sides. There was nothing useful to do. The woman’s face had the calmness of someone who had been interrupted halfway through a thought. He looked toward the valley, where the afternoon light lay in long bands across the trees.

Then, because silence felt unbearable, he began to talk.

“My name is Daniel,” he said. “I used to be a schoolteacher.”

The woman did not answer. He crouched a few feet away, careful not to touch the wreck.

“I taught history in a town called Bellwether. Not a remarkable town. One main street, a mill that closed before I got there, and a river that flooded every spring. My classroom was on the second floor. In winter, the windows rattled so hard the students thought someone was trying to get in.”

He glanced at her. Her eyes were closed now, or perhaps they had been closed all along.

“There was a boy in my class named Samuel. He never spoke unless called on. One day I asked him to explain the fall of Rome, and he said, ‘It got old.’ The whole class laughed. I laughed too. But he was right, in his way. Things that seem permanent are often just things that have not finished changing yet.”

Daniel rubbed his palms together. They were cold despite the day’s warmth.

“Samuel’s mother came to parent night once. She stood outside my door for nearly ten minutes before she came in. She told me he wanted to be a carpenter. I thought I should have something wise to say, so I told her he was a thoughtful boy, and he’d do fine. She thanked me. Years later, I heard he’d built houses somewhere in Oregon. I don’t know if that’s true. I hope it is.”

The mountain gave no sign of hearing him. The road curved away above, empty in both directions. He thought he could see a thin thread of dust far below, but it might have been a cloud shadow.

He took a bottle from his pack and drank. Then he set it beside him, in case the woman could somehow use it.

“I had a wife,” he said. “Her name was June. We met in a grocery store. I dropped a jar of peaches, and she told me I’d made a terrible mess. Then she helped me clean it up.”

He smiled, though the memory hurt in a dull, familiar way.

“She was a gardener. She believed every plant could be saved if you found the right place for it. We lived in a little house with a yard full of things she’d rescued from the clearance rack. Crooked roses. A pear tree that leaned like it was listening. One summer, she planted sunflowers along the fence. They grew taller than the house.”

He stopped. A gust moved through the trees and carried the scent of sap and damp earth. Somewhere a branch cracked.

“June died three years ago,” he said. “Not in an accident. Not suddenly. There was time to say things. Too much time, some days. I thought that meant we’d have time to say everything.”

The words hung between him and the wreck.

For a while he said nothing. He watched the clouds gather beyond the ridge, their undersides darkening. A small bird landed on the guardrail, cocked its head, and flew off.

“When she was sick, she asked me to tell her stories,” he continued. “Not about the hospital. She wanted stories from before we met, from when I was a child. I told her about the time my brother and I built a raft and launched it in the drainage ditch. We were certain it would take us all the way to the sea. It floated about twenty yards before it came apart.”

A faint, surprised laugh escaped him.

“My brother blamed me for tying the knots. I blamed him for choosing rotten boards. We were both right. We spent the rest of the afternoon in the ditch, fishing pieces of wood out of the mud.”

He looked down at his hands. A thin line of blood ran across one knuckle where he had cut himself on the guardrail. He hadn’t felt it happen.

“I don’t know what you were doing before this,” he said to the woman. “Going home, maybe. Or going somewhere you were excited to reach. Maybe someone was waiting. I hope somebody knows you were on this road.”

The dispatcher had said help was on the way. Daniel repeated the phrase to himself, as if it were a promise that needed holding.

The mountain was changing around him. The light had thinned. Shadows gathered at the bases of the trees, and the road, which had seemed merely empty at noon, now looked abandoned. Daniel could hear the river far below, though he could not see it. The sound was steady, indifferent, older than the road and likely to outlast it.

He thought of all the lives that had passed through this place: surveyors with their measuring chains, workers who blasted the road through stone, drivers carrying groceries or letters or children asleep in the back seat. Each had believed, for a while, in the importance of the next turn. The mountain had received them all without comment.

“That sounds bleak,” he said. “It isn’t meant to.”

He shifted closer to the car, staying where the dispatcher had told him to stay. The woman’s hand lay near the open window. On one finger was a plain silver ring.

“June wore a ring like that,” he said. “Not her wedding ring. She said she didn’t like jewelry that announced anything. She wore that one every day for twenty-six years, until her hands grew too thin and it started to slip off. I kept it in my desk for months afterward. I thought keeping it there meant she was still somewhere in the house.”

He swallowed.

“Eventually I gave it to her niece. She was young then. She wears it now, I think.”

A distant engine sounded, then faded. Daniel stood and peered up the road. Nothing. He sat again.

“You see, I used to think memory was a kind of shelter. If you remembered a person clearly enough, you could keep them from disappearing. But that’s not how it works. You remember a laugh, then forget the exact sound. You remember a room, but not the color of the curtains. The person keeps changing inside you. Maybe that’s its own way of continuing.”

The woman’s face remained still. He wondered whether he should cover her with his jacket, then remembered the dispatcher’s instructions and did not move her. Instead he took off his own coat and laid it over the broken edge of the door, shielding her from the wind as best he could.

The first rain began as a few cold drops. They spotted the road and darkened the dust on his boots. Daniel pulled his hood up. The woman’s hair stirred beneath the coat.

He talked until his voice grew hoarse. He told her about his father, who had taught him to sharpen a kitchen knife and never explained how to stop. He told her about the apartment he’d rented after June died, with a view of a brick wall and a window that would not close properly. He told her about deciding to walk north, and how the decision had seemed less like courage than like running out of reasons to stay put.

At last he ran out of stories.

He listened to the rain ticking on the car. The sound was gentler than the clocklike tapping that had first drawn him near. He looked into the trees, where the dusk was thickening. The wilderness did not feel hostile, exactly. It was simply there: broad-backed and patient, holding its own weather, its own small dramas. The road cut through it like a sentence that could be interrupted at any point.

Then the siren came.

It rose from far below, thin at first, then louder as it wound its way up the mountain. Daniel stood and waved, though the vehicle could not yet see him. Red lights appeared between the trees. A rescue truck rounded the bend, followed by an ambulance. Brakes hissed. Doors opened. Voices replaced the rain.

A paramedic came to him first. She asked what had happened, whether he had touched anything, whether he was injured. Daniel answered. He pointed toward the car and stepped back as the crew approached.

The woman’s hand disappeared from view beneath a blanket. The silver ring caught one last glint from the ambulance lights, then was gone.

The paramedic thanked him and asked if he had someone to call.

“No,” he said. “I’m all right.”

He picked up his pack. The road crew began setting flares, and the rescue team moved with practiced care around the wreck. Behind them, the mountain was already closing over the noise.

Daniel started walking north.

The rain softened the dust beneath his boots. Somewhere below, the river continued down through the valley. Somewhere ahead, the road kept turning. He did not know what waited around the next bend, and for the first time in a long while, he did not try to imagine it.
