### The Long Wait

By the time Elias reached the old pass, the clouds had lowered until the mountains seemed to be carrying them on their shoulders.

He had been walking since dawn, following a road that no longer appeared on most maps. Once it had connected the valley towns; now it served the occasional forestry truck and the stubborn few who preferred their journeys on foot. Gravel shifted beneath his boots. Spruce crowded the slopes, black-green and close, and far above them the bare peaks held the last of the season’s snow.

Elias liked the pass because it asked nothing of him except to keep walking.

Then he saw the tire marks.

They scored the road’s outer shoulder, two dark lines bending sharply through the gravel and vanishing over the edge. Beyond them, the slope dropped through alder and stone. A car lay below, its front end crushed against a stand of fir trees, one rear wheel turning slowly in the air.

Elias stopped.

For a moment, he heard only the wind moving through the treetops. Then came the ticking of an engine cooling.

He climbed down carefully, using the shrubs for balance. Pebbles slipped beneath his boots and skittered into the ravine. The car had come to rest on its side, nose angled downhill. Its windows were broken, and the driver’s door had buckled inward.

A woman was in the front seat.

Elias crouched beside the shattered window. He called to her, though he knew from her stillness that she could not hear him. One arm lay across her lap. Her dark hair had fallen over her face, and a thin silver chain shone at her throat. He reached through a gap in the glass and touched her wrist.

There was no pulse.

He tried again, counting slowly, as if patience might summon what his fingers could not find. He watched for breath. The cold mountain air moved over her face, but she did not.

When he was certain, he pulled his hand back.

The emergency number took three attempts to connect. On the third, a dispatcher answered, her voice faint beneath a crackle of static. Elias gave the road name, then the nearest marker, then what he could see. The dispatcher asked if anyone was injured.

“One person,” he said. “The driver. She’s dead.”

There was a pause, not long enough to be called silence.

“Stay with her,” the dispatcher said. “We’re sending help. It may take a while.”

Elias looked up through the trees. The road was empty. “I’ll stay.”

He climbed back to the shoulder, where the wind was stronger, and sat on a flat stone. From there he could see the car and, through its broken side window, a small triangle of the woman’s coat. He had no watch, but the afternoon had plenty of time left in it. Emergency services would come when they could.

For a while he said nothing. The wilderness filled the space around him. A raven passed overhead, black against the low clouds, and disappeared behind a ridge.

Then Elias began telling the woman about his father.

“My father repaired clocks,” he said. “Not the expensive ones. The ones people brought in because they couldn’t afford to replace them. Kitchen clocks, alarm clocks, the little brass ones that had belonged to someone’s grandmother. He used to say every clock was telling two stories: the time, and the story of how it had been kept.”

He picked a burr from his sleeve.

“When I was a boy, I thought that was nonsense. A clock either worked or it didn’t. Then one winter he brought home a pocket watch that had belonged to his brother. It had stopped at 3:17. My father opened the back and found a bit of grit caught in the gears. He cleared it out, wound the watch, and it started running again. I remember being disappointed. I wanted some mystery. He said, ‘Most mysteries are just things we haven’t looked at closely enough.’”

The car gave a small metallic groan below them, settling a little deeper into the brush.

Elias waited until the sound passed. Then he went on.

“My father was wrong about some things. He thought fixing something meant it was as good as new. But you can fix a clock and still hear the damage in it. A small hitch every hour. A hesitation. He never admitted that.”

His own father had been dead for eleven years. Elias had learned, slowly, that grief did not vanish so much as change its address. At first it lived in every room. Later it took up residence in a sound, a smell, a phrase one found oneself repeating. By the time Elias came to the mountains, grief had become less like a house and more like weather: sometimes clear, sometimes not.

The clouds pressed closer to the peaks.

He walked down to the car again. The woman had a purse on the passenger floor, open and partly crushed. A paper grocery bag lay near the back seat, its contents scattered among the glass. Two apples had rolled into the brush. One rested against a tire, untouched.

Elias did not try to move the woman. He had no training, and there was no reason to think movement could help her. Instead he stood beside the door and spoke about his daughter.

“Her name is June,” he said. “She’s thirty now. When she was little, she had a yellow raincoat with a broken zipper. She wore it everywhere, even on sunny days. One afternoon we went walking by the river, and she found a caterpillar on the path. She wanted to take it home in her pocket.”

He smiled, although the woman could not see him.

“I told her it would be better off where it was. She asked how I knew. I said, ‘Because it belongs to the world.’ I thought I was being wise. She cried all the way home.”

The story of June was full of small failures like that. A birthday he had missed. A winter when work had taken him away. A dozen conversations he had ended too soon because he had mistaken silence for agreement. She lived on the coast now, and they spoke every few weeks. Their calls were polite, affectionate, and carefully brief.

“Children grow up,” he said. “That’s the bargain, I suppose. You give them somewhere to begin, and they go somewhere you can’t follow. I don’t know if I did it well. I know I tried. Sometimes that’s not the same thing.”

The wind stirred the firs. Somewhere far above, a small fall of stones rattled down a slope.

When he was younger, Elias had believed that life was made up of decisive moments, each one opening the road to the next. He had imagined a person could look back and see where everything had turned: a departure, a marriage, a loss. But the longer he lived, the more the past resembled the mountain road beneath him. A bend appeared without warning; a stretch of gravel disappeared under snow. Sometimes the turning point was only a sentence he had forgotten saying.

He wondered what the woman’s last ordinary moment had been.

Perhaps she had been thinking about dinner. Perhaps she had left a message unanswered, or meant to stop for fuel in the next town. Perhaps she had been listening to music, or had been tired and wishing she were home. A life did not always end with a warning. Sometimes it simply arrived at its last minute without announcing itself.

Elias returned to his stone. He took a flask from his pack, poured a little water into its cap, and drank. The air was cold enough that his hands had begun to ache.

“I used to think permanence meant keeping things,” he told her. “Photographs, letters, furniture. I kept boxes of all of them. My wife used to say I was saving the past for a day when I might need it. She was right, though I never told her.”

His wife, Anne, had died in a hospital room with the curtains drawn against the sun. In the weeks afterward, Elias had cleared their home a drawer at a time. He found grocery lists and loose buttons, old receipts, a list of books she meant to read. He had kept the list for years. Then, one spring, he found it in his coat pocket, softened at the folds and almost unreadable.

He could no longer remember what he had done with it.

“Things leave,” he said. “Even when we try to make them stay. Maybe that’s not a cruelty. Maybe it’s just how the world keeps moving.”

The first distant siren came so faintly that he mistook it for wind through the ravine. He stood and listened. It came again, a thin rising note from somewhere beyond the ridge.

He went down once more to the car. The woman’s face was still hidden by her hair. He did not try to move it aside. There seemed something wrong in claiming that last small gesture for himself.

“I’m here,” he said.

It was the simplest thing he had said all day.

The siren grew louder, then vanished as the vehicle crossed into a hollow. Elias climbed to the road and stood with one hand lifted, though there was no one to signal yet. The clouds opened briefly over the eastern ridge, and a bar of pale sunlight touched the treetops. It moved across the slope, brightening the branches one by one, then slipped away.

When the rescue truck finally appeared, its lights strobed blue against the gray afternoon. Two responders came down the slope with equipment. A third stayed above and spoke to Elias, asking how long he had been there and whether he knew the woman.

“No,” Elias said. “I found her.”

They asked for his name. He gave it. They asked where he was going.

“North,” he said, though he had no particular destination.

One of the responders knelt beside the car. The other looked up at Elias with the careful expression of someone trained to meet strangers in the worst minutes of their lives. Elias nodded to show that there was nothing more he could tell them.

The responder thanked him. Elias looked once toward the broken window, where the silver chain at the woman’s throat caught the light.

Then he lifted his pack and started along the road.

The siren was quiet now. Behind him, voices moved among the trees, practical and low. Ahead, the pass curved out of sight. The wind continued over the mountain, carrying no news of what had happened, or what would happen next. It touched the roadside grass and passed on.
