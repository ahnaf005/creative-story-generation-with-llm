By late afternoon the road had forgotten it was a road.

It rose in loose, pale switchbacks along the shoulder of the mountain, narrowing where meltwater had bitten at its edges, widening again where avalanches had once spread stones like spilled grain. No houses stood there, no fences, no sign of grazing animals. Only the road, the spruce trees climbing black and close on the slopes, and above them the open faces of rock still holding bars of snow in their creases though it was nearly June.

Jonas walked in the tire tracks because the dust was firmer there. His boots had rubbed a raw place behind his left heel, and every few steps the sting flared bright, then faded into the general ache of the day. He had been walking since morning. That was the point, wasn't it? To walk until thought thinned out. To put one foot down and lift the other, and let the mind become as simple as a hinge.

He had not met a vehicle in three hours.

Far below, the valley had already filled with shadow. The river moved through it in a white thread, appearing and disappearing between ranks of fir, patient, indifferent. Somewhere beyond those folds of land were villages, hotels with blue signs, a petrol station where a woman with red nails had sold him coffee at dawn and asked where he was headed. Up, he had said, nodding to the mountains. She had laughed as though he had made a joke.

Up. As if that were a destination.

He was thinking of turning back—not admitting it, merely considering the quality of light and the small sensible fact of evening—when he saw the broken trees.

At first it was only an oddness in the pattern of the roadside: a gap where young pines had been sheared off, pale splinters catching the sun. Then he saw a strip of red metal among the branches below the road, too bright to belong to bark or stone. He stopped. The mountains stopped with him. No bird called. No engine sounded. Only the distant river kept speaking in its cold mouth.

A car had gone off.

He stood a moment longer than he should have, as though the sight were asking a question and he had not yet understood the language. Then he climbed down.

The slope was steep enough that he had to turn sideways and lower himself from trunk to trunk. Stones slipped under his soles and rattled away. The smell came to him before the whole car did: petrol, hot metal, the sour powdery odor of torn airbags. It had landed nose-first against a boulder perhaps thirty meters below the road. A red hatchback, though the front of it was folded beyond the idea of make or model. One wheel still turned faintly in the air. Not spinning—no, that would have been too much motion—but rocking with the shiver of something not quite settled.

The driver's door was flung open. A woman lay half out of it, one arm beneath her, the other extended into the grass as if reaching for something just beyond her fingers. Her hair was dark and full of dust. She wore a green coat, too fine for the mountain road, city cloth, the kind that repelled drizzle and folded into nothing. One shoe had come off. It lay near the crushed bonnet, absurdly upright.

Jonas said, “Hello?”

His own voice startled him. Too polite, too ordinary. As though she might lift her head and apologize for blocking the path.

He went to her and knelt. He had taken a first-aid course years ago in the factory, taught by a bored man with a plastic torso and a bag of rubber mouths. Check the scene. Check for danger. Speak loudly. Look for breathing. He remembered these things with the surprise of finding old coins in a drawer.

“Can you hear me?”

Her face was turned partly toward the earth. He did not want to touch her and touched her anyway, two fingers at the side of the throat. The skin was still warm, or perhaps his fingers were cold. Nothing moved beneath them. He held them there too long. He shifted, leaned near her mouth. No breath. One eye was open, the color of wet slate, and seemed not to accuse him, which was worse.

The course had said to begin compressions, but there were ways of knowing. He did not want to know them, but he knew. The angle of the neck. The impossible stillness, unlike sleep, unlike fainting. The mountain had received her before he arrived.

He took out his phone. No service.

Of course.

He climbed back up to the road, slipping twice, scraping his palm on a stone. At the top he raised the phone high and walked in small circles, watching the empty bars. One bar flickered, vanished. He cursed softly, not at the phone, not at the mountain, but at the old habit of expecting the world to arrange itself around need.

A little farther up the road, the bar returned. He dialed emergency services with fingers that felt thick.

The operator’s voice came and went, a woman’s voice flattened by distance. He gave what he could: the road number from the last sign, the approximate kilometers, the red car off the south side, one victim, no, not responsive, no, not breathing. Yes, he would remain. Yes, he understood. They would send mountain rescue and police from the nearest town. It could take time.

Time. He looked back toward the gap in the trees.

“I'll wait,” he said.

When the call ended, silence pressed in again, larger than before. It was not empty silence. It was full of needles rubbing high in the branches, unseen water under stones, the tiny settling noises of the ruined car. The world had continued at once. That was the insult, wasn't it? No pause, no bell struck across the peaks.

He slid down to the car again. The woman lay as before, her hand open in the grass. He could not move her; the operator had said not to unless there was fire. There was no fire. Only the smell of petrol, sharp but not growing, and a thin ticking from the engine like a watch losing confidence.

He took off his rucksack and sat on a flat stone a few paces away. Then, after a minute, he stood and took his jacket from the pack. He spread it over her shoulders and torso, awkwardly, apologetically, leaving her face uncovered. It was what one did. The dead were covered, though not entirely. They were to be hidden and witnessed both.

“There,” he said.

The word dropped between them and disappeared.

He had never sat with a dead stranger before. He had sat with his father, but that had been in a hospital room bright with machines and the squeak of nurses’ shoes. His father had become smaller in death, as if some private inflation had gone out of him. The hand on the sheet had been yellow, waxy, not the hand that once lifted sacks of flour two at a time and smacked Jonas behind the head for leaving the shed door open. His mother had kept asking whether anyone wanted coffee. His brother had cried in the hallway where he thought no one could hear him.

And Jonas, what had Jonas done? He had stared at the window latch. He remembered that more clearly than his father’s face: the latch painted white, paint thick in the corners, chipped where fingers had turned it for years. The mind chose such things. It could not hold death, so it held paint.

The woman's fingers were dirty. There was a thin gold ring on her right hand, not a wedding ring perhaps, unless she came from a place where they wore them there. Her nails were short, unpainted. A practical woman, then. Or a woman who had meant to paint them and had not found time. Dangerous, how quickly he began inventing her. She might have been cruel. She might have sung badly in the shower. She might have owed money, loved someone, hated the mountains, been driving here under protest. She might have been called Anna, or Mara, or Elise.

“I'm Jonas,” he told her. “Not that it matters.”

But perhaps it did, a little. If she had to lie here between the road and the dark trees, she might as well have a name beside her.

A cloud moved across the sun. The air cooled. Above the far ridge, the sky had begun to lose its blue, thinning toward silver. In an hour, perhaps less, the cold would come down properly. He thought of the rescue team assembling in some low valley garage, men and women pulling on orange jackets, checking radios, one of them annoyed to be called from dinner. The living were always being interrupted. The dead, never again.

He laughed once, quietly, and disliked himself for it.

“You know,” he said, “I used to believe every road led somewhere on purpose.”

His voice sounded less strange now. He spoke not to comfort her—she had gone beyond needing comfort—but because silence was too much like abandonment.

“When I was a boy, I thought if you followed any road long enough, you would arrive at the place it had been trying to reach all along. A city, the sea, a castle. Something intended. My father said roads went where men could afford to put them. That was his answer to most things. Money, weather, weight. He had no patience for mystery.”

He could see his father’s hands on the steering wheel of the old lorry, thumbs hooked over the rim, knuckles split from winter. Jonas small beside him, feet not reaching the floor, watching fields pass in the dawn. His father delivered flour to bakeries in five towns. The lorry smelled of dust and diesel and bread before bread existed. At each stop men shouted, doors slammed, sacks thudded down. Jonas had loved the certainty of the route: mill, bridge, square, alley, bakery yard. The same dog barking at the same gate. The same woman opening the same shutters. If the world repeated itself, then perhaps it could be trusted.

But it had not repeated. The mill closed. The bridge was widened. The woman with the shutters vanished, old or dead or moved away. His father’s lorry was sold for parts after the second stroke. Even the bakeries became shops selling phones and little cakes in plastic.

Everything was provisional. That was the lesson the years kept offering, and still he behaved like a slow pupil.

The wind shifted. From somewhere uphill came the soft knock of loose stones. Jonas looked sharply toward the trees. Nothing. A deer perhaps. Or a goat gone feral. The wilderness had seemed picturesque from the road—dark forest, clean air, peaks like teeth against heaven. Down here, beside the broken car, it had body. It leaned close. It smelled of rot under snow, of old leaves and animal paths. It did not threaten, exactly. Threat implied intention. It simply was, vast and without preference.

The woman's hair moved lightly in the wind.

He wondered what her last sight had been. Not the valley, surely. More likely the road leaping, the trees rushing up, a confusion of red dashboard light and white airbag. Had there been time to think? People said life passed before the eyes, but people said all manner of things to make endings theatrical. Perhaps there had only been surprise. A single, clear What? and then no one remained to ask it.

“I once nearly drowned,” he said.

He had not meant to tell that story. It had waited in him, apparently, ready.

“I was nineteen. There was a lake near the factory town where I lived then. In summer we went after shifts, all of us smelling of oil and iron, pretending the water made us new. I swam out farther than the others because a girl was watching from the jetty. Her name was Petra. She had a gap between her front teeth. I thought this was beauty itself.”

He smiled, and the mountain did not object.

“Halfway back my leg cramped. Nothing dramatic. No storm. No wave. Just a knot in the muscle, and suddenly the shore was impossibly far. I remember being angry. Not frightened at first—angry. At my leg, at the water, at Petra for watching, though she had done nothing. Then I went under. The lake was green-brown. There were bubbles in front of my face, and light above them. I thought, so this is how. Not with meaning. Not after saying the right things. Just because a muscle tightens.”

A man from the factory had pulled him out. Tomas, broad as a wardrobe, who later lost two fingers to a press and moved to his sister’s farm. Jonas had coughed water into the reeds while everyone shouted. Petra cried. He had been ashamed of making her cry and ashamed of loving that she cried for him.

For two weeks afterward, the world had blazed. Bread tasted miraculous. The bus exhaust smelled rich and human. He forgave everyone. Then, little by little, he returned to himself. He complained about wages. He forgot to call his mother. He was unkind to a waiter in a railway café because the soup was cold. Even resurrection wore off.

He looked at the woman’s still face. “You probably knew that already.”

Light withdrew from the wreck in stages. First the red paint dulled. Then the broken glass ceased to sparkle. Shadows gathered beneath the car and under the fallen branches. Jonas found his wool hat in the rucksack and put it on. He checked his phone: no new calls, little battery. He climbed partway up the slope to get service and called again. They were on their way, the operator said. The road was difficult. He should remain visible if possible. Was he safe? Safe. He glanced down at the car, at the trees opening into blackness. Yes, he said. Safe enough.

Back beside her, he ate a piece of hard cheese and felt ashamed of chewing. Hunger did not respect solemnity. The body made its claims under every circumstance. He broke another piece, held it, then returned it to the paper. Ridiculous. She could not share it. Yet eating beside her seemed rude, as if he had brought a picnic to a grave.

There had been a woman he might have married. Not Petra. Later. Clara, who taught geography and kept maps pinned above her desk. She had believed in knowing where one was. On Sundays they drove to small towns and read plaques on churches. She could identify clouds by their Latin names and had a way of listening that made his own thoughts appear more intelligent than they were. For three years they lived together above a pharmacy. Then her mother fell ill in another country, and she went for a month, then three, then forever. They wrote at first. The letters were tender, then informative, then seasonal. He had visited once and found her changed, or himself changed, or merely the room changed, which was enough. They parted without betrayal. This had seemed, perversely, harder to accept. No villain, no wound to display. Only life leaning elsewhere.

He wondered whether the dead woman had someone who would receive a call tonight. A husband looking at soup cooling on the stove. A sister folding laundry. A son pretending not to wait up. The police would ask questions in low voices. There would be that pause before the words, a pause in which the old world still existed whole. Then the words would enter and break it.

He wished he knew her name.

The first stars appeared above the ridge. The temperature dropped quickly now. Jonas stood and stamped his feet. His heel burned. He walked a small circle around the clearing, careful not to disturb anything. The wheel had stopped rocking. The engine no longer ticked. The car seemed less like an event than an object placed there long ago and uncovered by chance.

“It's strange,” he said, “how fast things become history.”

A stone wall in his childhood village had collapsed one spring after heavy rain. For days everyone spoke of it—the noise, the dust, old Mrs. Kral nearly struck as she walked to market. Children climbed the rubble until men came to haul it away. By autumn, no one mentioned it. Grass grew where the wall had been. Newcomers never knew to miss it. Even disasters had to fight for memory, and most of them lost.

Maybe that was mercy. If every loss remained sharp, people would be unable to lift a spoon.

Yet he wanted, suddenly and fiercely, for this not to vanish. Not entirely. Not into a police report, an insurance form, a roadside stain washed clean by rain. This woman had left the road here. Her hand had rested in this grass. A stranger named Jonas had sat beside her and told foolish stories because it was all he had.

Was that enough? Enough for what?

The forest answered with a crack from the darkness. He turned. Two eyes shone briefly between trunks, low and greenish, then disappeared. Fox, perhaps. He felt the old animal tightening in his stomach. The wilderness was patient. It would wait for all of them. Cars, coats, names, the bright rings on human fingers—given time, everything became weather, mineral, root.

He moved closer to the woman, not because he could protect her from the forest, not truly, but because the gesture seemed required.

After a while, faintly, he heard a sound that did not belong to the mountain: an engine straining in low gear. He climbed toward the road, breath hard in his chest. Lights swung between the trees above, white and amber. Voices followed, clipped, purposeful. The rescue team had arrived with ropes, helmets, radios, their orange jackets startling as flowers in the dusk.

A woman called down to him. Was he injured?

“No,” he shouted. “I'm all right.”

They descended with practiced care. Soon the clearing filled with movement. A paramedic knelt where Jonas had knelt, touched the woman’s neck, looked at her watch, said something quietly to another. A police officer asked Jonas his name, his address, what time he had found the vehicle. He answered. The words came from him neatly, as if from a form already filled in.

When they lifted his jacket from the woman's body, he felt unexpectedly exposed. One of the rescuers held it out to him. He took it, folded it though it was dirty, and pressed it under his arm.

“Do you know her?” the officer asked.

Jonas looked past him. A rescuer had brushed the hair back from her face with surprising gentleness.

“No,” he said. Then, after a moment: “I stayed with her.”

The officer nodded. Perhaps he understood; perhaps he was merely noting another fact.

They asked him to wait by the road while they worked. Up there, the night had opened fully. The valley was gone, the peaks reduced to darker shapes against stars. The red car below flashed in fragments between moving bodies and torchlight. Voices rose, fell, were taken by the wind.

Jonas put on his jacket. It smelled of petrol and crushed grass.

He had intended that morning to walk until thought thinned out. Instead thought had thickened around one place, one broken line in the trees. Tomorrow he would descend. He would pass the petrol station, maybe see the woman with red nails, maybe not. He would ride a bus. He would sleep in some room with curtains and a humming radiator. In time he might tell someone this story and watch it change in the telling. In more time he might not tell it at all.

But for now he stood on the road that had not known where it was going. Below him, strangers labored over a stranger. Above him, the mountain kept its immense silence. And within that silence, brief and wavering as breath on glass, her unknown life remained.
