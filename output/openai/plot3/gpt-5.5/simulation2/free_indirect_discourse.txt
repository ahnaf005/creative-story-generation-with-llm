By the time Martin reached the hairpin above Alder Gorge, the sky had lowered itself onto the mountains as if tired of holding any distance. Mist dragged through the black pines. The road was narrow there, little more than a shelf cut into granite, with white posts leaning at irregular intervals along the drop. No cars had passed him for more than an hour. That suited him. He had come this way because the map showed no villages, no cafés, no church spires, nothing but a thin grey line curling up into the high country and down again somewhere beyond the ridge.

He liked roads that seemed to forget people.

At first he mistook the broken rail for storm damage. A section of it bowed outward, twisted, its bolts ripped from the rock. Then he saw, twenty feet below, the red car lying on its side among dwarf birches and boulders, its undercarriage exposed like the belly of a dead beetle. One wheel still turned slightly, or perhaps it only seemed to in the fog. The windshield had burst into glittering fragments. Steam or smoke—he could not tell—rose from the crumpled hood and was flattened by the damp air.

Martin stopped walking.

There was, for one absurd second, an irritation in him. Why here? Why now, in this clean blank stretch of day, when he had nearly succeeded in becoming nobody? Then the thought passed, ashamed of itself, though not before he had recognized it as his own.

He climbed over the torn barrier, stiff knees complaining, and worked his way down the slope. The stones shifted under his boots. Once he slipped and caught a birch trunk, feeling its wet bark peel under his palm. He had never been quick in emergencies. Even as a child he had been the one who stood watching after the plate had fallen, studying the pieces, while his mother snapped, Well, do something, Martin.

He reached the car breathless. The driver’s door was pinned against the ground. The passenger side faced the sky, buckled but accessible. He called out, “Hello? Can you hear me?”

No answer came from inside. Beyond the car, the gorge opened, invisible under fog. Water moved far below, a continuous cold whisper.

He found her folded awkwardly against the steering wheel, though the steering wheel had been driven toward her. She was young, or at least younger than him by several decades, with dark hair loosened from a knot and spread across the torn upholstery. Blood had dried along one temple and at the corner of her mouth. Her eyes were half open. That troubled him most. Not the blood, not the angle of the neck, but the unfinished look of the eyes, as if she had been interrupted while considering something.

There was no pulse in her wrist. He checked because one checked; because television and posters in railway stations had insisted all his life that a decent person checked. Her skin was cooling but not yet cold.

“I’m here,” he said.

The words sounded foolish. Of course he was. And she, wherever she had gone, was not.

His phone had a signal only after he climbed back up to the road and walked fifty yards toward the pass, holding it in the air like an offering. The emergency operator’s voice came and went in pieces. Accident. One vehicle. One person. No, no sign of breathing. Location? He gave the road number, the last milestone he remembered, described the broken rail and the gorge. They were sending someone. It would take time. Weather had closed the western pass, she said. Was he in danger?

Was he? The fog breathed over the road. Somewhere above him rocks clicked, loosened by meltwater or animals or nothing at all. He said he would wait.

When he descended again, he brought his pack. He had a blanket rolled at the bottom, a blue wool thing Anna had bought years ago from a market in Donegal. He hesitated before taking it out. It still held, or he imagined it held, the smell of their hall cupboard: dust, cedar, soap. But what was a blanket for, if not this? He unfolded it and draped it over the woman as best he could, covering her from the shoulders down. He did not cover her face. That seemed both necessary and impossible.

“There,” he said. “That’s better.”

He sat on a flat stone beside the car. The wet seeped quickly through his trousers. He could have waited on the road, where the ambulance would arrive, but leaving her alone felt indecent. Not that she would know. There was the old stupidity again: behaving as though the dead were embarrassed by solitude.

Yet hadn’t he felt it himself? In hotel rooms in cities where no one knew his name, after Anna died, he had sometimes woken convinced that if he vanished in the night the bed would simply be stripped, the room aired, the bill charged to his card. A man could be erased by efficient housekeeping. No, better not to leave her.

“My name is Martin Hale,” he told her. “I was walking to the pass. Not for any good reason.”

Her face remained angled toward the shattered windshield. A small silver earring glinted at her lobe. It was shaped like a leaf. Someone had given it to her, perhaps; someone had watched her put it on that morning. She might have stood before a mirror in a warm room, irritated by a message, late for something, thinking of petrol or work or a quarrel. The mountain had been waiting all the while without waiting.

“You shouldn’t have come off there,” he said, looking up at the torn rail. “But I expect you know that.”

Wind moved through the birches. Drops collected on their thin branches and fell, ticking against the car roof.

He had not spoken this much aloud in days. In the hut two nights before he had said good evening to a German couple and thank you to the woman who sold him bread. His own voice now seemed to belong to a stranger, an older man, not the one he still privately inhabited. That inner man was forty-three, perhaps, or thirty-eight, depending on light: capable of lifting boxes, running for trains, wanting things with an almost comic seriousness. The outer man had hands like roots and a heart that knocked at stairs.

“I used to bring my son walking,” he said.

There it was. He had not intended to begin with Daniel. Then again, every road eventually bent that way.

“He hated it at first. He’d say, Dad, there’s nothing here. As if nothing were the worst thing a place could offer. I’d tell him to listen. There’s the stream, there’s the wind, there’s that bird—what was it? A pipit, maybe. I never knew birds as well as I pretended. Fathers pretend knowledge. It’s in the contract.”

He could see Daniel at twelve, scowling under a red waterproof, cheeks bright with cold. He could see Anna behind them, laughing silently, warning Martin with her eyes not to make a lesson of everything. Let the boy be miserable if he wants. Misery was sometimes the only property children owned outright.

“Later he liked it,” Martin told the woman. “Or said he did. Which is nearly the same, from a child.”

No, not nearly. Nothing said in kindness was the same as truth, though it might be more useful.

The fog thinned briefly, revealing the opposite slope: dark spruce rising and rising until snowfields began, pale and distant. Then the whiteness closed again. The wilderness had a way of appearing not as scenery but as presence. It leaned close, listened without sympathy. It had received the crash and would have received the silence after it, and if no one came it would receive them both—the woman first, then him in some later year—with equal calm.

He wondered who she was. There would be a handbag somewhere in the car, papers, a license with a photograph taken under bad light. He did not search. It felt wrong to rummage among the scattered contents of a life while she watched with those half-open eyes. A paperback lay under the passenger window, pages fanned and wet. A child’s drawing, or perhaps only a folded map, was trapped beneath the dashboard. He hoped it was not a child’s drawing. Let there be no child waiting at a kitchen table for this woman. But hope had no authority over facts.

“My wife was called Anna,” he said. “She would have known what to do better than I do. She always did.”

That was sentimental and untrue. Anna had known what to do in social discomforts, family feuds, kitchens, train stations. In a car wreck she might have panicked as anyone would. But she would have panicked usefully. She would have spoken to the operator in a clear voice; she would have found the blanket before being proud of the sacrifice; she would have taken the dead woman’s hand.

Martin looked at the hand lying near the gearshift. The fingers were slender, nails unpainted, two knuckles grazed. He reached in through the broken window and touched them. Cold now. He withdrew his hand and folded it between his knees.

Anna had died in June, absurdly, among peonies. A vessel in her brain, the doctor said, as if she had been a porcelain basin that had cracked from within. One moment she was in the garden arguing that the roses needed cutting back, the next she was on the grass with one arm beneath her. He remembered the bees continuing. That had offended him—their industry, their golden stupid bodies pushing into flowers while Anna’s mouth opened soundlessly. Later, for months, he could not bear flowers. People sent them anyway. Lilies, roses, chrysanthemums, all of them declaring life and decay in the same breath until the house stank of water gone green.

“You’d have liked her,” he told the woman. “Everyone did. Or everyone says so now. The dead become very agreeable.”

He almost smiled. Anna, agreeable. Anna who returned soup in restaurants, who wrote letters to the council, who once called a bishop a coward to his face at a wedding reception because of something he had said in a sermon. But memory sanded people down unless one resisted it. He tried to resist. He owed her the grain of herself.

From the road above came the faintest sound: not sirens, only a lorry engine far away, perhaps on another road entirely. Then nothing.

He checked his watch. Twenty-seven minutes since the call. Time had become both thick and slippery. He thought of the woman’s morning. Had it been only an hour ago that she was alive? Two? She had made decisions, each one as weightless as a seed: turn left, answer later, take the mountain road, hurry, don’t hurry. Then the skid, the rail, the brief terrible education of gravity. And now all those decisions had hardened into one fact.

How unfair, that a life should be revised backward by its ending. If a man died falling from a ladder, people remembered the ladder as destiny. If a woman crashed on a mountain road, every bend behind her became an omen. But while living, one moved through ordinary weather. One did not hear the orchestra tuning beneath breakfast.

He had thought, after Daniel’s departure, that the worst part of losing a child to distance was not grief but administrative uncertainty. There was no funeral, no certificate, no sanctioned mourning. Daniel had not died; he had merely taken his wife and little girls to New Zealand, following work, following light, following a version of himself in which Martin occupied less space. They spoke on holidays, sometimes on Sundays. The girls called him Granddad with the bright politeness of strangers. He could not blame them. Affection required maintenance, and the world was large.

Anna used to say, Go visit him. Martin would say, It’s too far. Too expensive. Too long in the air. What he meant was: Suppose I go and find I am unnecessary there too?

“Cowardice looks very like practicality from the inside,” he said.

The woman’s earring caught the dim light again. Leaf-shaped. He wondered if she had a mother who would soon receive a call. Or a husband. A sister. A friend who had been expecting her for dinner and had already chopped onions, already set out two glasses. Somewhere, perhaps, a phone would ring in an ordinary room, and the mountain would enter it.

He told her about the summer he and Anna had rented a cottage by a lake because they could not afford the sea. Daniel had been seven. It rained for twelve days. They played cards, badly. Daniel accused both parents of cheating, then cheated so obviously that Anna fell off her chair laughing. The cottage smelled of damp wool and frying onions; a mouse lived behind the stove; the lake was grey as tin. At the time Martin had thought it a failed holiday. He had apologized to Anna on the drive home. She had looked at him as if he were simple. Failed? We were happy, weren’t we?

They had been. He knew it now, which was the usual cruelty. Happiness rarely announced itself. It sat in corners, quiet, and only after it had gone did one recognize its face.

“I’m sorry,” he said to the woman, though he did not know for what.

The mist darkened. Evening was beginning its slow gathering, though it could not be later than four. The mountains did not care for clocks. He put on his hat and zipped his jacket to the throat. The cold had found the sweat beneath his shirt. He imagined the ambulance crew descending, brisk and competent, their boots dislodging stones. They would ask questions. He would answer. They would cover her face. That would be the moment, perhaps, when she fully became dead to him. For now she was a listener.

A raven came out of the fog and settled on a broken pine above the car. It cocked its head, black eye polished and exact.

“Go on,” Martin said.

The raven remained.

Of course it would. This was its country, not his. He was the trespasser with his sentimental blanket and his stories. The wilderness wasted nothing. It made no distinction between tragedy and weather, between a woman and a deer, between the blood on a steering wheel and berries ripening under snow. Once he would have found that brutal. Now he was not sure. Human tenderness was precious because it was temporary, local, almost ridiculous. A hand over a cold hand. A voice beside a wreck. What could be smaller? What could be more defiant?

He began to tell her about Anna’s last morning, then stopped. No. Not the collapse, not the hospital, not the machines performing their bright, useless rituals. He told instead about Anna at twenty-six, before marriage, before Daniel, standing on a railway platform in a yellow coat. He had been late. He was always late then, believing time would stretch for him. She had threatened to leave without him, and when he arrived running, full of apologies, she had said, I waited, didn’t I? But don’t make a theology of it.

He had loved her from that sentence, or later decided he had. Memory liked beginnings as much as endings. It shaped the shapeless middle into paths that could be walked again.

A sound rose at last from above: the thin, wavering cry of a siren, caught and broken by the gorge. Martin stood too quickly and his hip sent a hot spark down his leg. The raven lifted away without hurry.

“They’re coming,” he said.

He looked at the woman and felt a strange reluctance. When the others arrived, she would belong to procedures, to forms, to the people who knew her name. His vigil would become a footnote: elderly walker discovered scene, remained until emergency services arrived. That was all right. That was proper. Still, for this suspended hour, she had been entrusted to him, and he to her. He had spoken into the last warmth of a stranger and heard, in the silence, the echo of his own life returning altered.

The siren grew louder, then stopped somewhere on the road above. Doors slammed. Voices called.

Martin leaned close, not touching her.

“I hope you weren’t afraid for long,” he said. “I hope there was something kind at the end, even if it was only the fog.”

A helmet appeared over the edge of the road, then another. Men and women in bright jackets began descending with ropes and bags, bringing human urgency into the wet grey hollow. One of them asked if he was hurt. Martin shook his head. Another moved past him to the car, gloved hand already reaching for the woman’s neck though there was no need, no need at all, except that one checked.

Martin stepped back. His blanket was lifted, adjusted, replaced. Questions came. What time had he found her? Had he moved her? Had he seen anyone else? He answered as well as he could. His voice had become ordinary again.

Above them, the fog parted for a moment. High on the slope, snow shone with an almost evening blue. Then cloud covered it, and the mountain resumed its hidden shape.

When they told him he could go up to the road, someone offered an arm. He accepted. Pride was another temporary thing, and not the best of them. At the top, he looked once over his shoulder. The red car was half concealed by birches and bright jackets. The gorge whispered below, patient as ever.

Martin thought of the pass ahead, the village beyond, the small inn where he might find a room. He thought of calling Daniel—not on Sunday, not at Christmas, but now, from this wet road with his hands shaking. He could say very little. He could say, I was walking in the mountains and something happened. He could say, How are the girls? He could say, I have been foolish. Or perhaps he would say none of that and only listen to the delay across oceans, that tiny gap in which a life could still change direction.

A paramedic asked again if he was sure he was all right.

“Yes,” Martin said, and then, because accuracy mattered, “No. But well enough.”

He began walking toward the ambulance lights, which flashed red against the fog and vanished, flashed and vanished, as if the world itself were remembering and forgetting them by turns.
