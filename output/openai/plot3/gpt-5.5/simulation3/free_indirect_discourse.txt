The road had no business being there, he thought, though of course that was not true. It had been blasted out of the mountain long before he first came to these parts, a narrow black ribbon laid across scree and heather, slung from one valley to the next with the arrogance of men who believed in arrivals. Still, seen from the footpath above, where the peat gave under his boots and the wind worried at his coat, it looked temporary. A line drawn in charcoal, waiting for rain.

Martin had been walking since dawn. He had told himself, as he often did, that the early start would give him the hills to himself, as if they were something a man could possess by being punctual. The village had still been shuttered when he left: the pub sign creaking, a van ticking cold by the bakery, the church clock showing the wrong time because nobody had yet bothered to climb the tower and set it right. He had enjoyed that, the wrongness of it. Proof that time did not care for clocks. Or perhaps clocks did not care for time.

Now, in the afternoon, the light had thinned to pewter. The ridges stood in ranks, each one paler than the last, until the farthest dissolved into cloud. Somewhere below him, hidden among the folds of the land, water moved over stones with a sound like paper being crumpled. He had not met another walker all day, which pleased him and then, as the hours lengthened, began not to please him. Solitude, like whisky, was best measured carefully. Too much of it turned medicinal.

He saw the gap in the crash barrier first.

It was not much, just a place where the pale rail kinked outward and vanished, one of its posts lifted like a broken tooth. Then he saw the marks on the road: two dark curves, crossing and correcting and failing to correct. Beyond the barrier, down a steep run of bracken and loose shale, something red showed among the birches.

For a moment he stood still.

There were explanations. A scrap of tarpaulin, a farmer’s trailer, a child’s toy balloon escaped from a birthday and come to grief in the wilds. But no, there was glass glittering on the tarmac, little cubes of safety glass sprinkled in the lane like frost, and on the air a smell he knew from old petrol stations and his father’s garage: fuel, hot metal, the sour breath of ruptured things.

He scrambled down from the path toward the road, sliding twice, one hand out to save himself. His knee protested. Not a young man, was he, though he was still surprised by that fact each time the body announced it. Sixty-two last March. Or had he stopped counting properly after Ellen? No, sixty-two; there had been a card from Claire with bluebells on it and three lines written in the haste of a woman with children and a job and her own sorrows, none of which were his fault, though once he had behaved as if they were.

At the broken barrier he paused, breathing hard. The car lay fifteen yards below, nose-first against a birch, its rear wheels lifted slightly, as if it had tried to climb the tree. One door had sprung open. Steam, or smoke, drifted faintly from under the bonnet and was torn away by the wind. He listened for movement, for crying, for the desperate animal noise of someone trapped and alive.

Nothing.

“Hello?” he called.

His voice went out thinly and did not come back.

He had to go down. Obviously he had to go down. There was no useful thought to be had on the road, only the childish wish to be a passerby in a story told by someone else. He picked his way down the slope, grabbing at birch saplings and tufts of heather. Stones rattled beneath his boots and dropped away. The car’s red paint was scratched white, the windscreen cracked into a blind, glittering flower. A woman was inside, half turned toward the open door, seat belt twisted across her chest.

He said again, “Hello,” but softly this time, as if entering a room where someone slept.

Her face was pale, marked by a little blood at the temple. Her hair, brown and loosened from whatever had held it, lay across one cheek. She was perhaps thirty-five, perhaps forty. Age was difficult on the dead; he knew that from his mother in the chapel of rest, where the mortician had made her both older and younger than she had ever been. The woman’s eyes were closed. One hand hung down near the gear stick, fingers lightly curled, not gripping anything. A wedding ring shone on her left hand.

Martin leaned in and smelled blood, petrol, the powdery sweetness of the exploded airbag. He touched two fingers to her neck, clumsily, as he had seen done on television. There was no pulse he could find. But did he know where to look? He pressed again, then against her wrist. Nothing. He thought of moving her, of pulling her free, of doing the heroic thing, but the shape of the car and the stillness of her body warned him away. Her chest did not rise. There was no faint mist from her mouth in the cold air.

Phone.

He patted his pockets with ridiculous urgency. Map, chocolate, gloves, the folded sandwich he no longer wanted. The phone was in the inside pocket of his coat. One bar of signal flickered, then vanished, then returned as a single trembling line. He climbed back toward the road until the signal held long enough to dial. Emergency. Which service? Ambulance, police, fire—yes, all of them, perhaps all of them. He gave the road number, or thought he did. He described the bend below the old quarry, the broken barrier, the red car down the slope. The operator’s voice remained calm, exact, human in the way a lighthouse was human: not intimate, but made for saving strangers.

“Is the casualty breathing?”

“No,” he said. Then, after a pause, “I don’t think so.”

“Can you check?”

“I have. I did. I can again.”

“Do not put yourself at risk. Help is on the way.”

Help is on the way. Such a phrase, so certain of itself. As if help were a dog running over the moor, as if all distance were merely a matter of patience.

He returned to the car. The woman had not changed. Why should she have? He sat on a rock beside the open door, close enough that if she had woken, if she had been only deeply hidden inside herself, she would not wake alone.

“I’ve called them,” he said.

The wind moved through the birches. A hooded crow lifted from the slope opposite, black wings beating slowly, offended by his arrival.

He had never sat with a dead stranger before. With his father, yes, though not at the moment of dying. That had happened in the hospital at 3:17 in the morning, according to the nurse, while Martin was asleep in a plastic chair with his mouth open and a magazine sliding from his lap. His father, who had disliked lateness in all forms, had slipped out between one breath and the next without waiting for his son to witness it. Afterwards Martin had stood by the bed feeling cheated, though whether on his own behalf or his father’s he could not say. The body had already become an object requiring decisions. Ring the relatives. Choose a funeral director. Find the suit.

“You see,” he told the woman, “they don’t tell you that part. How quickly the world starts making arrangements.”

He should not talk too much. It was absurd. But the silence pressed against him, and the woman, whose name he did not know, seemed entitled to something more than the ticking engine and the crows. He looked away from her face toward the hills. Mist gathered along the ridge, flowing like milk over a lip of stone.

“When I was a boy,” he said, “my father brought me up here. Not here exactly. Over there, beyond that pass. He had an old canvas tent that smelled of mildew and paraffin. We boiled tea in a tin pot and he burned the sausages. I thought it was the finest meal ever cooked.”

Had he? Or had he been cold and bored and frightened of the dark, and only later made happiness from the fragments? Memory had a talent for forgery. His father had sung in the night, quietly, a song from the war years, though he had been too young for the war and had acquired its songs secondhand like everything else. Martin remembered the curve of the man’s back against the lantern light. He remembered wanting to ask whether mountains were alive, but not asking because boys learned early which questions could be allowed into the air.

“They seemed permanent then,” he said. “The hills, I mean. And him.”

A gust came hard down the gully, rattling the birch branches together. The open car door gave a small metallic creak. He stood and looked into the back seat. A blue scarf. A paperback facedown on the floor. A plastic water bottle, flattened at one side. On the passenger seat lay a handbag, spilled open. Keys, lipstick, a packet of mints, a receipt. No child seat, thank God. He had been afraid of that without knowing it. Afraid of looking and finding another silence.

He did not search for her name. It felt like theft. Besides, once he knew it, what would that change? She would become particular and therefore heavier. Yet she was already particular: the fine lines beside her closed eyes, the small silver stud in one ear, the mud on the heel of one boot. She had been going somewhere. Everyone was always going somewhere, right up to the instant when the sentence ended.

He sat again.

The operator had asked him to stay if it was safe. Was it safe? The car might catch fire. The slope might give way. Weather might come down. But he knew he would not leave. It was not virtue. Virtue sounded too polished for the feeling in him. It was stubbornness, perhaps, or the fear that if he climbed back to the road and waited there, she would vanish into a category: casualty, female, one deceased at scene. Here, she was a woman in a red car among birches, and he was Martin Vale, retired teacher, widower, father of one daughter who rang on Sundays when she remembered, and he could offer only the warmth of his continued presence.

He told her about Ellen.

Not at once. He began with the safe things: the town where he had taught history for thirty-four years; the boys who drew tanks in the margins of their exam papers; the girl who had once written that Henry VIII broke from Rome because he fancied interior design and wanted new curtains. He laughed at that, and the sound shocked him. Laughter here? But the hills did not object. The dead woman did not object.

“Ellen would have liked that one,” he said.

Ellen had laughed easily. Too easily, he used to think when he was young and solemn and sure that seriousness was a form of intelligence. She had laughed at dogs slipping on ice, at badly translated menus, at herself most of all. In photographs she was often blurred, turning toward someone outside the frame. He had loved her first for that brightness and later resented it, as men resent the very quality that drew them close because it reveals their own poverty.

When the cancer came, she laughed less. Not never. Less. There were months of appointments, charts, hopeful words spoken in careful voices. Disease taught its own geography: oncology, pharmacy, car park level C, the corridor with the vending machine that ate pound coins. He would drive her home along the bypass and she would look at the warehouses, the roundabouts, the wet fields, as if all of it had been newly made.

“One day,” Martin said, “she told me the worst part wasn’t dying. It was leaving halfway through everything. Bread in the freezer. A book on the bedside table. The garden needing weeding.”

He had told her that the garden could go hang, that he would finish the book aloud if she wanted, that there was nothing halfway about them. What nonsense. Kind nonsense, perhaps, but nonsense all the same. Everything was halfway. Marriage, grief, the washing up. Even the mountains were halfway through their long collapse into the sea.

A sound rose faintly from the valley. He lifted his head. Wind? No, perhaps an engine. It faded, returned, faded again. Too soon? Not soon enough. He checked his watch. Twenty-eight minutes since the call. Only twenty-eight. It seemed ungenerous of time to move so slowly beside the dead when it had hurried so brutally for the living.

The light was lowering. Shadows collected under the trees, and the temperature dropped with that sudden mountain decisiveness which suggested the day had been tolerating warmth rather than providing it. He took off his rucksack and pulled out his wool hat. For a moment he considered placing it over the woman’s hands, then felt foolish. She was beyond cold. Still, dignity had strange customs. He laid the hat gently across her lap.

“There,” he said. “Borrow it as long as you like.”

He imagined her waking and finding a stranger’s hat on her knees. She would think him odd. She might say, very reasonably, Who are you? And he would say, Nobody much, I was passing. A whole life could be reduced to that. Passing.

The wilderness gathered around them, though he knew that word was theatrical. There was a road above, sheep on the higher pasture, a hydro line beyond the ridge. Yet the place felt old in a way that made human works seem provisional. The birches, thin and silver, clung where they could. Lichen bloomed on rock with patient hunger. The mountain did not threaten exactly. It loomed because it could not do otherwise. It had no opinion of red cars, broken rails, husbands waiting at home, phones ringing unanswered on kitchen counters. That was its terror and its mercy.

He thought of Claire at eight years old, running ahead on a beach with a yellow bucket. He had shouted, Not so far, and she had turned, annoyed by his fear. Children belonged to the future and therefore found caution insulting. Later, at sixteen, she had accused him of being absent even when present, which was clever and cruel because it was true. He had been thinking always of work, money, the leak in the roof, Ellen’s appointment, the next necessary thing. He had missed the actual life while guarding its perimeter.

If this woman had a daughter, someone might be angry with her for being late. Someone might be composing a message—Where are you?—with irritation still intact, blessed irritation, not yet converted into dread.

“I hope they were kind to you this morning,” he said.

This seemed important. That she had not left a house full of sharp words. That no one had let her go with a slammed door or a muttered complaint. But of course people did. They left that way all the time, carrying ordinary wounds into extraordinary endings.

The engine sound came again, closer. Then a siren, thin at first, winding through the valley. Martin stood too quickly and his knee flared. He climbed the slope toward the road, slipping, grabbing, cursing under his breath. Halfway up, he looked back. From above, the car seemed smaller, almost toy-like. The woman’s brown hair was visible through the open door.

“Still here,” he called, though whether to her or himself he did not know.

At the roadside he waved as the first vehicle rounded the bend: police, then an ambulance behind it, blue lights startling in the grey afternoon. Doors opened. People in bright jackets stepped out with bags and equipment and competent faces. He pointed down. Words came from him in clumps: one occupant, female, no breathing, called twenty-eight—no, thirty-six minutes ago, unstable slope, fuel smell. They thanked him. One officer asked if he was injured. He said no, though his hands were shaking.

They went down to her. Not hurriedly now, not with the urgent hope of rescue, but with practiced respect. One paramedic glanced back up and shook his head slightly to the police officer. Martin saw the gesture and felt, absurdly, that it confirmed his failure. As if he had been keeping watch over a candle and allowed it to go out before they arrived, though it had been out already; he knew that.

The officer, a young woman with rain on her eyelashes, asked for his details. He gave them. She wrote in a notebook. Had he touched anything? Her neck, wrist. The hat—he stopped. My hat is on her lap, he said, embarrassed. The officer looked at him for a second, then nodded as if this were not strange at all.

“You did the right thing staying with her,” she said.

Had he? The phrase entered him but found no place to settle. The right thing. There were so many right things a man failed to do until the occasion became simple enough: stay or leave, speak or be silent, hold or let go. Most of life was not like that. Most of life was fog over a pass.

They told him someone would drive him back to the village. He said he could walk. They said no, the weather was coming in. He looked toward the ridge and saw cloud descending, the mountain drawing a grey curtain over itself. Already the road was damp with the first fine rain. The broken glass shone and dimmed, shone and dimmed.

Before they led him to the police car, he turned once more toward the slope. The responders moved below among the birches, bright as autumn leaves. The red car rested against the tree. For a little while, this scene would be everything: radio calls, measurements, photographs, forms, a name spoken carefully into a phone. Then the car would be hauled up, the glass swept aside, the barrier replaced. Rain would soften the tyre marks. Spring would put green fire through the bracken. Walkers would pass above and complain of midges, or praise the view, or think of nothing at all.

That was not cruelty. Or if it was, it was the same cruelty that allowed grief to loosen its grip, children to grow, bread to be baked after funerals, clocks to be corrected in church towers. The world did not stop because it could not. Stopping was for hearts, engines, watches left in drawers.

In the back of the police car, wrapped in a foil blanket he did not need, Martin watched the mountains withdraw into mist. He had the odd sensation that he had forgotten something, and then remembered: his hat. For a moment he nearly mentioned it. Then he pictured it there, warming nothing, a small dark bundle in the lap of a woman whose name he would perhaps read in the paper, or perhaps never know.

Let her keep it, he thought.

The village lights appeared below, scattered and frail. Behind him, beyond the bend and the broken rail, the wilderness resumed its long, indifferent breathing. Martin closed his eyes and listened to the sirenless car carry him down from the hills, passing through the weather, passing through what remained of the day.
