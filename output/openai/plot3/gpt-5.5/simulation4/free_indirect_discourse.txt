At the first bend above the timberline, where the road narrowed and the mountain shouldered close to the drop, Elias heard the ticking.

Not a bird, not water. A small, patient ticking, like a watch under a pillow.

He had been walking since dawn with no destination more exact than the pass. That was how he preferred it: a pack light enough not to mean anything, boots old enough to know his feet, the valley falling away behind him as if it had never held a town, a bed, a name. The air was thin and bright. Marmots had whistled at him from the scree. Once, far below, he had seen a red mail van crawling along the lower road, and the sight of it—so purposeful, so insectlike—had made him smile. People were always carrying messages to one another. Bills, apologies, invitations, test results. As if any of it arrived in time.

The ticking continued.

He rounded the bend and saw the broken guardrail first. Two posts bent outward, the cable snapped and curled like a tendon. Beyond it, down the slope among dwarf pines and shattered rock, lay a car on its side. Pale blue, or perhaps silver before dust and pine needles had claimed it. One wheel turned slowly in the air. Tick, tick, tick. Metal cooling. Fluids dropping onto stone.

For a moment he stood with his hands still looped through the straps of his pack. The scene had an impossible neatness, as accidents sometimes did: the road empty, the mountains immense, the car placed there below as if for his instruction.

Then he scrambled down.

His boots slid on gravel. A stone clattered past him into the brush. He called out before he could see inside.

“Hello? Can you hear me?”

His voice struck the slope and came back thinly.

The driver’s door was crushed against the ground. The passenger side faced the sky. Glass starred the remaining window; the windshield had folded inward in a greenish veil. He climbed around the back, bracing one hand on the warm metal. The smell of petrol was sharp enough to sting his nose. He should not touch anything, should he? There were rules. There had always been rules, appearing the instant after they might have helped.

Inside, a woman hung at an angle from her seat belt. Her head rested against the deflated airbag. Dark hair, cut just below the jaw. A scarf, yellow with white dots, twisted at her throat. Her eyes were closed.

“Madam?” he said, absurdly formal.

No answer. Of course no answer? No, not of course. He had not earned that knowledge yet.

He pressed two fingers below her jaw, where he remembered the pulse should be. Her skin was warm from the enclosed heat of the car but already slack in a way that made his hand recoil inside itself. He felt nothing. Tried again. Nothing but his own pulse in his fingertips, eager and fraudulent.

The phone in his pocket had one bar, then none, then one again. He climbed back to the road, lifted it toward the sky like an offering, walked ten paces, twenty, cursed softly, found a place near the shattered guardrail where the emergency number rang. The operator’s voice was calm, almost bored with disaster. Location? He gave the road number from the sign he had passed an hour ago, the approximate distance, the bend before the old avalanche gallery. How many injured? One vehicle, one person, unresponsive. Was he sure she was not breathing? He looked down the slope, at the pale car nested in scrub. He was not sure of anything. He had once been sure of plenty—his wife’s laugh in the next room, his son’s hand closing around his thumb, the soundness of a bridge he designed thirty years ago—but those certainties had gone the way snow went in April, revealing mud.

“I don’t think she is,” he said.

Stay nearby if safe. Do not move the victim unless there is immediate danger. Help is on the way.

On the way. Everything was always on the way until it wasn’t.

He went back down to her.

The ticking had slowed. The wheel no longer turned. A fly discovered the broken window and circled, tentative and officious. Elias waved it away.

“I’ve called,” he told her. “They’re coming.”

The woman did not look young exactly, nor old. Thirty-five? Forty? There was a thin silver ring on her right hand, a smudge of something—ink? grease?—near her wrist. On the passenger seat, half spilled from a canvas bag, were oranges, a paperback novel swollen with use, and a small wooden horse painted red. A toy? A charm? It lay on the ceiling now, which had become the floor.

He sat on a rock beside the car. It seemed indecent to stand over her. The sky above the peaks was a hard enamel blue. Somewhere higher up, unseen, the wind dragged itself through stone.

“You won’t mind if I talk,” he said. “People say that hearing is the last to go. They say many things.”

He had not meant to begin with bitterness. A dead woman deserved better company than that. He cleared his throat, as if addressing a room.

“My name is Elias Venn. I’m sixty-eight. I used to build bridges. Not with my hands, mostly. With drawings. Calculations. Men in helmets did the better work.”

He glanced at her face. The yellow scarf had shifted when he moved near her; he wanted to untwist it, smooth it, but did not.

“There was a bridge in Kettering,” he continued. “A little pedestrian thing over the river behind the mill. Nothing grand. Children crossed it to school. Lovers carved initials underneath, though how they got there without breaking their necks I never knew. It lasted twenty-two years before the council replaced it with something uglier and safer. I went to see it taken down.”

Why was he telling this? Because silence had teeth. Because the mountain pressed close, listening with no mercy at all.

“They cut it apart in sections. My bridge. A crane lifted each piece like a bone from a body. I thought I’d be sadder. Instead I kept wondering whether the initials would go to scrap with the steel. J plus M. A heart. Someone’s forever sold by weight.”

He laughed once, but the sound was wrong here.

The woman’s hair moved slightly in the draft coming through the broken glass. If he squinted, she might be asleep after a long party, tired of music and talk. Wake up, he thought, and I will be embarrassed for having said all this. Wake up and I will apologize, and you can be angry, and anger would be a luxury spread before them like a feast.

No movement.

He checked his phone. No signal again. Time had become lumpy. Ten minutes might have passed, or two.

He told her about his wife next. Miriam. It surprised him that he could say the name aloud without the old inward flinch.

“She hated the mountains,” he said. “Said they were theatrical. ‘All that looming,’ she’d say. ‘As if height were a personality.’ She preferred marshland. Flat places. Reeds, birds, water thinking about being sky.”

Miriam had worn green dresses and read crime novels backward, the last chapter first, because she did not like suspense. She had kept basil on every windowsill and killed every plant except basil, which flourished under her neglect as if grateful not to be fussed over. When their son, Daniel, was small, she invented songs for ordinary tasks: buttoning coats, pouring milk, searching for lost socks. Elias had pretended to find this charmingly ridiculous. In truth he had depended on it. Her songs proved the world could be made bearable if one named its chores brightly enough.

“She died in February,” he told the woman. “Years ago now. A stupid month to die. The ground too hard. Flowers too expensive.”

Cancer, though the word seemed both too large and too small. There had been appointments, pamphlets, cheerful nurses, silent drives home. There had been hope, naturally; hope arrived automatically, like a receipt printed after a purchase one regretted. Miriam had refused certain treatments near the end. Enough, she said. Her voice was so calm that he had become angry with her for leaving gracefully, as if death were a host she did not wish to offend.

And then Daniel—no, perhaps not Daniel yet. One did not thrust all one’s dead upon a stranger at once.

A raven crossed the sky, black and decisive. It landed on a pine above them and folded itself into watching. Elias picked up a pebble and threw it poorly; the bird hopped aside but did not leave.

“Go on,” he muttered.

The mountain gave back no answer. It had accepted car, woman, man, raven, petrol smell, emergency call, all with the same indifference. In the valley, people were surely buying bread, reversing from parking spaces, taking photographs of menus. A kettle whistled somewhere. A child refused shoes. The whole machinery continued, ignorant of this stopped vehicle under the road. That was the insult and the mercy.

He noticed a smear of blood at the woman’s temple. Not much. Less than he would have imagined death required. He wanted to know her name.

There might be a handbag within reach. But should he search? The operator had said not to disturb. Still, a name was not disturbance; a name was the smallest shelter. He leaned carefully through the broken passenger window, avoiding the glass teeth, and drew the canvas bag closer by its strap. Oranges rolled against one another with soft thuds. The paperback was a book of poems in translation. The wooden horse stared upward with painted blue eyes.

In a side pocket he found a wallet. Driver’s license.

“Clara Meisner,” he read. “Well, Clara.”

The name altered her immediately. She was no longer the woman, the victim, the driver. Clara. There had been people who said it impatiently, tenderly, across kitchens, at office doors, into telephones. Clara, you forgot your coat. Clara, wait. Clara, I’m sorry. Clara, are you coming home?

“I’m Elias,” he said again. “In case you missed it.”

He put the wallet back where he had found it and sat down.

Clouds gathered behind the western ridge, building themselves out of nothing. In mountains, weather did not approach so much as reveal that it had been present all along. The air cooled. He zipped his jacket.

Daniel came then, because Daniel always came when there was weather.

“My son drove too fast,” Elias said. “That is not the whole truth, but it is a truth with handles. People prefer handles.”

Daniel at nineteen had been all wrists and opinions, a boy elongated suddenly into a man and angry at everyone for noticing the fit was poor. He loved cars. Elias, engineer of load and stress, had lectured him on braking distance, reaction time, wet roads, black ice. Daniel had nodded with exquisite boredom. Of course, Dad. Naturally, Dad. The universe, Dad, will file your concern in the appropriate folder.

A summer night. Not mountains; a country lane lined with hedges. Another car coming the other way, a fox perhaps, a swerve, a tree. The policewoman had removed her hat before speaking, and he remembered hating that gesture. Politeness was unbearable at the edge of ruin.

“Miriam was alive then,” he said. “For three years after, though alive is a broad word. We moved around the house like people in a museum of ourselves. Daniel’s shoes by the back door. Daniel’s mug. Daniel’s idiotic poster of a band he claimed I was too old to understand, as if age were the difficulty.”

He had not cried when the coffin lowered. He had counted the straps, the lowering mechanism, the angle of the grave’s sides. Later he wept because he found one of Daniel’s socks inside the lining of the washing machine, absurdly preserved, smelling faintly of damp cotton and nothing else. The great events were too great; only the small ones got through.

Clara listened with the patience of the dead.

“I was not kind afterward,” he admitted. “To Miriam. To anyone. I mistook grief for intelligence. It’s common, I think. Sorrow makes one feel profound. Mostly it makes one rude.”

A sound rose from below the road: wind moving up the ravine, combing the pines. The raven finally lifted away, displeased. Elias looked toward the bend, expecting flashing lights. Nothing. Only the empty road, the broken rail, his own pack leaning against a post.

He imagined Clara before the crash. Hands on the wheel, perhaps singing, perhaps thinking of the errand ahead. The oranges suggested a visit. The wooden horse suggested a child. Or no, he was arranging her too neatly, building a bridge from scraps and pretending it led somewhere. She might have bought oranges for herself. She might collect wooden horses. She might hate children. The dead deserved not to be simplified, though the living could hardly resist.

“Were you afraid?” he asked her.

The question entered the car and stayed there.

He hoped it had been quick. Everyone hoped that, as if speed redeemed violence. He hoped she had looked at the mountains and thought them beautiful, or had not seen them at all. He hoped there had been no instant of understanding, no terrible arithmetic: guardrail, drop, stone, end.

The first drops of rain struck the car roof. Widely spaced, dark coins blooming on dust. Elias pulled the emergency blanket from his pack. He hesitated. Then, carefully, he draped it over the broken window opening, tucking one edge under a strip of torn metal so the rain would not fall directly onto her face. The blanket crackled and shone silver, ridiculous and ceremonial.

“There,” he said. “Not much.”

The rain thickened, soft at first, then earnest. The smell of petrol faded beneath wet pine and stone. Water ran in threads down the slope. He moved closer beneath a jutting rock but kept within arm’s reach of the car. Stay nearby. Do not move the victim. Help is on the way.

He began telling Clara things he had not told anyone because no one had asked and because he had made a life in which asking was discouraged. How, after Miriam died, he sold the house and kept only two boxes: photographs and documents. How the photographs remained unopened in storage while the documents traveled with him, passport, insurance papers, proof that he officially existed. How he walked because movement resembled purpose from a distance. How in villages he ate soup among strangers and invented destinations if they questioned him. The pilgrimage route, he would say. The old military road. A cousin in the north. Lies harmless enough, almost polite.

“Truth makes demands,” he told Clara. “Lies often let everyone finish their soup.”

Thunder rolled somewhere beyond the ridge. Not close. A grumble of old machinery.

He thought of impermanence, though not in the grand way books did. Not cherry blossoms, not candle flames. Impermanence was a receipt fading in a coat pocket. It was the way Miriam’s handwriting still existed on recipe cards though he could no longer hear precisely how she said his name. It was a bridge cut apart, a sock in a machine, a woman named Clara carrying oranges through the mountains and then not carrying them. It was this rain, which would pass; this petrol smell, which would dissipate; this grief, which would not disappear but would change its clothes until one greeted it by mistake as something else.

Perhaps nothing vanished. Perhaps everything merely lost its shape.

The siren came so faintly he mistook it for another bird. Then again, rising through the rain, bending around the mountain road. Blue light flickered against wet rock at the curve above.

Elias stood. His knees hurt; he had been sitting longer than he knew. Two paramedics descended with equipment, followed by a police officer in a yellow jacket. Their competence filled the slope, brisk and merciful. Questions came. Was he the caller? Had he touched her? Had there been anyone else? He answered. His voice sounded remote, borrowed.

One paramedic leaned into the car, fingers at Clara’s neck as Elias had done, then looked to the other and gave the smallest shake of the head. So it became official, then. Death required witnesses, paperwork, a practiced nod.

The police officer guided Elias up to the road. “You did the right thing staying with her,” she said.

Had he? The right thing was so often merely the thing left to do. Still, he nodded.

At the roadside, he picked up his pack. Rain dripped from his nose, his sleeves, the broken guardrail. Below, they worked around Clara with straps and tarps and quiet voices. The silver emergency blanket he had placed over the window lay folded back, bright as fish skin.

“Do you need a lift somewhere?” the officer asked.

He looked up the road toward the pass. Mist had descended, erasing the peaks one by one. The wilderness, having loomed all afternoon, now withdrew behind its own breath. Soon the bend would show only tire marks, then not even that. New posts would be installed. Cars would slow for a week, perhaps two. Somewhere a phone would ring in a room where Clara was expected. Somewhere a person would hear their own life divide.

“No,” Elias said. “Thank you. I’ll walk.”

The officer frowned, but people often frowned at what they could not prevent.

He started toward the pass. After a dozen steps he stopped and looked back. The blue lights pulsed through rain and mist, not urgent now, almost gentle. He lifted one hand, though to whom he could not have said.

Then he walked on, carrying nothing of Clara but her name, which was not nothing, and the road curved ahead into cloud.
