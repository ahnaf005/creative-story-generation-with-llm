The road had been empty all morning.

It ran like a careless pencil mark through the high country, beginning somewhere beyond the last village and vanishing, without apology, into the shale-gray shoulders of the mountains. Snow still clung to the shaded gullies though it was late May, and above the road the peaks stood austere and indifferent, their ridges sharpened by wind. On either side, larch trees grew thinly out of the slope, twisted by weather, their new needles soft and green against the stone.

Elias had been walking since dawn.

He walked because he had long ago learned that if he stopped for too many days, his thoughts gathered weight. In town, the rooms were too square, the clocks too loud, the faces too full of questions they were too polite to ask. In the mountains, no one asked anything. The marmots whistled and vanished. The ravens laughed from invisible perches. The wind arrived, took what it wanted from him, and passed on.

He carried a rucksack with bread, cheese, a tin cup, a small stove, and an old blue sleeping bag rolled tight beneath it. A walking stick clicked at intervals on the broken edge of the road. He had no destination particular enough to name. There was an old shepherd’s hut, maybe, if it still stood where the map suggested. Beyond it, another pass. Beyond the pass, another valley.

A man could spend a lifetime walking from one beyond to the next and never arrive anywhere final.

It was near noon when he saw the crows.

At first they were only black flecks rising and settling beyond a bend in the road. Then he heard the sound of something ticking in the silence—not a clock, but metal cooling. He smelled hot oil and the bitter green scent of crushed pine.

He rounded the bend and saw the car.

It had left the road where the gravel shoulder gave way to a steep decline, then plunged nose-first into a stand of young firs twenty meters below. The vehicle lay tilted against the trunks, its rear wheels half in the air, one still turning lazily. The windshield was a white burst. Steam threaded up from beneath the hood and vanished in the thin mountain air.

For a moment Elias stood still.

There are sights the mind refuses to assemble. A red car. Broken glass. A scarf caught in a branch. One headlight shining though the sun was high. The road above intact, empty, bearing no memory of what had happened.

Then he dropped his stick and climbed down.

The slope was loose with scree. Twice he slipped, tearing the skin of his palm on stone. He called out as he went, first in the formal voice of a stranger—“Hello? Can you hear me?”—then louder, less controlled. No answer came but the faint hiss of the radiator and the crows complaining from the trees.

The driver’s door was folded inward. He could see her through the broken window.

She was young, or seemed young to him, perhaps thirty, perhaps less. Dark hair lay across her cheek. There was blood on her temple, already dulling. Her hands rested strangely in her lap, palms upward, as if she had been interrupted while asking for something. One of her shoes had come off. It lay beneath the brake pedal, absurdly neat.

Elias knew before he touched her wrist.

Still, he touched it. He pressed two fingers below the hinge of her jaw. He waited. He moved his fingers and waited again. In that small pocket of time, the mountains widened around him. He could hear a distant waterfall, unseen beyond the trees. He could hear his own breathing. He could hear nothing from her.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

The words fell out of him. They seemed both necessary and useless.

His phone had no signal where the car lay, so he climbed back to the road, slipping and swearing under his breath. At the bend there was one thin bar, then none, then one again when he raised the phone above his head like an offering. The emergency operator’s voice arrived broken into pieces. He gave the road number, the last milestone he remembered, the description of the bend and the ravine. He repeated himself twice. He was told help was coming from the valley, perhaps forty minutes, perhaps more because of the state of the road after winter.

“Is the victim breathing?” the operator asked.

“No,” Elias said.

“Are you certain?”

He looked down at the red car among the firs.

“Yes.”

“Stay nearby if it is safe to do so.”

He almost laughed. Safe. As if safety were a place one could stand in.

He promised he would wait.

The phone lost signal as soon as he lowered it. He put it in his coat pocket and stood a while in the middle of the road, feeling the wind come cold across the pass. The crows watched him. He thought of continuing on—of doing exactly what was needed and no more—but the thought shamed him before it had fully formed.

No one should wait alone among broken glass and trees.

He climbed down again.

The car had settled further, creaking softly. Elias tested the ground and decided not to move her. Years ago, in a first-aid course required at a factory where he had worked only three months, a woman with orange hair had told them never to move the injured unless there was fire or flood. She had said it while standing beside a rubber dummy, and they had all nodded, each secretly believing disaster would announce itself clearly when it came.

He could smell no smoke, only oil and pine resin. He took off his coat and draped it awkwardly over the woman’s shoulders through the window, though she could not feel the cold. Then he sat on a flat stone a few feet away.

“My name is Elias,” he said, because the silence seemed to demand some introduction. “I don’t suppose that matters now. It might have mattered this morning.”

The woman did not answer.

The ticking under the hood slowed.

He looked at her face as much as he could bear. There was nothing grotesque in it, which somehow made it worse. The face retained an expression of faint surprise, as if she had seen, at the last instant, something unexpected but not frightening. A fox in the road. A patch of ice. Her own life stepping aside.

“I used to drive too fast,” Elias said. “When I was younger. I had a yellow motorcycle. Not yellow like a flower. Yellow like machinery. My brother called it a wasp.”

A raven passed overhead, its wings making a thick, fabric sound.

“My brother was named Tomas. He was careful in all the ways I wasn’t. Folded his shirts. Balanced his accounts. He would take an hour to do a thing that took me ten minutes, and then he’d spend another hour fixing what I’d broken by hurrying. When we were boys, we built a boat from boards we stole from behind the mill. We meant to sail it down the river to the sea.”

Elias smiled faintly.

“The river was not much of a river. Brown water, slow, full of reeds. The boat sank before we got past the old footbridge. Tomas had brought sandwiches wrapped in paper, and they floated away like little parcels of hope. We went home soaked and hungry, and our father beat us for stealing the boards, but not very hard, because he was laughing. I remember that most. Not the beating. Him trying not to laugh.”

The wind stirred the woman’s hair. Elias leaned forward and, with great care, moved a strand away from her mouth. His fingers shook afterward, so he tucked them under his arms.

“It’s strange what remains,” he said. “You’d think the important things would stand firm. Births, funerals, vows. But sometimes they blur. And then some foolish little thing—the smell of river mud, a sandwich sailing away—stays bright for fifty years.”

He listened for sirens. There were none.

The mountains were enormous around them, yet the place where he sat felt enclosed, almost intimate. Fir trunks rose close together. Patches of sunlight shifted over the car’s dented roof. Ants had already found a smear of sweetness from something spilled inside: a bottle of soda, perhaps, or juice. Life wasted no time being life.

“What were you carrying?” Elias asked her.

He did not search the car. It felt indecent. But he could see fragments: a brown leather bag on the passenger floor, papers scattered like frightened birds, a blue sweater, a child’s drawing wedged halfway under the seat. A house in green crayon. A sun with long rays. Three people standing stiff as fence posts.

He looked away.

“My wife could draw,” he said after a while. “Not like artists in museums. Better, I thought. She drew quickly. A cat sleeping, a hand holding a cup, my face when I was pretending not to be angry. She would leave drawings in books instead of bookmarks. Years after she died, I kept finding them. Once, in a dictionary, there was a sketch of our kitchen table. Two plates, one pear, a knife. Nothing else. I sat on the floor and cried like I’d found her body all over again.”

He closed his eyes. Behind them came the kitchen as it had been: morning light on tiles, Marta humming off-key, coffee boiling too long because neither of them wanted to cross the room and take it from the flame. She had died in a hospital, not suddenly, though it had still seemed sudden when the final breath left her. Illness had announced itself with letters and scans and appointments, but death had entered quietly, like someone who knew the house.

“Her name was Marta,” he told the woman. “She said mountains were too dramatic. She liked fields. Places where the sky had room. I brought her here once, before we married. She stood on a ridge not far from this road and said, ‘Everything here is either falling down or pretending it won’t.’ I thought she meant the rocks. Later I understood she meant us too.”

A gust came down the slope, bending the young firs. Somewhere above, stones loosened and clattered. Elias looked up sharply. The wilderness resumed its stillness, as if nothing had moved.

He thought of the emergency crew making its way from the valley: men and women in bright jackets, an ambulance shouldering through the switchbacks, radios crackling. Their arrival would restore order. They would measure and photograph and lift. They would ask his name and write it down. The woman would become “female, approximately thirty,” then a name, then a voice on a telephone telling someone far away to sit down.

Until then, she was here.

“I don’t know your name,” Elias said. “That troubles me.”

He wondered if she had been loved loudly or quietly. If she had left dishes in a sink somewhere. If someone was angry with her and would remain angry for another hour, unaware that anger had lost its object. Perhaps she was expected at a school, a clinic, a house with geraniums on the sill. Perhaps no one expected her until evening. Perhaps the child who drew the green house was waiting with a backpack on.

He took the tin cup from his rucksack and drank a little water. Then, feeling rude, he set it down.

“I had a daughter,” he said.

The sentence surprised him. He had not said it aloud in months.

“Her name is Lena. Not had. Have. She’s alive. Lives by the coast now. Sends postcards sometimes, though not as often as I’d like, and more often than I deserve. She has a son I’ve seen only twice. He was small and suspicious of me. Sensible child.”

He rubbed his thumb over the torn skin on his palm.

“When Marta was sick, I became a very useful man. I learned medicines, schedules, soups she could swallow. I slept in chairs. I argued with doctors. Everyone praised me. After she died, usefulness left, and there was only me. Lena was seventeen. She needed a father, and instead she had a monument to grief sitting at the kitchen table. I thought sorrow excused everything. It does not. That is one of the things a person learns too late.”

The car gave a soft metallic pop. Elias flinched.

“I walked out one afternoon to buy bread and kept walking. Not forever. Just six hours. But when I came back, she had packed a bag and gone to a friend’s. We spoke after that, of course. We still speak. But some departures continue even after everyone returns.”

The crows had settled again, black beads among the branches. One hopped closer, head cocked. Elias picked up a stone and tossed it—not to hit, only to warn. The bird lifted resentfully into the air.

“No,” he said. “Not yet.”

He did not know to whom he spoke.

Time passed strangely. The sun moved behind a shoulder of cloud, and the ravine cooled. Elias found himself telling her things he had not told the living: that he was afraid of dying on a path with no one beside him; that he sometimes envied people of faith their architecture of certainty; that he carried Marta’s wedding ring tied inside the hem of his rucksack because he could not bear to wear it or leave it behind.

He told her about a dog he had owned as a boy, about the night his father came home drunk and weeping because the mill had closed, about the first time he saw the sea and felt cheated that it was merely water and sky after so many stories. He told her about Tomas, careful Tomas, dying under a load of timber in a yard where every rule had been followed except the one that mattered. He told her how, after the funeral, their mother washed the same clean cup three times and placed it upside down on the rack as if completing that small action could delay the next unbearable fact.

All the while the mountains listened with their old, stone patience.

At last, faint and far, came a siren.

Elias stopped speaking. The sound wound up from the valley, thin at first, then fuller, breaking against the bends in the road. He felt relief, and beneath it a strange reluctance, as though strangers were coming to interrupt a conversation.

“I suppose that’s for you,” he said.

He stood, knees stiff, and brushed dirt from his trousers. He looked at the woman one final time as she was now—not as she had been before impact, not as she would be in the hands of officials and relatives, but here in the brief interval where their lives had crossed: she in her stillness, he in his helpless witness.

“I hope someone knows your stories,” he said. “If they don’t, I hope they invent kind ones.”

The ambulance stopped above with a grind of tires on gravel. Doors slammed. Voices called down. Elias answered and climbed toward them, his hands raised slightly, though no one had accused him of anything.

Two paramedics descended with practiced urgency, followed by a police officer and a firefighter carrying equipment. The ravine filled with bright jackets, clipped questions, the language of procedure. Elias gave his account. Walking north. Found the vehicle. Called from the road. No pulse. Did not move her. The officer wrote everything in a small notebook, nodding without looking up.

“You did the right thing staying,” one of the paramedics said.

Elias glanced down at his coat still draped over the woman’s shoulders.

“It was nothing,” he said, and knew it was both true and not true.

They brought a stretcher. Someone asked him to wait by the ambulance in case there were more questions. He stood at the roadside, holding his walking stick, watching the clouds gather around the peaks. The wilderness had withdrawn a little from the commotion, but it had not gone. It waited beyond the red flash of lights, beyond the diesel smell, beyond human urgency. It would remain after the glass was swept away, after rain washed the oil from the stones, after new needles grew from the broken firs.

A police officer came to return his coat. It was folded neatly, though stained at one sleeve.

“Thank you,” she said.

Elias took it. “Do you know her name?”

The officer hesitated. “We found identification. Her name was Anna.”

Anna.

The name entered him quietly. It did not explain her. It did not restore her. But it gave shape to the space she had occupied.

“Anna,” he repeated.

When they finally allowed him to go, the afternoon had begun to lean toward evening. The ambulance drove back toward the valley without siren. The police remained behind with cones and measuring tape. Elias stood for a moment at the bend, uncertain whether to turn back or continue upward.

Below, through the firs, he could see the scar where the car had come down. Already it seemed less violent than before, becoming part of the slope’s long history of falls: rockfall, snowfall, treefall, life falling into death.

He thought of Lena by the coast. He imagined her kitchen, though he had never seen it. He imagined the boy, older now, perhaps still suspicious. He took out his phone. There was one bar.

For a long moment he only held it.

Then he wrote: I am in the mountains. I saw something today. I would like to visit, if you’ll have me. No need to answer quickly. Love, Dad.

He sent it before he could revise the tenderness out of it.

The message hung, unsent, then vanished into the invisible.

Elias put the phone away and began walking. Not back. Not yet. The road ahead climbed toward the pass, where evening light lay pale on the snowfields. His boots struck gravel. His stick clicked. Behind him, men and women spoke in low voices around the bend. Ahead, the mountains waited, immense and temporary, shedding stone grain by grain into the valleys below.

After some time, the phone buzzed once in his pocket.

He stopped.

The wind moved around him, smelling of ice and resin and distant water. He did not look at the message immediately. For a few breaths he simply stood there, alive under the vast indifferent sky, holding within him the name of a stranger and all the fragile, vanishing stories of the world.
