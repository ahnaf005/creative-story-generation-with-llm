Barbara had chosen the aisle seat because of the baby.

At eight months, there were calculations in everything: how far to the restroom, how many steps to the doors, whether a chair had arms, whether a stranger’s knees would have to be disturbed if she needed to rise. Even prayer required geometry now. She lowered herself carefully onto the pew in the synagogue in Southfield, smoothing her coat over the high, hard globe of her belly. February light lay flat against the windows. Detroit winter light, gray and withholding.

The sanctuary was full in the way it always was when Rabbi Adler spoke—full not only with bodies, but with the expectation that something firm and decent would be said, something one could carry home like a folded handkerchief. Barbara had heard people say he was renowned, beloved, a man of learning. To her he was also a voice: warm, measured, never rushing the old words, never treating them as old.

The baby moved. A heel, perhaps. Impatient already. Wait, she wanted to tell it. We are in shul. Behave.

Then the young man stood.

At first, no one seemed to understand his standing. People stood for many reasons. To leave, to approach, to ask, to be noticed. He was only a young man, thin-faced, with an agitation about him that might have been sorrow or fever. Barbara turned slightly, one hand pressed beneath her ribs. Someone nearby inhaled sharply.

The sound that came next did not belong in that room.

It cracked the air open. For one absurd second Barbara was angry at it, as if a chair had fallen during the most sacred part of the service. Then Rabbi Adler’s body changed—changed from speaker to struck, from man to weight—and the sanctuary seemed to tilt.

No, no, not here. Not him. Not with the ark behind him and the prayer books open and the children somewhere under their mothers’ arms.

The young man’s hand still held the gun.

Barbara rose before she knew she had decided to rise. Her belly pulled at her back; the pew blocked her knees. He was someone’s son. That fact came to her with terrible clarity. Someone had once buttoned his coat, wiped his mouth, worried over his fever. He stood there shaking, and the room was breaking apart around him, and if someone could reach him—if someone could say his name, any name, darling, stop, please—

She took one step into the aisle.

The crowd moved like water released from a shattered pipe. Bodies surged toward the exits, toward anywhere else, away from the impossible center of the room. A shoulder struck her. Hands caught at her sleeves, not to help but because everyone was falling and pushing and blind with fear. She tried to keep her feet. The baby lurched inside her, or perhaps that was only her own body, seized by the terror of being crushed.

“Wait,” she meant to say, but no word came.

She saw the young man lift the gun again.

Then she saw nothing but coats, hats, the pale backs of hands, the dark blur of winter wool. Someone’s elbow grazed her cheek. Someone cried out for a mother. Someone was praying in a broken whisper. Barbara was carried backward, sideways, away from the aisle, away from the boy with the gun, away from the rabbi who had been speaking and now was not.

Outside, the cold struck her face.

She found herself on the synagogue steps among strangers who looked suddenly intimate, their mouths open, their eyes emptied. Snow lay in dirty ridges along the curb. A siren began somewhere far off, or perhaps it had been sounding all along.

Her hands went to her stomach. The baby answered with a slow, deliberate turn.

Alive, then. Still here.

Barbara looked back at the doors through which the crowd continued to spill, and shame rose in her as hot as blood. She had tried to go to him. Had she? One step. Only one. What was one step against a bullet, against panic, against fate? But the question would remain with her, fastening itself to the child before the child was even born: how close one could come to mercy, and how easily the world could sweep a person away.
