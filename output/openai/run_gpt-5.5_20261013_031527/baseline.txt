**The Noise of Many Feet**

On the morning of February 12, 1966, Barbara woke before dawn with a hand pressed to the side of her belly, waiting for the slow, answering roll beneath her palm.

“There you are,” she whispered.

The baby had been quiet through the night, as if listening. Outside, winter pressed its face to the bedroom window. Southfield lay pale and still beneath a crust of snow, and from somewhere down the block came the sound of a car coughing itself awake.

Her husband, Daniel, stood at the mirror fixing his tie. “You don’t have to come,” he said gently, as he had said twice already.

Barbara looked at him over the rise of her stomach. “Rabbi Adler is speaking. I’m coming.”

Daniel smiled, but worry remained in his eyes. She was due any week now, and moving had become less like walking and more like negotiating with gravity. Still, she dressed carefully, pinning her hair back, buttoning her blue coat as far as it would go.

At the synagogue, the air was warm and familiar. Coats rustled. Men and women greeted one another in hushed, cheerful voices. Children slipped between adults like fish through reeds. Barbara felt the baby stir again as they took their seats.

Rabbi Adler stood at the front, dignified and beloved, his voice carrying with the steady grace that always made Barbara feel, even in anxious times, that the world could still be mended by good people saying true things aloud.

She did not remember afterward what he had been saying at the exact moment the young man rose.

She remembered only a break in the room.

A shape where no shape should have been. A movement too sharp. Then the sound—impossible, violent, cracking through the sanctuary.

For one second, no one moved. The congregation seemed held inside a glass bell.

Then the screaming began.

Barbara saw Rabbi Adler fall. She saw people duck beneath pews, saw prayer books scatter, saw Daniel turn toward her with his mouth open, though she could not hear what he said. The baby kicked hard, once, as if startled awake.

The young man stood with the gun in his hand.

He looked younger than Barbara expected terror to look.

His face was pale, almost empty, and in that terrible suspended instant, before the room broke fully into panic, she felt—not forgiveness, not understanding, but a wild, unreasonable certainty that someone had to reach him before the world closed over them all.

“Wait,” she said, though no one could have heard her.

She pushed herself to her feet.

Daniel grabbed her sleeve. “Barbara!”

But she was already moving into the aisle, one hand beneath her belly, the other raised in front of her, palm open.

The young man turned the gun toward himself.

“No,” Barbara said.

She took one step.

Then the crowd surged.

People fleeing pressed against her from every side. A shoulder struck her back. Someone’s elbow caught her arm. Daniel shouted her name again, but he vanished behind a rush of coats and hands and terrified faces. Barbara tried to keep her balance, tried to plant her feet, tried to say that they must stop, must not push, must let her through.

But fear had become a river.

It carried her backward, sideways, away.

Another shot sounded.

The river became a flood.

Barbara was swept through the doors and into the cold brightness outside, where women wept into gloved hands and men stumbled hatless into the snow. Someone was reciting a prayer. Someone else kept saying, “Call an ambulance, call an ambulance,” though sirens were already rising in the distance.

Daniel found her near the steps, bent over, breathing hard.

“Are you hurt?” he asked, his hands moving helplessly over her shoulders, her arms, her face.

She shook her head. She could not speak. Inside her, the baby rolled once, slowly, alive.

Only then did Barbara begin to cry.

In the days that followed, the city spoke in stunned voices. Rabbi Adler’s name passed from mouth to mouth with reverence and disbelief. People brought casseroles, folded newspapers, murmured theories, shook their heads. The young man became a question no one knew how to hold.

Barbara sat by the window and watched the snow soften along the curbs.

At night, she dreamed of the aisle stretching longer and longer before her, of her body heavy and slow, of a hand she could not reach in time.

“You couldn’t have stopped it,” Daniel told her.

She knew he meant to comfort her. She even knew he was right.

But memory does not always obey truth.

Three weeks later, in the first gray hour of morning, Barbara gave birth to a daughter. The baby arrived red-faced and furious, with fists clenched tightly against the world. When the nurse laid her against Barbara’s chest, the child quieted at once.

Barbara touched the damp dark hair and thought of the sanctuary, the terrible noise, the crowd, the rabbi’s voice before it was taken from them.

“What shall we name her?” Daniel asked softly.

Barbara looked down at the baby, who had opened her eyes just enough to seem suspicious of the light.

“Hope,” Barbara said.

Daniel blinked. “In Hebrew?”

Barbara nodded. “Tikvah.”

Years later, Barbara would tell her daughter very little about that day. Not because it had faded, but because it had not. Some memories lived behind doors one opened only with care.

But every February, she lit a candle.

And when the flame steadied, she would rest her hand over her heart and remember the moment before panic, before the rushing feet, when she had stepped into the aisle because some small, stubborn part of her still believed that a person might be called back from the edge by the sight of an open hand.

The world had not been mended that morning.

But her daughter slept in the next room, breathing.

And Barbara, listening, believed that mending was still required of them.
