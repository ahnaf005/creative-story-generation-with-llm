### The Deep End

At college, Q was the sort of person you could lend your life to and expect back neatly folded.

He returned books before they were due. He remembered the names of people you had mentioned once, in passing, three weeks earlier. When our radiator broke in February, he borrowed a wrench from the building manager, fixed it, and left a note explaining what he’d done, in case we needed to do it again. He was handsome without appearing to know it, clever without making anyone feel slow, and good at parties in the way some people are good at piano: as though they’d been born with the right hands.

His real name was Quentin, but one of our friends started calling him Q after a professor mistook his handwriting for a question mark. The name stuck. By junior year, even professors used it. It suited him: a clean, mysterious letter, all promise and no visible effort.

I was Q’s friend, although I would have had trouble explaining why he chose me. I was late to everything, broke several umbrellas in one semester, and once forgot to submit a paper I had spent a month writing. Q sat with me in the library that night while I reconstructed it from notes, and didn’t mention that he had finished his own work days earlier. When I apologized, he said, “You’re doing it now,” as if time were a thing we could negotiate.

For a while, I thought our friendship was permanent. Then college ended, as it does, not with a door slamming but with a dozen small departures. Q went to Boston for a job at a consulting firm. I moved west for a newspaper internship that turned into a job, then a different job, then a life. We exchanged holiday emails for a few years. His were always thoughtful. Mine were always late. Eventually, we stopped sending them.

I didn’t think of Q often. Or rather, I thought of him in the way you think of a well-kept house you once visited: not with longing, exactly, but with the impression that the people inside must still be doing everything correctly.

Ten years after graduation, I saw him at a hotel pool in Minneapolis.

I was there for a conference, though I had skipped the last afternoon session. The pool was nearly empty, a blue rectangle under a vaulted glass ceiling. I’d taken a chair in the corner with a towel over my shoulders and a book open on my lap. I wasn’t reading. The room was warm and echoing, and the book had become an alibi.

Q came in wearing a navy suit and no tie. He looked older, naturally, but not in any way that seemed to have gone wrong. His hair had thinned slightly at the temples. He had a pale, neat face and the same unhurried posture, as if he had been given advance notice of every room he entered.

He glanced around, and I lowered my eyes to the book.

I wasn’t ready to be recognized. I wasn’t sure he would recognize me, either. Ten years is long enough to turn familiarity into a guessing game. So I watched him from behind my book while he crossed to a table near the pool and set down a folder.

A woman was already sitting there. She was about my age, maybe a little younger, with damp hair and a hotel robe tied tightly at the waist. She had a paper cup in front of her and both hands around it. When Q sat down, she looked at him with the rigid attention of someone waiting for a verdict.

“I appreciate you making time,” she said.

“Of course,” Q said. His voice was unchanged. “I’m sorry it had to be in the middle of your trip.”

“It’s fine.” She gave him a brief smile that did not reach her eyes. “You said it couldn’t wait.”

Q opened the folder, then closed it again. He glanced toward the pool, though there was no one in it. I felt the old reflex to help him, absurdly, as if I could somehow rescue him from a conversation I had no right to hear.

“I’ve been thinking about how to say this,” he began.

The woman nodded once. “That usually means it’s bad.”

“I wouldn’t say bad.”

“Q.”

He paused. I knew that pause. It was the same one he used in college when a professor asked a question he didn’t want to answer too quickly. He was buying time, trying to find the right sentence, the one that would be honest and painless.

“There’s been a change in the structure of the team,” he said. “The company’s decided to consolidate the regional offices. The role we discussed—”

“Is gone?”

“Not gone, exactly. Reconfigured.”

She looked down at her cup. “You told me to take the job.”

“I told you it was a good opportunity.”

“You told me they needed someone who could build the department. You said, ‘If you come here, I can make sure you have the resources.’”

“I believed that at the time.”

“So did I.”

Q leaned forward, hands clasped. “I’m not saying you shouldn’t have come.”

“I sold my house.”

“I know.”

“My daughter changed schools. My mother is in assisted living two states away. I moved here because you said—”

“I know,” he said again, and this time it came out too quickly, too much like a plea. “I know what you gave up.”

She sat back. “Do you?”

He looked at her. For the first time, his composure seemed less like confidence than effort. There were faint lines beside his mouth I hadn’t noticed from across the room. His fingers worried the edge of the folder.

“I’m trying to find another position for you,” he said. “There are a few possibilities. It may take a couple of weeks.”

“Am I being fired?”

“No. Not today.”

“Is that a yes?”

He rubbed his forehead. “Your position is being eliminated at the end of the quarter.”

She nodded slowly. “There it is.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Are you sorry because it’s happening, or because you have to tell me?”

He opened his mouth, then stopped. I could almost see him searching for the answer that would be both true and kind. He had always been good at finding those. I realized then that he might have spent his adult life believing they were the same thing.

“Both,” he said at last.

She looked away, toward the water. In the bright surface of the pool, the ceiling broke into wavering lines. “You keep offering me possibilities,” she said. “Do you actually have a job for me, or are you trying to make this sound less like what it is?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then say that.”

“I don’t know yet,” he repeated.

“And don’t tell me you’ll make sure I’m taken care of. You can’t.”

“No,” Q said. “I can’t.”

The woman’s shoulders lowered a fraction. Not forgiveness, not relief. Just the small easing that comes when a person stops having to argue with a sentence.

Q shut the folder. “I should have been clearer earlier. I thought if I waited until I had a solution, I wouldn’t have to bring you half an answer.”

“You brought me a half answer anyway.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t need you to feel terrible,” she said. “I need to know what’s real.”

“I understand.”

“I don’t think you do yet.”

He accepted that. There was no quick apology, no polished reply. He sat with the words between them.

I closed my book.

The woman stood first. “Send me what you know by Friday. No possibilities. What you know.”

“I will.”

“And Q?”

“Yes?”

“Don’t call me before then to see how I’m doing.”

He nodded. “All right.”

She left through the glass doors toward the elevators. Q remained at the table, looking down at the folder. The pool’s filtered blue light moved over his suit sleeve. For a minute he didn’t touch anything. Then he took out his phone, stared at the screen, and put it back in his pocket.

I could have gone over. I could have said his name and watched his face change. Perhaps he would have been glad. Perhaps he would have made room for me in the old, easy way, and we would have traded updates about the years we’d missed. I imagined telling him I’d seen the conversation, though that was exactly the sort of thing a person shouldn’t say.

Instead, I stood, folded my towel over the chair, and walked toward the other exit.

At the door I glanced back. Q was still alone at the table. He had taken off his jacket and draped it over the chair. Without it, he looked less like the man who could fix a radiator and more like someone who had arrived with a problem he couldn’t repair.

I didn’t call out. I left him his privacy, which was not the same thing as leaving him alone. I carried the sight of him with me through the lobby and into the cold afternoon: Q, trying to tell the truth in a way that would not hurt, discovering that no such way existed.

For years, I had mistaken his calm for certainty. Now I wondered how often it had been only the pause before he spoke.
