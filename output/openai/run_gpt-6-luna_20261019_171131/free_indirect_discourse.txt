### The Night Nursery

At 2:13 in the morning, Mara woke certain that the hospital had moved.

Not the bed. The bed was still there, narrow and plastic-sided, with its stiff white sheet and the rubbery pillow that made her neck ache. But the room had shifted around it. The door was on the wrong wall. The window, which had looked out on the parking garage all evening, now showed a black garden, glossy with rain.

And the baby was gone.

She sat up too quickly. Pain opened beneath her ribs, bright as a match. There was an empty hollow against her arm where the nurse had laid him, wrapped in a striped blanket.

He must be in the nursery, she told herself. Of course. That was what nurseries were for: keeping babies safe while mothers slept. Except she had not agreed to it. Had she? She could have. She’d been asked so many things. Did she want ice? Did she want to try feeding him? Did she want the blood pressure cuff on her left arm or her right? At some point, surely, someone had asked whether they could take her baby away.

The door opened. A nurse came in carrying a clipboard and a plastic cup of water.

“Where is he?” Mara asked.

The nurse glanced at the empty cot. “They’ve taken them down to the nursery.”

“They?”

“All the babies.” A smile, practiced but not unkind. “It’s a busy night.”

There was nothing for it but to drink the water. It tasted of the paper cup. The nurse checked the chart at the foot of the bed, and Mara watched the numbers on the clock advance without appearing to move.

“Can I see him?”

“In a little while. Let’s get you settled first.”

Settled. As if Mara were a drawer someone had left open.

When the nurse went, Mara got out of bed. Her feet touched the cold floor, and the room tipped slightly. She waited for it to right itself. Then she followed the pale stripe of light under the door into the corridor.

The maternity ward was quiet in the particular way a place is quiet when it is full of people trying not to make noise. Somewhere, a pump made a soft, rhythmic clicking. Somewhere else, a woman laughed once and stopped, startled by the sound of herself.

At the end of the hall, a woman in a yellow hospital gown stood beside a row of bassinets. Her hair was wet, though it could not have rained in here.

“Have they told you which one is yours?” the woman asked.

Mara looked at the babies. They were bundled up and sleeping, all of them with their faces turned slightly to one side. “No.”

“That’s all right,” the woman said. “I don’t think they know.”

One of the bassinets had a card clipped to it. The name on the card was upside down. Mara bent to read it, but the letters slid into each other, like wet ink.

“Are you here with your first?” she asked.

The woman considered. “I was, earlier.”

A laugh came from the nurses’ station. Or maybe a baby crying. Mara couldn’t tell. The woman in yellow was already walking away, slow and careful, as if she had been instructed not to disturb the floor.

Mara returned to her room, though she had no memory of deciding to go back. A nurse was standing beside the bed with a bundle in her arms.

“Here he is,” she said. “You can have a cuddle.”

The baby was warm, and very small. His face was wrinkled, his mouth puckered with the serious effort of being new. Mara touched one finger to his cheek.

There you are.

Then she saw his hands.

Her son’s left thumb, the midwife had said, had a tiny pale crescent at the base of the nail. It was a birthmark, perhaps, or simply something he’d brought with him. Mara had stared at it while he lay on her chest, exhausted and astonished, before they took him away to weigh him.

This baby had a faint red mark on his wrist instead.

She looked at the bracelet. The name printed on it was not her son’s.

“Wait,” she said.

The nurse leaned over. “What is it?”

“This isn’t—”

But she could not finish. The baby had turned his face toward her, and his eyes were open: dark, unfocused, shining. He made a small sound, not quite a cry. Mara felt something in her chest answer it, some old and wordless place.

The nurse checked the bracelet, then the chart. “Oh. I’m sorry. There’s been a mix-up.”

“Where is mine?”

“We’ll find him. It’s all right. These things happen.”

These things happen. Babies were carried from room to room. Names got turned upside down. People slept through the most important moments of their lives. The whole hospital was full of women with empty arms and strangers’ children, and no one seemed especially alarmed.

The baby in her arms sighed. His cheek rested against her wrist.

“Can I keep holding him?” Mara asked.

“For a minute, yes.”

A minute stretched. The nurse left to fetch someone. The baby slept. Mara sat in the chair beside the window, where there was no garden now, only the parking garage after all. A strip of rainwater trembled down the glass.

In the corridor, wheels squeaked. A door opened and closed. From somewhere nearby, a woman said, “I know she’s mine because she smells like toast.”

Another woman answered, “Mine doesn’t smell like anything.”

“You’ll know.”

Mara looked down at the baby. He smelled faintly of milk and warm cotton. Not toast. Not nothing. He smelled like the beginning of a thing she could not name.

The nurse came back with another woman, both of them speaking in low voices. Their words gathered at the edge of the room and refused to settle. One asked for the bracelet number. The other said the nursery was being checked. They might have said Mara’s name. They might have said someone else’s.

At last, the nurse took the baby from her arms.

“I’ll bring yours as soon as we sort it out.”

Mara nodded. It was the right thing to do. She could be patient. She could be sensible. There were systems for this, labels and forms, people whose whole job was knowing who belonged to whom.

She lay down and tried to sleep.

The next time she woke, the room was pale with morning. The baby’s cot was full. A small, tightly wrapped shape lay there, one fist tucked beside his chin.

Mara sat up slowly. Her body felt as though it had been borrowed and returned in a slightly altered state. Beside the cot sat a brown paper bag with her clothes folded inside. On the table lay a discharge form. Someone had written her name in blue ink.

She looked at the baby.

It was the same one she’d held in the night. The red mark on his wrist showed above the blanket.

A nurse came in carrying a stack of papers. “You’re going home?”

“Is he mine?” Mara asked.

The nurse stopped. Her eyes moved from Mara to the baby and back again. She looked tired enough to tell the truth.

“Your paperwork says so.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

For a moment, neither of them spoke. Beyond the door, a cart rattled down the corridor. A newborn began to cry, then another joined it, and then the sounds softened, as if the building were breathing out.

“We’ve checked the bracelets,” the nurse said. “Everything matches.”

Mara looked again at the child. The pale crescent on her own baby’s thumb came back to her—not as an image, exactly, but as a certainty. She knew what she had seen. She knew this child was not the one who had lain against her chest at dawn, before the lights and the weighing and the long bright hallway.

This was another baby.

He opened his eyes. They were not hers, of course. Babies’ eyes belonged to no one yet. Still, something passed between them, an unhurried recognition. He had no idea she was standing at the edge of a mistake. He was simply here, warm and awake and waiting to be held.

The nurse handed her the discharge papers. “We can look into it further if you’d like.”

Mara’s mind went to the woman in yellow, the wet hair, the row of bassinets with their upside-down names. It went to the voices in the corridor, all those women trying to be certain by smell, by touch, by a mark no one else could see. Somewhere, perhaps, another mother was holding Mara’s son. Perhaps she had looked at his thumb and understood. Perhaps she was already carrying him through a morning that had opened like any other.

Mara signed her name.

It was an absurd thing to do. It was a terrible thing, if one stood far enough away from it. But the baby shifted in the cot and made the softest complaint, and Mara’s heart moved toward him with a calm that felt older than reason.

She dressed slowly, pausing when the room swayed. Then she lifted him. He fit into the bend of her elbow, small as a loaf of bread. Outside, the rain had stopped.

In the elevator, a man carrying a bunch of flowers stepped aside to let her enter. He glanced at the baby, smiled, and looked away. Mara watched the floor numbers descend. There was a faint, clean scent of soap on the baby’s blanket, and beneath it, milk.

You can go home now, she thought. A home was a place one brought a child into. A home was a door waiting to open.

At the lobby, the automatic doors parted. The morning air touched her face. She had expected fear. She had expected to feel the weight of the wrongness grow heavier with every step.

Instead, as she crossed the pavement, the baby curled his hand around her finger.

Mara laughed. The sound surprised her, but it did not frighten her. She held him closer and kept walking, carrying a small stranger into the light as if the light had been waiting for them both.
