### The Deep End

At college, they called him Q because his name was Quentin and because, according to him, “Quentin sounds like a man who owns a waxed moustache.” He said it once in the first week of term, and everyone adopted the nickname. Q suited him. It was neat, memorable, and just a little mysterious.

Mara met him in the library when he reached over her shoulder and pulled down the book she’d been pretending to read for twenty minutes.

“You’re holding it upside down,” he said.

She looked. She was. “I’m testing whether anyone’s paying attention.”

“Excellent. You passed.”

That was how it went with Q. He could tease without making you feel foolish, and he could notice what you needed before you asked. When Mara had the flu, he left soup outside her room. When she got a low mark on an essay, he read it and found the one paragraph she’d written well. He remembered birthdays, kept spare pens, and never seemed to be late.

He was good at everything, but not in the showy way. He played piano at parties only if someone else asked. He ran the student paper’s budget without losing a receipt. He could make a roomful of strangers feel they had known one another for years. When a professor praised him, Q thanked the professor and changed the subject.

Mara, who was usually one spilled drink away from a crisis, found this comforting. There was Q. Q would know what to do.

They spent a lot of time together during those three years. They ate chips on the steps of the old humanities building and argued about whether a person could be kind and selfish at the same time. They walked home after parties, Q making up elaborate biographies for the people they passed. Mara told him about her parents’ divorce, the real version and not the cheerful one she gave everyone else. He told her that he’d once been so nervous before a school concert he vomited into a piano.

“You played after that?” she asked.

“Had to. The audience was already there.”

“You were nine.”

“An unforgiving profession.”

She loved him for stories like that: the little windows he opened onto himself, always tidy, always with a punchline at the end.

In their final year, Mara was accepted into a graduate program in another city. Q got a job at a consulting firm. They promised to visit, and did, once. Then work got busy. Then the holidays became complicated. Then a year had passed, and another. No argument, no betrayal, nothing to point to and say: there. The friendship simply grew quiet, like music from a room at the end of a hall.

A decade after graduation, Mara saw him at the public pool.

She was there because her doctor had recommended swimming for her shoulder, which had started to complain about years of desk work. She was there because the pool was cheap and close and no one expected conversation. Mostly, she was there because swimming demanded that she count: one length, two, three. Not think about the unfinished grant application at work, or the leaking gutter, or her mother’s doctor calling twice.

She came up for air at the deep end and saw a man standing by the lane ropes.

The first thing she recognized was the way he held his towel: folded twice, tucked under one arm. The second was his profile, unmistakable even after ten years. Q had a little more gray at his temples now. His shoulders looked less like the shoulders of a man who could carry an entire department’s worth of boxes by himself.

He turned to speak to the lifeguard, and there it was: the easy, attentive smile. Not a copy of the old one. The old one.

Mara gripped the pool edge.

She could have called his name. He might have been happy to see her. He might have been horrified to see her in a bathing cap, her hair flattened to her skull, and there was no telling which of those possibilities was worse.

So she lowered herself back into the water.

A few minutes later, Q left the pool and sat at one of the little tables near the vending machines. He was still damp, in a dark blue T-shirt, with his towel draped around his neck. A woman came over carrying two paper cups. She was perhaps his age, perhaps a little younger, with short hair and a canvas bag beside her chair.

Mara climbed out in the lane beside them. She had no reason to linger, except she had forgotten her sandals and didn’t want to walk across the wet deck barefoot. The pool was loud with children and splashing. The woman’s voice carried in little snatches.

“—the board’s decision, not mine,” Q was saying.

The woman looked at him over her cup. “But you told me you were sure.”

“I said I thought we had a good chance.”

“You said you’d put your name behind it.”

Q rubbed his thumb along the edge of the table. Mara had never seen him do that. Or perhaps she had, and had never thought to notice.

“I did,” he said. “I sent the recommendation.”

“But not the budget revision.”

He blinked. A pause opened between them, and in it Mara understood that this was the part Q had been trying to avoid.

“No,” he said.

The woman looked down at her cup. “Why not?”

“They’d already made it clear they didn’t want to increase the budget. I thought if I pushed, they’d pull the whole project.”

“So you let them decide without my numbers.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Q, I’m not asking you to be sorry in a general way. I’m asking what happened.”

He had a clean answer ready. Mara could almost see him reaching for it: the committee’s shifting priorities, the impossible timeline, the need to keep the project afloat. He could make all of those things true. He had always been good at making the truth comfortable.

Instead, he pressed his lips together.

“I got scared,” he said. “I thought if I made myself difficult, they’d stop listening to me altogether. And I told myself I was protecting the work.”

The woman’s expression softened only slightly. “You were protecting your position.”

“Yes.”

His voice was too quiet. For a moment Mara could hear only the pool: the shriek of a child jumping from the low board, the slap of water against tile.

“I should have told you before the meeting,” he said. “And I should have sent the numbers, even if they said no. I didn’t do either. You gave me your work and trusted me with it, and I treated that like it would keep.”

The woman let out a breath. “I have to tell my team this afternoon. They’ve been building toward this for months.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t.”

He looked at her. This time there was no quick reply, no perfect adjustment of tone. He sat very still, with the towel slipping off one shoulder.

“You’re right,” he said.

The woman leaned back and closed her eyes. She didn’t cry. She didn’t shout. It seemed she had been carrying this news already, and Q had only made her sit down to hear it again.

Mara could have left. She had her sandals now. But she stayed where she was, holding them by the straps and looking at the water as if she were waiting for someone.

At college, Q had once talked her through a terrible night when she was certain she’d failed a course and ruined her future. He’d listened, asked questions, and then sat on the floor of her room while she cried. He had not tried to make her stop. That, too, was a kind of skill. Mara had been grateful, and afterward she’d mistaken the skill for certainty. Hadn’t everyone?

She had thought Q was the kind of person who could always see the right thing to do, and therefore did it. How convenient that had been. How easy, to let him be the one who knew.

The woman pushed her cup away. “I don’t know what I want you to say now.”

“Neither do I,” he said.

There it was: the answer she had never heard from him. Not a solution, not a joke, not a polished sentence that made everyone feel they could go on. Just a man sitting at a pool with damp hair and a bad cup of coffee, unable to repair what he had broken.

It wasn’t much. It was also not nothing.

Mara put on her sandals. Q still hadn’t looked her way. He was watching the woman, waiting. Not the confident, gracious attention he used to give at parties, but something heavier and more uncertain. He had learned, perhaps, that waiting was sometimes all there was to do.

The woman gathered her bag. “I have to go.”

“I know.”

She stood. He stood too, then seemed to realize he had no right to follow her. He stayed beside the table.

Mara walked past them toward the changing rooms. She did not say his name. She did not wave. In another life, she might have gone over, put a hand on his shoulder, and told him she knew he’d tried. But she didn’t know that. Not really. He had been afraid and had made a choice, and someone else would have to live with it. She could not tidy the scene up for him.

At the door, she glanced back once. Q was still standing there, his hands empty now, watching the place where the woman had been.

Mara thought of their last year at college, of the two of them on the humanities steps, laughing over a stranger’s invented life. She remembered the way Q had looked at her when she told him the truth about her parents: calm, open, with no joke waiting at the end.

Maybe that had been the real thing all along. Not that he never failed. That he could sit beside a failure without pretending it was something else.

She went into the changing room. The water ran down her arms and gathered on the tile beneath her feet. Outside, the pool filled itself with noise again. At least that was something it knew how to do.
