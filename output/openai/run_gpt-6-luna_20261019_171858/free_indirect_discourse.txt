### The Dry Side

The first sign was a brown crescent on the ceiling above Mara Venn’s desk.

It appeared on a Tuesday in October, while she was reading a student’s essay about the moral authority of maps. By noon, the crescent had widened into a continent. A drop gathered at its southern edge and fell onto the essay.

Mara looked up. The ceiling tiles were the color of old teeth, the sort of tiles that gave a person no reason to expect anything good from them. She moved the essays, found a wastebasket, and went downstairs to report the leak.

At the facilities desk, a man with a headset told her they had already logged it. The building had a history, he said. It was on the list.

Bellwether Hall had been built in the seventies for the faculties of Arts and Letters, which meant that it contained many offices, several classrooms, and no corridor wide enough for two people carrying books to pass without negotiation. Its outer walls were brick, its windows stubborn, its interior a bewilderment of low ceilings and sudden staircases. In winter, the east wing was cold. In spring, the west wing smelled faintly of wet wool. Everyone had a theory about the building. Nobody had a solution.

A week later, a notice appeared beside the elevator. *Water infiltration assessment and repairs. Please expect intermittent noise and restricted access.* It gave no dates. Dates, Mara had learned, were a form of optimism.

The contractors arrived in November. First came a row of orange barriers around the small courtyard, where the building’s lower level met a paved terrace. Then came the drilling: a sound like someone repeatedly trying to break into the faculty lounge with a very large key.

Mara’s office faced the courtyard. The crew cut into the paving stones, then the concrete beneath them, and stacked the rubble in bins. Dust found its way through the window seams and settled on her books. She wiped it away before teaching, and by afternoon the desk had a fresh pale coating. The workmen wore masks. The faculty did not.

“The membrane’s missing,” said Luis from Facilities, standing in Mara’s doorway with his hands in his pockets.

“Missing where?”

“Under the whole terrace, apparently.”

He said it with the strained satisfaction of a man whose suspicion had finally been confirmed, though confirmation had not improved his day. The waterproofing layer, the sheet that should have kept rain from seeping into the structure below, had never been installed. Or had been installed in one section and abandoned in another. The drawings were unclear. The drawings were always unclear when the building needed them most.

“So the water’s just coming through the concrete?”

“More or less.”

More or less. Mara glanced at the ceiling stain, now dry but still spreading. She imagined the rain travelling through the building’s bones, choosing its own rooms.

The first repair was supposed to take three weeks. It lasted until the end of term. The crew removed the terrace paving, chipped away concrete, and applied a sealant that smelled sharp enough to make Mara’s eyes water. On the days they sprayed it, she taught with the classroom windows open, though the cold made the students keep their coats on. One student wore a scarf over her nose. Another left to be sick and came back apologizing.

“Could we move the class?” Mara asked the department administrator.

“To where?”

The question was not rude. That was the trouble. There were no empty rooms, not in November, not in a university that had somehow managed to enroll more students without building more space for them. The class stayed where it was.

By December, the terrace had been covered with a black sheet and a temporary fence. The courtyard, once a place where faculty ate lunch and students smoked between lectures, became a construction site. Mara began carrying her tea upstairs in a travel mug. She began closing her office door, although the noise came through the walls.

At the faculty meeting, Professor Bellamy proposed that everyone who could should work from home until the project was finished.

“Some of us teach in person,” said a colleague from Classics.

“Some of us have students who can’t work from home,” said Mara.

“Some of us have asthma,” said Dr. Rahman, who sat near the door and had started wearing a mask in the corridors.

Everyone looked at the table. The table had a long white scratch on its surface, older than most of the people in the room. A person could have made it by dragging a chair. A person could have made it by trying to leave.

The dean promised that the university was taking the concerns seriously. The phrase drifted over them like the odor of the sealant: present, faintly irritating, impossible to pin down.

During the break, the contractors disappeared. The barriers remained. Rain pooled on the black sheet and froze overnight. In January, someone put a notice on the main entrance saying the courtyard was closed for safety reasons. The side door was closed too, though no one had posted a notice there. People found this out by arriving with their hands full and pulling on a locked handle.

Then the leak returned.

This time it appeared in the seminar room on the lower floor, where a wet patch spread above the projector screen. The room smelled damp, though the maintenance staff brought in a fan and said there was no mold. Dr. Rahman thought otherwise. She had a headache by the end of every lecture and a cough that she didn’t have at home. The department chair said the university had arranged an inspection.

“An inspection by whom?” Mara asked.

The chair had not been told.

The next morning, the seminar room was closed. A printed sign asked faculty to relocate classes. Mara shifted hers to the library annex, where the seats were too small and the screen did not work. She taught a lesson about the language of uncertainty beneath a poster advertising a lecture on certainty. It was the sort of joke a person might make if she had slept well.

In February, the contractors returned and removed the temporary work. The new surface was neat, pale, and sloped in a way that suggested someone had measured it. For three weeks, it did not leak. Faculty stepped carefully over the fresh concrete and admired it with the wary politeness usually reserved for new colleagues.

Then, after a hard rain, water came through the ceiling above Mara’s desk again.

This time the leak was worse. It ran down the wall behind her books and soaked the bottom shelf. She and a graduate student carried the books out in armfuls, stacking them in the corridor. The student, who was writing a dissertation on seventeenth-century travel diaries, held each volume by its spine as though it might be frightened.

The repair crew opened the wall. Behind it, the insulation was damp. A technician in a mask examined the cavity and said something Mara couldn’t hear over the fans.

“Is it mold?” she asked.

“It could be,” he said.

That answer was not reassuring, but it was probably honest. The wall was sealed off, and plastic sheeting appeared across the corridor. For two weeks, faculty entered their offices through a maze of taped doors and temporary partitions. The air had that sweet, chemical edge again. Mara kept her windows shut against the cold and left them open against the smell. It was a choice between two kinds of discomfort, which was becoming the university’s preferred form of choice.

At a department meeting, Bellamy suggested that they refuse to hold classes in Bellwether until the building was declared safe. The chair rubbed his forehead.

“We can’t just close,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Because the timetable—”

“Students are coughing in the classrooms.”

“Not everyone.”

A silence followed. There was always someone who was not coughing. There was always someone whose office wasn’t leaking, whose child did not have asthma, whose book collection had not spent a night in the corridor. Misfortune was most persuasive when it belonged to the person speaking.

Mara had started to feel tired before she got out of bed. She told herself it was the term, the marking, the ordinary exhaustion of being useful to too many people at once. She bought a small air purifier with her own money and set it on the filing cabinet. The machine whirred through lectures, through office hours, through the hours when she was supposed to be writing. Its little blue light promised nothing except that it was on.

In March, the university sent out an email. The original work, it said, had revealed deficiencies requiring additional repairs. The project would continue through the summer. Faculty members were thanked for their patience.

Patience. Mara read the word twice. It was a small, convenient word, capable of carrying almost any amount of damp.

She had options, in theory. She could move into a spare office in the building across campus, though the office was shared and the commute would take her away from her students. She could work from home, though she lived in a one-bedroom apartment with a neighbor who practiced trumpet at unpredictable hours. She could file another complaint. She could make a list of complaints, arrange them chronologically, and give the list a title.

Instead, she replied to the email with three questions about the inspection and the ventilation. Then she saved the reply in a folder called *Bellwether*.

The response came nine days later. The concerns had been forwarded to Facilities. The inspection results were not yet available. An updated schedule would be circulated when possible.

It was not nothing. It was not enough. Mara filed it anyway.

By April, she had learned to keep her most important books in boxes. She carried a folded mask in her satchel. She checked the ceiling before sitting down. When rain was forecast, she placed the wastebasket under the stain, though the stain had been dry for weeks. Habit was a kind of architecture too, a structure built from small precautions.

The crew worked through the summer. Mara left for a research fellowship in June and returned in August to find the courtyard freshly paved, the corridor repainted, and a new ceiling tile above her desk. The building smelled of paint and nothing else. The air purifier was gone; she had taken it home at the end of term. She stood in the office for a while, listening. Somewhere below her, a pipe clicked as it cooled.

On the first day of classes, she walked the long way around the courtyard. The paving was level. At the edge, near the brick wall, a narrow drain ran along the terrace. A new grate covered it, clean and straight. She looked down at it for longer than she needed to.

The dean’s email had promised continued monitoring. The work, it said, had addressed the source of the infiltration. The phrase sounded almost like an ending. Mara was old enough to know that endings were often just intervals in which nothing had happened yet.

Her first class gathered in the seminar room downstairs. Dr. Rahman was there, wearing no mask. She looked better, though perhaps it was only that she was glad to be back. The students found seats. Someone opened a window. Someone else asked whether it would be cold.

“Probably,” Mara said.

The room held the familiar smell of books, dust, and old radiators. She set her notes on the desk, glanced once at the ceiling, and began.

There was work to do. The building would hold, or it would not. The university would listen, or it would not. For now, there were thirty students waiting, and a text to read, and the small hope that this year the rain would stay where it belonged. That was not confidence. It would have to do.
