The first time I saw them, they were standing under the broken clock outside the station, arguing softly about pears.

He was a tall man in a rain-dark coat, holding the paper bag too carefully, as if fruit bruised by attention. She was small beside him, bareheaded despite the drizzle, her hair pinned in a silver twist. Nothing about them should have held me there. Couples argued about groceries every day. Trains came and went. The gutters choked with leaves.

Yet I could not look away.

Perhaps it was the way she listened without turning her face toward him, her eyes fixed on something far down the street. Perhaps it was how he touched her elbow and then withdrew his hand, as though she were hot. They began walking, and I followed.

At the corner of Alder Lane, we passed the chair.

It lay on its side beside a mound of split rubbish bags, a dining chair with one leg missing and its back burned black. The seat had been scorched through in the middle, leaving a ragged hole. Rain beaded on the charred wood, shining like oil. The woman stopped.

For a moment, she and the ruined chair seemed to recognize each other.

Then the man murmured something, and she moved on.

Their house was narrow and pale, pressed between two shuttered shops near the canal. By then the rain had strengthened. Water was already spreading over the pavement in silver sheets. The man unlocked the door; she entered first. A light came on upstairs, then below. I stood across the street beneath the dead awning of a bakery and watched their shapes move behind the curtains.

The flood arrived without drama. It rose from the drains, climbed the curb, swallowed the tires of parked cars. Cold water lapped around my ankles, then my calves. Still I stayed.

Inside, the couple sat at a kitchen table. The man peeled a pear with a knife, the skin falling in one continuous curl. The woman sat very straight, hands folded in her lap. The yellow lamp above them made her face look wooden, polished, patiently worn.

Then she turned her head toward the window.

I knew, with a certainty that tightened around my heart, that she was not looking at me. She was looking past me, down the drowned street, toward the heap of refuse and the burned chair rocking gently in the current.

The water lifted it free.

Slowly, impossibly, the chair drifted into view. It turned once, displaying its blackened back, its hollow seat, its missing leg trailing below like a broken bone. It knocked against a lamppost, spun, and came to rest before their window.

Inside, the woman stood.

The man did not move. His knife hung in the air. The pear rolled from the table and struck the floor with a sound I heard even through the rain.

The woman pressed both palms to the glass. Her reflection and the chair’s reflection overlapped: her dark dress becoming the burned frame, her pale face hovering in the empty hole where the seat had been.

For one breath, they were the same thing—rescued from fire, set out as rubbish, waiting for water to carry them home.

Then the lights went out.

The flood climbed to my waist. The chair bumped softly against the house, again and again, as if knocking to be let in.
