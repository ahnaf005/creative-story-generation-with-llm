At the corner of Dyer and Quince, where the rainwater had begun to stitch the gutters together, there was a chair.

It had been left beside the bins as if waiting for collection, though no one collected such things anymore, not in that weather. A kitchen chair, once painted yellow perhaps, now blistered black along the backrest and one leg charred down to a crooked stump. Someone had tried to burn it and failed. Or the chair had tried to burn and survived. That was the more natural thought, somehow.

Martin stopped under the awning of the pharmacy and looked at it.

Poor thing, he thought. Ridiculous. A chair was not a thing to pity. Still, there it stood in the rain, hunched and ashamed, its wet seat shining like an eye.

Then the couple passed.

They were ordinary at first glance: a man in a brown coat holding a paper bag against his chest, a woman with a blue scarf knotted at her throat. Husband and wife, surely. Or nearly. They walked without touching, but with the practiced distance of people who had measured each other for years. The woman laughed at something the man said, and Martin felt, absurdly, that the laugh had been meant for him. Not offered to him, exactly. Released into the air for him to find.

He forgot the chair.

No, not forgot. It remained behind him, blackening in the rain, but the couple had taken its place in his attention. There was something in the woman’s posture—the way she held herself a little stiffly from the waist, as if one side of her had been damaged and badly repaired. Her scarf was too bright for the day. Her hair, pinned low, had loosened in the rain, and one dark strand clung to her cheek like a crack.

They crossed the street when the light changed. Martin crossed too. Why not? His flat was that way, more or less. Not directly. Not at all, if one wanted to be precise, but precision was a tyrant. He could walk where he liked.

The rain thickened. Water ran over the curb in quick brown sheets. The man said something, and the woman tilted her head toward him, listening, or pretending to. Martin admired that: the performance of attention. He had performed it all his life, at counters, in offices, at funerals. Yes, yes, of course, how terrible, how interesting, how good to see you. But this woman’s inattention had grace. She was listening to something else beneath the rain.

They turned onto Bellwether Lane.

Martin had never liked Bellwether Lane. The houses leaned close together there, their windows narrow and high, as if designed to keep secrets from climbing out. The drains were already overwhelmed. Water pooled around the tires of parked cars, lapping at hubcaps with small, eager sounds.

The couple stopped before a brick house with a green door.

Home, then. Their home. So they had one. It seemed almost unfair. The man fumbled with keys while the woman stood under the porch light. For a moment she looked back down the street.

Martin stepped behind a plane tree.

Had she seen him? No. Impossible. The rain was a curtain. He was a sensible man waiting out the weather. Anyone might stand behind a tree in a flood.

The door opened. Yellow light spilled out, and the couple went in.

That should have been the end of it.

But the street was filling. It would be foolish to walk on now. Dangerous, even. Better to wait. The tree offered little shelter, so Martin moved closer to the house, along the side path where the hedge scraped his coat. A side window glowed beyond the leaves. The curtains had not been fully drawn.

People were careless about curtains. They believed darkness protected them, or light. They believed being inside made them invisible.

Through the narrow gap, Martin saw a kitchen.

The man had set the paper bag on the table. He removed a loaf of bread, a tin of peaches, a bottle wrapped in newspaper. The woman unwound her blue scarf and laid it over the back of a chair.

Martin’s breath caught.

The chair inside was not burned. It was a plain wooden kitchen chair, painted yellow. Four legs. Slatted back. Perfectly ordinary.

And yet.

The woman sat down on it with the same injured stiffness he had noticed in her walk. She placed both hands on the table, palms down. The man moved behind her, saying something Martin could not hear. He touched her shoulder. She did not turn.

The water had reached Martin’s shoes. Cold seeped through the seams. He hardly noticed.

Yellow, he thought. The chair in the rain had been yellow before the fire. He knew it now. The same shape, the same narrow back, the same patient, domestic misery. One burned and abandoned at the corner; one whole and waiting beneath her body.

The man opened the bottle. Wine, perhaps. Or something stronger. The woman lifted her face toward the ceiling, and the kitchen light poured over her throat, her cheek, the damp strand of hair like a black split in porcelain.

She had been burned too. No, that was nonsense. Her skin was unmarked. Still, there are fires that leave the paint intact. There are flames that eat only the inside, hollowing a thing until it sits upright out of habit.

The floodwater climbed his ankles.

Inside, the woman began to laugh. The sound did not reach him, but he saw it move through her shoulders. The man laughed too, though a second late. Then the woman stopped. Her head turned slowly toward the window.

Toward him.

Martin did not move. The hedge held him in its wet fingers. The water tugged at his legs.

She rose from the yellow chair.

Behind her, for one instant, the chair seemed black. Not shadowed—blackened. Its slats curled like burned ribs. Rain ran down the window, distorting the kitchen into streaks of gold and soot. The woman stepped closer, and her face wavered in the glass, joined with his reflection until he could not tell which of them stood outside in the flood and which had come to the window from within.

Poor thing, he thought again.

But he no longer knew if he meant the chair, the woman, or himself.

The water climbed higher, cold and insistent, and the green door remained shut.
