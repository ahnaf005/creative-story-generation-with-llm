The road had been cut into the mountain like a question no one had answered.

Jonas walked it every autumn, when the tourists had gone and the larches burned gold against the black firs. He liked the emptiness of it: the thin air, the far-off clatter of stones falling somewhere unseen, the way the peaks stood without apology above weather, memory, and men.

He had not spoken aloud in three days.

Then he came around a bend and saw the car.

It lay nose-down in the ditch below the road, one wheel still turning slowly, though nothing else moved. The windshield was webbed white. Steam rose from the crumpled hood and drifted into the cold morning like breath leaving a body.

Jonas climbed down carefully, sliding on shale, calling out before he reached it.

“Hello? Can you hear me?”

The driver’s door had been torn half open. Inside, a woman sat slumped against the seat belt, her dark hair fallen across her face. She was young, or seemed young to him—though age, Jonas knew, changed depending on who was looking.

He checked for a pulse because that was what one was supposed to do. Her wrist was warm, then not warm. There was no answering beat beneath his fingers.

For a moment he simply knelt there with his hand around her hand.

Above them, the mountains remained themselves.

His phone found a single bar only when he climbed back to the road and stood on a boulder with one arm raised. He gave the dispatcher what he could: the old pass road, mile marker seventeen, one vehicle, one victim, no sign of anyone else. The woman on the line told him to stay nearby if it was safe.

Safe, Jonas thought, looking at the sky already thickening with cloud. Nothing here was safe. Not the road, not the weather, not the heart.

But he stayed.

He found a flat stone near the car and sat beside her where she could see him, had she been able to see. It seemed rude to leave her alone in such a place. The forest pressed close on the slope below, dark and listening. A raven turned once overhead and vanished toward the ridge.

“My name is Jonas,” he said, his voice rough from disuse. “I suppose you already know that doesn’t matter much.”

The woman said nothing.

He nodded, as if she had been polite enough to listen.

“I had a wife once,” he continued. “Marta. She hated mountains. Said they made her feel watched. We lived by the sea for twenty-two years because of that. Every morning she would open the curtains and judge the weather like it had come to her for approval.”

He smiled faintly.

“She was usually kinder than she pretended.”

A gust of wind moved through the firs. The car ticked and settled.

Jonas looked at the woman’s hands. No ring. A silver bracelet. Fingernails painted pale blue, chipped at the edges.

“After Marta died, people kept telling me I would get used to it. As if grief were a room with furniture you eventually stopped bumping into. But that isn’t true. You just learn where the sharp corners are.”

He watched the clouds drag their shadows over the opposite slope. Whole forests disappeared beneath them, then returned.

“I used to be a teacher,” he said. “History. That was my subject. Empires, treaties, borders. Men drawing lines through places they hadn’t walked. I told children that events shaped the world. Battles, inventions, revolutions.”

He shook his head.

“Now I think most things vanish almost as soon as they happen. A king shouts an order, a river changes course, a woman misses a curve in the road. For a while, everything is different. Then moss grows over it.”

Far below, water rushed invisibly through a ravine.

He wondered who was waiting for her. A mother perhaps, annoyed that she had not called. A lover making coffee. A child at a window. Or no one. That was possible too. The world contained many kinds of loneliness, and some wore bright bracelets.

Snow began to fall around noon—not much, only a few wandering flakes, lost and early. One landed on the woman’s cheek and melted there.

Jonas took off his wool scarf and laid it gently across her shoulders, though she did not need warmth anymore. He did it for himself, and because the wilderness was watching.

“When I was a boy,” he said, “my father brought me up this pass. We camped somewhere beyond the saddle. I remember waking at night and hearing wolves. He told me not to be afraid. He said they were only singing to each other.”

Jonas laughed quietly.

“Years later I learned there were no wolves here then. They’d all been hunted out. What I heard was wind in the rocks. But I liked his lie better.”

The afternoon dimmed. He checked the road again, but there were no headlights, no siren rising through the valley. Only the immense patience of stone.

He returned to the woman and sat with his back against the bent frame of the car.

“I don’t know your name,” he said. “That troubles me.”

He imagined several for her, trying each silently and rejecting it. Names belonged to the living; the dead carried something larger and less precise.

“I hope,” he said after a while, “that your last thought was not fear. I hope you saw the light on those trees, or remembered someone’s hand, or heard a song you loved.”

The raven came back near dusk and perched on a twisted pine above the ditch. Jonas glared at it until it lifted away with a croak of offended dignity.

At last, when the cold had entered his knees and fingers, when the road had turned the color of iron, the siren came.

It was faint at first, almost another trick of the wind. Then blue light flashed against the trunks above him. Voices called. Boots scraped down the slope.

A paramedic touched his shoulder. “Sir? Are you hurt?”

Jonas looked up, surprised by the question.

“No,” he said. “No, I’m only here.”

They worked around him with practiced tenderness. Someone asked him what had happened, and he told them what little he knew. Someone thanked him for staying.

As they carried the woman up toward the road, Jonas stood aside. His scarf was still around her shoulders. He almost asked for it back, then did not.

Night gathered quickly in the mountains. The ambulance doors closed. The blue lights turned the falling snow briefly luminous, each flake appearing from darkness and returning to it.

Jonas watched until the vehicles disappeared around the bend.

Then the road was empty again.

He began to walk.

Above him, the peaks faded into cloud. Beneath him, the ravine kept speaking its cold, endless language. By morning, snow would soften the skid marks. By spring, meltwater would carry bits of glass into the soil. Grass would grow at the edge of the ditch. Birds would cross the pass without remembering.

Jonas walked on through the dark, listening to the wilderness breathe, and for the first time in three days he did not feel alone.
