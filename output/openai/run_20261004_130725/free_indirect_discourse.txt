The road had been empty all morning.

It unrolled ahead of him in a pale ribbon, cut into the shoulder of the mountain, with loose stones ticking away under his boots and falling, after a long silence, into the pines below. Above, the peaks stood as if they had always stood there and always would, though he knew that was vanity on their part. Even mountains were only weather happening slowly.

He had come walking because there was nowhere else he particularly needed to be. That was the truth of it, though if asked he would have said he liked the air, the distance, the old shepherd tracks threading between ridges. A man his age could call loneliness solitude and be believed.

Then he saw the broken guardrail.

At first it looked like a torn strip of tin, bright against the gravel. A little farther on, black scars crossed the road in wavering lines. He stopped. The valley gave back no sound except wind and, somewhere lower down, water worrying stones.

The car lay twenty yards below, nose crushed against a larch, one wheel still turning with absurd patience. A red car. Small. City plates.

He climbed down carefully, sliding more than stepping, catching at roots. By the time he reached it, he already knew. The windshield had starred inward. The driver’s door hung open. A woman lay half out of the car, one arm folded beneath her as if she had decided, mid-fall, to rest.

She was young, though not very young. Dark hair had come loose across her face. Her coat was the color of plums. There was blood at her temple, already darkening in the cold air.

He said, “Can you hear me?”

Of course she could not. Still, a man said such things. He said them into rooms where wives had stopped breathing, into hospital telephones, into the backs of departing trains. Can you hear me? As if hearing were the last rope thrown across the gap.

He checked her wrist because one did what one had seen done in films, two fingers pressed to the delicate place below the thumb. Nothing. He tried her throat. Nothing there either, except the cooling fact of her.

His phone had one thin bar, then none, then one again when he stood on a rock and lifted it toward the indifferent sky. He managed to call emergency services. A woman’s voice, clipped and distant, asked for coordinates, road markers, signs of life.

“No,” he said. “No signs.”

Stay with the victim if it is safe to do so, she told him. Help is on the way.

Help. Yes. Help was always on the way in the way tomorrow was on the way, in the way spring was on the way under snow, in the way forgiveness might have been on the way if one had only waited another decade. He put the phone in his pocket and looked up at the road. It seemed much higher now.

He took off his pack, removed his wool scarf, and covered the woman’s face.

“There,” he said. “That’s better.”

But it was not better. It was only arranged.

He sat on a stone near her, close enough that she would not seem abandoned if anyone looked down from above. The wind moved through the larches with a sound like breath drawn between teeth. Clouds were gathering behind the western ridge, bruised and slow.

“Well,” he said to the covered face, “they told me to stay.”

His own voice startled him. It had been a long while since he had spoken at length to anyone who could not interrupt.

He told her first about the dog he had had as a boy, a yellow mongrel named Captain, though Captain had possessed no authority over anything except the kitchen scraps. The dog used to follow him to the river and bark at the current as if it were an intruder. One spring the river rose suddenly with meltwater, and Captain ran along the bank, furious, until he slipped and was gone. For weeks the boy had searched downstream, certain that love, properly stubborn, could retrieve what water had taken.

“Foolish,” he said. “But I suppose children have to believe the world can be argued with.”

The woman did not disagree.

A crow landed on the shattered guardrail above them. It cocked its head, black eye sharp as a nail. Waiting too, was it? Everything waited. Trees waited for rot, snow waited for sun, the body waited to become unrecognizable. He felt a sudden anger at the bird, then shame. The crow was only being a crow. Better than most men, perhaps.

He told her about Elena next. He had not meant to; her name rose by itself, like a trout from dark water. Elena with her copper laugh, Elena hanging sheets in the courtyard of their first apartment, Elena saying he walked as if apologizing to the ground. They had been married thirty-two years, which at the beginning had seemed an impossible country to cross. Then they crossed it, and at the far border she grew thin and tired and left him standing with all their maps.

“She hated mountains,” he said. “Said they were dramatic for no reason.”

He smiled, and the smile hurt.

Elena had kept every postcard anyone ever sent. She had pressed them into cookbooks, bills, old dictionaries. After she died, he found them everywhere: Naples, Prague, a beach in Crete, a hotel in Zurich they had once promised to visit and never did. Whole cities blooming between unpaid invoices. Proof, he thought then, that life was not a road but a weather system. Plans evaporated, returned elsewhere as rain.

The clouds lowered. The temperature slipped.

He stood and climbed partway back toward the road to check for flashing lights, for the mutter of engines. Nothing. Only the long bends of asphalt, empty in both directions. The broken guardrail looked freshly wounded.

When he returned, he noticed the woman’s hand. Her fingers were bare, nails painted a chipped blue. There was a small ink mark on her wrist, perhaps a tattoo, perhaps only a stain. He wondered who waited for her. A husband? A sister? A child drawing at a table, looking up whenever a car passed? Somewhere, a phone would ring and a life would divide itself into before and after. The before always looked so sturdy until it was gone.

He wanted to know her name. It seemed wrong not to know it. He leaned toward the car, careful of the glass, and saw a canvas bag spilled open on the passenger floor. A book, an orange, a pair of gloves, a folded map. No purse in sight. He did not touch anything. Let the living do their cataloguing. Let them put numbers on grief and photographs in plastic sleeves.

Instead he sat again and told her about the time he and Elena missed a train in Milan and spent the night drinking bitter coffee in a station café. How furious he had been then—young enough to believe inconvenience was tragedy. Elena had laughed until she cried. The train is gone, she had said. Look at us, still here.

Still here. For a while.

Snow began as a few uncertain flakes, dissolving on the red hood of the car. The crow lifted away without ceremony. The wilderness seemed to draw closer, not moving exactly, but becoming more itself: the larches darkening, the slopes steepening, the ravine below filling with blue shadow. Out here, human catastrophe was a small warmth fading into the larger cold. The mountain would accept the tire marks, the blood, the broken metal. By spring, grass would rise through glass.

He wished this thought were cruel, but it was not. There was mercy in it, or something adjacent to mercy. Nothing held forever—not terror, not love, not the sharp first edge of loss. Even the woman’s last moment, whatever it had been, had passed beyond happening. The crash was over. The fear was over. She had been delivered into the one place no story could follow.

At last, far off, came a sound that did not belong to wind or water: a siren, thin and wavering among the mountains. It approached, vanished behind stone, returned louder.

He rose stiffly. His knees complained; his hands were numb. He brushed snow from his coat and looked down at the covered face.

“They’re coming,” he said.

It sounded inadequate, almost foolish. They were coming too late, and also as soon as they could. Both things were true. Most of life was like that, he supposed: too late and as soon as possible.

The first ambulance appeared above, lights turning the snow red and blue. Men shouted. Doors slammed. The empty road filled suddenly with purpose.

He stepped back to make room for them, but for one more moment he stayed beside her, a solitary man in the mountain cold, keeping watch over a stranger whose name he did not know. The wilderness leaned in around them, vast and patient, and the siren’s echo broke apart against the cliffs until it was only another passing sound.
