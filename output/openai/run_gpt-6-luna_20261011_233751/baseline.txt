On February 12, 1966, Barbara had promised herself she would stay through the service.

She was eight months pregnant, and the baby had spent the morning pressing insistently against her ribs. Still, she sat near the aisle in the Southfield synagogue, one hand resting on her belly as Rabbi Adler spoke.

Then a young man stood.

The sharp crack silenced the room. Rabbi Adler fell, and for one impossible second nobody moved. Then the congregation surged toward the doors. Barbara was carried with them, shoulder to shoulder, her feet scarcely touching the floor.

“Wait!” she cried. “He’s only a boy!”

She had seen the terror in the young man’s face before the gun turned on himself. She wanted to reach him—not to excuse what he had done, but to keep him from being alone in his final moments.

The crowd swept her outside. In the cold, someone caught her before she fell. Barbara clutched her belly and listened to the synagogue doors close behind her.

The baby kicked.

Barbara held on to that small, stubborn movement. Around her, people wept and called for help. She closed her eyes and whispered a prayer—not for answers, which would not come that day, but for everyone who had been left behind.
