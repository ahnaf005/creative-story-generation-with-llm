The smell reached him before the wreckage did: hot copper, scorched rubber, and the sweet, sickly reek of vaporized coolant cutting through the scent of wet pine.

Julian paused on the crest of the logging road. Below him, down a sheer forty-foot embankment choked with blackberry brambles and splintered alder, sat a dark sedan. It was crumpled nose-first against the broad, mossy flank of a western red cedar, its roof buckled like a stepped-on tin can. 

The silence that followed the mountain wind was absolute. No horn blared. No steam hissed. 

Julian scrambled down the scree, digging his boot heels into the loose shale, sending small avalanches of slate clattering into the ditch. When he reached the crushed driver’s side, he didn't need a medical degree to know the truth. 

She was young—perhaps thirty—with dark hair matted across her forehead and a silver ring on her thumb. Her head rested against the deployed, deflated airbag as though she had merely grown tired of the road and decided to sleep. Her skin had already taken on that waxy, translucent stillness that belongs only to the departed. Julian pressed two trembling fingers to the side of her cool neck. Nothing. Only the quiet rustle of ferns in the breeze.

He climbed halfway back up the ridge to find a sliver of cellular reception, balancing on an exposed outcrop of granite. The dispatcher’s voice was distant, crackling with static. *A team is en route from the valley basin,* she told him. *Two hours. Maybe three, if the fog holds.*

Julian told her he would wait by the car. 

When he returned to the floor of the ravine, the shadows had lengthened. The sun had slipped behind the ragged teeth of the mountain range, casting the forest into an indigo twilight. The wilderness here was ancient, vast, and completely indifferent; it watched without eyes, breathed through the swaying branches, and absorbed the violent intrusion of steel and glass as casually as it would a fallen leaf.

He took off his heavy wool jacket and laid it gently over her shoulders, then sat down on a moss-covered boulder beside the open, ruined driver’s door. He did not want to leave her alone. 

"They’re coming," Julian said softly. The sound of his own voice felt foreign, brittle against the cathedral hush of the trees. "It’ll just be a little while."

He looked at her hands, still curled loosely near the steering column. An open thermos lay on the floorboard amid a constellation of safety glass. On the dashboard, a parking permit from a coastal university fluttered in the draft. 

"I used to live near the ocean," he murmured, his eyes drifting upward into the darkening canopy. "Cape Foulweather. Every winter, the storms would rip chunks of the headland away. You’d go to sleep listening to the surf, and you’d wake up and find fifty feet of grass and soil just… vanished into the gray water."

A raven croaked from high above, its wings snapping against the cold air as it wheeled toward the valley. 

"I had a house there once," Julian continued, speaking not to demand an answer, but to keep the creeping void at bay. "Built the deck with my own hands. Cedar planks, brass screws. I thought it would outlive me. I was married then—her name was Clara. We thought we had anchored ourselves to the earth. But the cliffs gave way, millimeter by millimeter, year after year. And then Clara got sick, and she was gone faster than the cliffside."

He looked back at the woman. The faint light caught the dust motes hanging motionless inside the cabin.

"We spend so much time pretending things are permanent," he whispered. "We build roads over ancient rock. We set alarms for tomorrow morning. We buy cars and plan vacations and make promises that span decades. But the world only tolerates our lines. A wet patch of asphalt, a blown tire, a deer stepping out of the mist… and the momentum simply stops."

The air grew sharp, carrying the icy bite of snowmelt from the peaks. Julian pulled his knees to his chest, his breath clouding in the dimming light. Around them, the wilderness seemed to lean in—not with malice, but with the quiet weight of eternity. Trees that had germinated during the Renaissance stood witness; the moss that padded the stones would slowly consume the iron frame of her car, given enough decades. 

"It isn't cruel, though," Julian said, his voice dropping to a gravelly hum. "I used to think it was. After Clara, I hated the trees for staying green. I hated the ocean for continuing to crash against the rocks. But it’s just the order of things. We are brief, like the spark from a flint. It doesn't make the light any less real while it’s here."

He fell silent for a long time. He watched her profile—the curve of her cheek, the stillness of her lashes. He wondered who was waiting for her, whose heart was still beating normally, oblivious to the fact that their world had already split in two. He held vigil for them, too.

An hour passed. The forest dissolved entirely into blackness, save for the bright, indifferent stars peering through gaps in the boughs. Julian stayed where he was, his shoulder pressed against the twisted frame of the car, keeping the dark company.

Then, faint at first, a wash of red and blue light flickered through the high timber miles up the pass. The low, mournful wail of a siren echoed down the canyon, bouncing off stone and water, weak and small against the vast expanse of the mountains.

Julian stood, his joints aching with the cold. He reached out and touched the woman's shoulder through the wool of his coat, a gentle, parting gesture.

"They're here," he told her softly. "You're not alone."
